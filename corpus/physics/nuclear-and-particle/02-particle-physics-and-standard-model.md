# Particle Physics and the Standard Model

Particle physics asks the most basic questions: What is matter made of? What forces act between its constituents? The answer developed over the 20th century is the **Standard Model** — a quantum field theory describing all known elementary particles and three of the four fundamental forces (electromagnetic, weak and strong; gravity is not included). It has passed every experimental test for half a century, culminating in the discovery of the Higgs boson in 2012.

## 1. From Atoms to Quarks: A Brief History

- **1897:** J. J. Thomson discovers the electron.
- **1911–1932:** Rutherford's nucleus; the proton (1919); the neutron (Chadwick, 1932).
- **1928–1932:** Dirac predicts antimatter; Carl Anderson discovers the positron in cosmic rays (1932).
- **1936:** the muon is discovered — "Who ordered that?" (I. I. Rabi).
- **1947:** the pion, predicted by Yukawa, is found in cosmic rays.
- **1950s–60s:** accelerators produce hundreds of new "elementary" particles — the "particle zoo".
- **1956:** the neutrino is detected (Cowan and Reines). Parity violation in weak decays (Lee, Yang; Wu's cobalt-60 experiment).
- **1964:** Gell-Mann and Zweig propose **quarks** to organize the hadrons (the "Eightfold Way", 1961). The Higgs mechanism is proposed (Brout, Englert; Higgs; Guralnik, Hagen, Kibble).
- **1967–68:** Glashow, Weinberg and Salam formulate **electroweak theory**.
- **1968–69:** deep inelastic scattering at SLAC reveals point-like constituents inside protons.
- **1973:** **asymptotic freedom** (Gross, Wilczek, Politzer) establishes QCD; neutral currents discovered at CERN.
- **1974:** charm quark (J/ψ, "November Revolution"). **1975:** tau lepton. **1977:** bottom quark.
- **1979:** gluon jets observed at DESY.
- **1983:** W and Z bosons discovered at CERN (Rubbia, van der Meer).
- **1995:** top quark at Fermilab.
- **1998:** neutrino oscillations (Super-Kamiokande) — neutrinos have mass.
- **2000:** tau neutrino observed directly (DONUT).
- **2012:** Higgs boson discovered at the LHC (ATLAS and CMS).

## 2. The Elementary Particles

### Fermions (spin ½): the matter particles
Arranged in **three generations** of increasing mass. Ordinary matter is made entirely of first-generation particles.

**Quarks** (feel all forces, including the strong force):

| Generation | Up-type (charge +⅔) | Mass | Down-type (charge −⅓) | Mass |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | up (u) | ~2.2 MeV/c² | down (d) | ~4.7 MeV/c² |
| 2 | charm (c) | ~1.27 GeV/c² | strange (s) | ~93 MeV/c² |
| 3 | top (t) | ~172.6 GeV/c² | bottom (b) | ~4.18 GeV/c² |

**Leptons** (no strong interaction):

| Generation | Charged lepton (charge −1) | Mass | Neutrino (charge 0) | Mass |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | electron (e) | 0.511 MeV/c² | electron neutrino ($\nu_e$) | < 0.45 eV/c² |
| 2 | muon (μ) | 105.7 MeV/c² | muon neutrino ($\nu_\mu$) | tiny |
| 3 | tau (τ) | 1777 MeV/c² | tau neutrino ($\nu_\tau$) | tiny |

Each fermion has an **antiparticle** with opposite charges (electric charge, color, lepton or baryon number) and the same mass: positron $e^+$, antiquarks $\bar u$, $\bar d$, etc. (Whether neutrinos are their own antiparticles — **Majorana** fermions — is unknown; neutrinoless double beta decay experiments are searching.)

The top quark is as heavy as a gold atom and decays (~$5\times10^{-25}$ s) before it can form hadrons. Masses span at least 12 orders of magnitude, from neutrinos to the top quark — an unexplained pattern ("flavor puzzle").

### Bosons: the force carriers and the Higgs

| Boson | Spin | Charge | Mass | Mediates |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Photon (γ) | 1 | 0 | 0 | Electromagnetism |
| Gluons (g), 8 types | 1 | 0 (carry color) | 0 | Strong force |
| W⁺, W⁻ | 1 | ±1 | 80.4 GeV/c² | Weak force (charged current) |
| Z⁰ | 1 | 0 | 91.19 GeV/c² | Weak force (neutral current) |
| Higgs (H) | 0 | 0 | 125.1 GeV/c² | Gives mass to W, Z and fermions |
| Graviton (hypothetical) | 2 | 0 | 0 | Gravity (not in the Standard Model) |

In total the Standard Model has 12 fermion types (×3 colors for quarks, plus antiparticles), 12 gauge bosons (8 gluons, W⁺, W⁻, Z, γ) and the Higgs boson, with **19 free parameters** (26 or more with neutrino masses) measured but not predicted by the theory.

## 3. The Four Fundamental Interactions

| Interaction | Carrier | Relative strength (at ~1 fm) | Range | Acts on | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strong | Gluons | 1 | ~$10^{-15}$ m (confined) | Color charge (quarks, gluons) | Binding quarks into protons; nuclear binding (residual) |
| Electromagnetic | Photon | ~$10^{-2}$ ($\alpha \approx 1/137$) | Infinite ($1/r^2$) | Electric charge | Atoms, chemistry, light |
| Weak | W±, Z⁰ | ~$10^{-6}$ (at low energy) | ~$10^{-18}$ m | Weak isospin (all fermions) | Beta decay, solar fusion |
| Gravity | Graviton? | ~$10^{-38}$ | Infinite ($1/r^2$) | Mass–energy | Planets, stars, galaxies |

The range of a force mediated by a particle of mass $m$ is roughly its reduced Compton wavelength $\hbar/(mc)$: massless photons and gravitons give infinite range; the heavy W and Z give the weak force a range of ~$2.5\times10^{-18}$ m. (Gluons are massless, but confinement limits the strong force's effective range.)

### Feynman diagrams
Richard Feynman introduced diagrams (1948) representing particle interactions as exchanges of virtual force carriers. Each vertex corresponds to a coupling (e.g. a factor of $\sqrt\alpha$ in QED), and diagrams translate into precise mathematical expressions for amplitudes. Electron–electron scattering is depicted as exchange of a virtual photon; beta decay as a $d$ quark emitting a virtual W⁻ that becomes an electron and antineutrino.

## 4. Quantum Electrodynamics (QED)

QED, developed by Tomonaga, Schwinger and Feynman (Nobel 1965) with Dyson, is the relativistic quantum field theory of electrons and photons. Key features:
- Coupling strength $\alpha = \frac{e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\hbar c} \approx 1/137$; since $\alpha \ll 1$, perturbation theory (expansion in Feynman diagrams) converges rapidly in practice.
- **Renormalization** handles infinities from virtual-particle loops by absorbing them into measured charge and mass.
- The effective charge increases at short distances (**vacuum polarization**: virtual electron–positron pairs screen the bare charge). At the Z mass, $\alpha \approx 1/128$.
- Predictions: the electron's anomalous magnetic moment (agreement to better than 1 part in $10^{12}$); the Lamb shift; positronium energy levels.

## 5. Quantum Chromodynamics (QCD): The Strong Force

### Color charge
Quarks carry a **color charge** with three values — conventionally red, green, blue (no relation to visual color) — and antiquarks carry anticolors. Gluons carry a color and an anticolor (8 independent combinations). The theory is based on the gauge group SU(3). Evidence for three colors: the decay rate $\pi^0\to\gamma\gamma$; the ratio of $e^+e^-\to$ hadrons to $e^+e^-\to\mu^+\mu^-$; the existence of the $\Delta^{++}$ ($uuu$, spin 3/2), which would violate the Pauli principle without an extra antisymmetric quantum number.

### Confinement
**Quarks are never observed in isolation.** Only color-neutral ("white") combinations exist freely. Because gluons themselves carry color and interact with each other, the field between separating quarks forms a narrow **flux tube** with roughly constant energy per length (~1 GeV/fm, a "string tension" of ~16 tonnes-force). Pulling quarks apart costs ever more energy until it becomes energetically favorable to create a new quark–antiquark pair from the vacuum, producing more hadrons rather than free quarks. In high-energy collisions this appears as **jets** of hadrons. Confinement is supported by lattice QCD simulations but a rigorous proof is a Millennium Prize Problem (the Yang–Mills mass gap).

### Asymptotic freedom
Conversely, at short distances (high energies) the strong coupling $\alpha_s$ **decreases** — quarks behave almost as free particles inside hadrons (Gross, Wilczek, Politzer; Nobel 2004). $\alpha_s \approx 0.118$ at the Z mass, but ~1 at the scale of proton structure. This is the opposite of QED's behavior, because gluon self-interactions cause anti-screening.

### Quark–gluon plasma
At temperatures above ~$2\times10^{12}$ K (~150 MeV), hadrons "melt" into a quark–gluon plasma — the state of the universe during its first microseconds. It has been created in heavy-ion collisions at RHIC (Brookhaven) and the LHC, where it behaves as a nearly perfect liquid with extremely low viscosity.

## 6. Hadrons

Composite particles made of quarks, bound by the strong force:

### Baryons (three quarks, $qqq$; fermions)
| Baryon | Quarks | Charge | Mass (MeV/c²) | Lifetime |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Proton (p) | uud | +1 | 938.3 | Stable (> $10^{34}$ years) |
| Neutron (n) | udd | 0 | 939.6 | ~879 s free (stable in nuclei) |
| Lambda (Λ⁰) | uds | 0 | 1115.7 | $2.6\times10^{-10}$ s |
| Delta (Δ⁺⁺) | uuu | +2 | ~1232 | ~$5.6\times10^{-24}$ s |
| Omega (Ω⁻) | sss | −1 | 1672.5 | $8.2\times10^{-11}$ s |

The Ω⁻ was **predicted** by Gell-Mann's Eightfold Way (1962) — mass, charge and strangeness — and discovered at Brookhaven in 1964, a decisive confirmation of the quark model.

**Where does the proton's mass come from?** The three valence quarks' masses (Higgs-generated) total only ~9 MeV — about 1% of 938 MeV. The rest is the energy of gluon fields and quark kinetic energy (QCD binding energy), via $E = mc^2$. So most of the mass of ordinary matter is not due to the Higgs mechanism directly.

### Mesons (quark–antiquark, $q\bar q$; bosons)
| Meson | Quarks | Mass (MeV/c²) |
|---|---|---|
| Pion π⁺ | $u\bar d$ | 139.6 |
| Pion π⁰ | $(u\bar u - d\bar d)/\sqrt2$ | 135.0 |
| Kaon K⁺ | $u\bar s$ | 493.7 |
| J/ψ | $c\bar c$ | 3097 |
| Upsilon ϒ | $b\bar b$ | 9460 |
| B⁰ | $d\bar b$ | 5280 |

Exotic hadrons — **tetraquarks** ($qq\bar q\bar q$) and **pentaquarks** ($qqqq\bar q$) — have been observed (e.g. LHCb, 2015 onward). **Glueballs** made purely of gluons are predicted but not yet confirmed.

## 7. The Weak Interaction and Electroweak Unification

### Properties of the weak force
- The only force that changes **flavor** (quark or lepton type): $d\to u$ in beta decay, $s\to u$ in strange particle decays, $\mu\to e$ in muon decay.
- Responsible for beta decay, the first step of solar fusion ($pp\to de^+\nu$), and all neutrino interactions.
- **Violates parity (P):** demonstrated by Chien-Shiung Wu (1957) in the beta decay of polarized cobalt-60 — electrons emerged preferentially opposite to the nuclear spin. Only **left-handed** particles (and right-handed antiparticles) feel the charged weak force. The universe distinguishes left from right!
- **Violates CP** (charge conjugation × parity) slightly: discovered in neutral kaon decays (Cronin and Fitch, 1964); also seen in B mesons. CP violation is described by complex phases in the CKM quark-mixing matrix (Kobayashi and Maskawa, Nobel 2008, who predicted a third generation to accommodate it).
- Quarks mix across generations through the **CKM matrix**; e.g. the Cabibbo angle (~13°) governs how often $s$ quarks decay to $u$ quarks compared with $d\to u$.

### Electroweak theory
Glashow, Weinberg and Salam (Nobel 1979) unified electromagnetism and the weak force into a single **electroweak** interaction based on the gauge group SU(2)×U(1). At high energies (above ~100 GeV) they are aspects of one force; at low energies, the Higgs mechanism breaks the symmetry, giving large masses to the W and Z while the photon remains massless. The theory predicted **weak neutral currents** (found 1973) and the W and Z masses (confirmed 1983). 't Hooft and Veltman proved its renormalizability (Nobel 1999).

## 8. The Higgs Mechanism and the Higgs Boson

Gauge symmetry seems to require force carriers to be massless, and the weak force's chiral nature forbids simple fermion mass terms. The **Higgs mechanism** resolves this: a scalar field — the Higgs field — fills all of space with a nonzero vacuum value ($v \approx 246$ GeV). Its potential has a "Mexican hat" shape: the symmetric point at the center is unstable, and the field settles into one of the degenerate minima, **spontaneously breaking** the electroweak symmetry.
- W and Z bosons acquire mass by interacting with the Higgs field (absorbing three of its four degrees of freedom as their longitudinal polarization states).
- Fermions acquire mass through **Yukawa couplings** to the Higgs field, with each particle's mass proportional to its coupling strength: $m_f = y_fv/\sqrt2$.
- The remaining degree of freedom is a physical particle: the **Higgs boson**.

On **4 July 2012**, the ATLAS and CMS collaborations at CERN's Large Hadron Collider announced the discovery of a new boson at ~125 GeV, consistent with the Standard Model Higgs. Peter Higgs and François Englert received the 2013 Nobel Prize. Subsequent measurements confirmed spin 0, even parity, and couplings proportional to mass for W, Z, top, bottom, tau, and muon.

**Why it matters:** without the Higgs field, electrons would be massless, atoms would not form, and the weak force would be long-ranged. The Higgs boson's measured mass also implies our vacuum may be **metastable** — tunneling to a lower-energy vacuum is possible in principle but would take vastly longer than the age of the universe.

## 9. Neutrinos

- Three flavors, extremely light, interacting only weakly (and gravitationally). A neutrino can pass through light-years of lead with only a ~50% chance of interacting.
- **Solar neutrino problem:** Ray Davis's Homestake experiment (from 1968) detected only about one third of the electron neutrinos predicted from the Sun. Resolution: **neutrino oscillations** — neutrinos change flavor as they travel, because flavor states are superpositions of mass states with slightly different masses. Confirmed by Super-Kamiokande (atmospheric neutrinos, 1998) and SNO (solar neutrinos, 2001) — Kajita and McDonald, Nobel 2015.
- Oscillation probability (two-flavor approximation): $P(\nu_\alpha\to\nu_\beta) = \sin^22\theta\,\sin^2\left(\frac{1.27\,\Delta m^2[\text{eV}^2]\,L[\text{km}]}{E[\text{GeV}]}\right)$.
- Oscillations prove neutrinos have **mass**, which the original Standard Model did not include. The absolute mass scale is unknown but small: KATRIN limits the effective electron-neutrino mass to below 0.45 eV (2024); cosmology suggests the sum of masses is below ~0.1–0.2 eV.
- Open questions: mass ordering, CP violation in the lepton sector (DUNE, Hyper-Kamiokande), Majorana nature, sterile neutrinos.
- **Neutrino astronomy:** neutrinos from Supernova 1987A (about two dozen detected); high-energy cosmic neutrinos at IceCube (South Pole); the Sun imaged in neutrinos.

## 10. Conservation Laws and Symmetries

| Quantity | Strong | EM | Weak |
|---|---|---|---|
| Energy, momentum, angular momentum | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Electric charge | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Baryon number $B$ | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ |
| Lepton number (each flavor) | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (but violated by neutrino oscillations) |
| Strangeness, charm, etc. | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ |
| Parity (P) | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ |
| Charge conjugation (C) | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ |
| CP | ✓ | ✓ | ✗ (small) |
| CPT | ✓ | ✓ | ✓ (fundamental theorem) |

Baryon number: quarks $+\frac13$, antiquarks $-\frac13$; baryons $B = 1$, mesons $B = 0$. Lepton number: leptons $+1$, antileptons $-1$.

**Examples:**
- $p \to e^+ + \pi^0$? Forbidden (violates baryon and lepton number); proton lifetime > $10^{34}$ years (Super-Kamiokande).
- $\mu^- \to e^- + \gamma$? Forbidden by lepton flavor conservation; never observed (branching ratio < $3.1\times10^{-13}$, MEG II).
- $\mu^- \to e^- + \bar\nu_e + \nu_\mu$: allowed (lifetime 2.2 μs).
- $\pi^+\to\mu^+ + \nu_\mu$: allowed (99.99% of decays).
- $\Lambda^0 \to p + \pi^-$: allowed weak decay (strangeness changes by 1).

**CPT theorem:** any Lorentz-invariant local quantum field theory is invariant under combined C, P and T. Implies particles and antiparticles have identical masses and lifetimes — tested to extraordinary precision (e.g. antihydrogen spectroscopy at CERN's ALPHA experiment).

## 11. Experimental Tools

### Accelerators
- **Large Hadron Collider (LHC)** at CERN near Geneva: 27 km ring, 1232 superconducting dipole magnets at 1.9 K producing 8.3 T, proton–proton collisions at 13.6 TeV (Run 3), up to ~1 billion collisions per second. Major experiments: ATLAS, CMS (general purpose), LHCb (b-physics, CP violation), ALICE (heavy ions).
- Historical: Tevatron (Fermilab, top quark), LEP (CERN, precision Z measurements; established exactly 3 light neutrino species), SLAC linear accelerator, HERA (proton structure).
- Future proposals: High-Luminosity LHC (from ~2030), Future Circular Collider (91 km), International Linear Collider, muon colliders.

### Detectors
Layered like an onion around the collision point: silicon trackers (charged-particle paths and momenta in a magnetic field), electromagnetic calorimeters (electrons, photons), hadronic calorimeters (hadrons), muon chambers (outermost, since muons penetrate everything else). Neutrinos escape undetected and are inferred from **missing transverse momentum**. Triggers select ~1000 interesting events per second from ~40 million bunch crossings.

### Discovery statistics
Particle physicists require a **5σ** significance (probability ~$3\times10^{-7}$ that background alone would fluctuate to produce the signal) to claim a discovery. New particles appear as **resonance peaks** in invariant-mass distributions (e.g. the Higgs in $H\to\gamma\gamma$ and $H\to ZZ^*\to4\ell$).

## 12. Beyond the Standard Model

Despite its success, the Standard Model is incomplete:
1. **Gravity** is not included; no consistent quantum theory of gravity is established.
2. **Dark matter** (~27% of the universe's energy density, ~5× ordinary matter) has no Standard Model candidate. Proposals: WIMPs, axions, sterile neutrinos, primordial black holes. Direct searches (LZ, XENONnT), indirect searches and collider searches have not yet found it.
3. **Dark energy** (~68%) driving accelerated cosmic expansion is unexplained; the naive vacuum-energy estimate is off by ~120 orders of magnitude (the cosmological constant problem).
4. **Matter–antimatter asymmetry:** the universe contains matter but almost no antimatter; Standard Model CP violation is far too small to explain it (baryogenesis requires Sakharov conditions).
5. **Neutrino masses** require extension (right-handed neutrinos, seesaw mechanism?).
6. **Hierarchy problem:** why is the Higgs mass (~125 GeV) so much smaller than the Planck scale (~$10^{19}$ GeV), given quantum corrections that should drive it up? Proposed solutions: supersymmetry, compositeness, extra dimensions — none confirmed at the LHC so far.
7. **Strong CP problem:** why does QCD not violate CP (neutron electric dipole moment < $1.8\times10^{-26}$ e·cm)? The axion was proposed as a solution (Peccei–Quinn).
8. **Why three generations? Why these masses and mixings? Why these charges** (charge quantization suggests grand unification)?
9. **Anomalies under study:** the muon $g-2$ (the 2025 final Fermilab result agrees with updated lattice-QCD Standard Model predictions, weakening the case for new physics), and various flavor-physics tensions.

**Grand unified theories (GUTs)** (e.g. SU(5), SO(10)) unify the strong and electroweak forces at ~$10^{16}$ GeV and predict proton decay. **String theory** attempts to unify all forces including gravity, positing one-dimensional strings in 10 or 11 dimensions; it remains without direct experimental support.
