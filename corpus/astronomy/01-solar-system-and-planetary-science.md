# The Solar System and Planetary Science

The Solar System consists of the Sun and everything bound to it by gravity: eight planets and their more than 400 known moons, dwarf planets, millions of asteroids, comets, and dust, extending out to the Oort cloud about a light-year away. Exploring it — with telescopes and with spacecraft that have now visited every planet — has transformed our understanding of how planets form and evolve, and of where life might exist beyond Earth.

Planetary science is unusual among the sciences because it is both descriptive and strongly quantitative. A short list of physical principles — Newton's law of gravitation, conservation of energy and angular momentum, the laws of thermal radiation, and the kinetic theory of gases — explains why planets move as they do, why some are hot and others frozen, why some keep thick atmospheres while others are airless, and how we can detect worlds around other stars. This chapter combines a tour of the Solar System with those tools, and includes worked examples and practice problems so that the reader can do the calculations, not just read about them.

## 1. Historical Development

### 1.1 From the naked-eye sky to the Copernican revolution

- **Geocentric model:** Aristotle and Ptolemy (*Almagest*, ~150 CE) placed Earth at the center, with planets moving on epicycles — accurate enough to be used for 1400 years.
- **Aristarchus of Samos** (~270 BCE) proposed a heliocentric model. He also tried to measure the relative distances of the Sun and Moon from the angle between them at first-quarter Moon; his geometry was correct, but the angle is so close to 90° that he concluded the Sun was only about 18–20 times farther than the Moon (the true factor is about 390).
- **Eratosthenes** (~240 BCE) measured Earth's circumference using the different noon shadow angles at Syene and Alexandria. He obtained about 250 000 stadia; because the exact length of the stadion he used is uncertain, his result was within somewhere between a few percent and about 16% of the true value of ~40 000 km — remarkable either way.
- **Hipparchus** (~130 BCE) compiled a star catalog and discovered the slow precession of the equinoxes by comparing his positions with older records.
- **Nicolaus Copernicus** (*De revolutionibus*, 1543): heliocentric system, explaining retrograde motion naturally as the effect of Earth overtaking an outer planet (or being overtaken by an inner one).
- **Tycho Brahe** (late 1500s): the most precise naked-eye observations ever made, with positional errors of only about one to two arcminutes.
- **Johannes Kepler** (1609, 1619): using Tycho's data on Mars, found elliptical orbits and his three laws of planetary motion (the first two in *Astronomia nova*, 1609; the third in *Harmonices mundi*, 1619).
- **Galileo Galilei** (1609–1610): first telescopic observations — Jupiter's four large moons (proving not everything orbits Earth), phases of Venus (supporting heliocentrism), lunar mountains and craters, sunspots, the Milky Way resolved into stars.
- **Isaac Newton** (*Principia*, 1687): universal gravitation explained Kepler's laws and unified the physics of falling apples, the Moon and the planets.

### 1.2 Measuring and mapping the system (1650–1900)

- **Christiaan Huygens** discovered Titan (1655) and correctly explained Saturn's strange "ears" as a thin, flat ring (published 1659). **Giovanni Domenico Cassini** discovered the dark gap in the rings now called the Cassini Division (1675).
- **Ole Rømer** (1676) noticed that eclipses of Io ran late when Earth was far from Jupiter and early when it was near, and concluded that light travels at a finite speed, taking minutes to cross Earth's orbit — the first quantitative evidence on the speed of light, obtained with a planetary "clock".
- **Edmond Halley** (1705) used Newton's theory to show that the comets of 1531, 1607 and 1682 were one object and predicted its return; it was recovered on Christmas night 1758, after his death. Halley also proposed using **transits of Venus** to measure the Sun's distance; expeditions to observe the transits of 1761 and 1769 (including James Cook's voyage to Tahiti) produced the first reasonably accurate value of the astronomical unit.
- **Henry Cavendish** (1798) measured the gravitational attraction between lead spheres in the laboratory, which fixed the constant $G$ and therefore let astronomers convert orbital data into the masses of Earth, the Sun and the planets.
- **William Herschel** discovered Uranus (1781) — the first planet found with a telescope.
- **Giuseppe Piazzi** discovered **Ceres** on 1 January 1801; Carl Friedrich Gauss developed new orbit-determination methods to recover it. Ceres was called a planet for about half a century, until so many similar bodies were found that the category "asteroid" was adopted.
- **Neptune** (1846) was predicted mathematically from irregularities in Uranus's orbit by Urbain Le Verrier (and John Couch Adams) and found by Johann Galle within 1° of the predicted position — a triumph of Newtonian physics.
- **Édouard Roche** (1848) calculated the distance inside which a fluid moon would be torn apart by its planet's tides (the Roche limit, Section 7.3).
- **Daniel Kirkwood** (1866) noticed gaps in the distribution of asteroid orbits and linked them to resonances with Jupiter.

### 1.3 The twentieth century and the space age

- **Pluto** was discovered by Clyde Tombaugh in 1930 and reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006.
- **Milutin Milanković** (1920s–1941) calculated how slow changes in Earth's orbit and tilt alter the distribution of sunlight, proposing an astronomical pacing of ice ages; deep-sea sediment records confirmed the main periodicities in 1976 (Hays, Imbrie and Shackleton).
- **Jan Oort** (1950) proposed a distant cloud of comets to explain the orbits of long-period comets; **Kenneth Edgeworth** (1940s) and **Gerard Kuiper** (1951) speculated about a disk of bodies beyond Neptune. The first Kuiper belt object after Pluto and Charon was found by David Jewitt and Jane Luu in 1992.
- **Clair Patterson** (1956) used lead isotopes in the Canyon Diablo iron meteorite to date Earth and the meteorites at about 4.55 billion years — a value that has barely changed since.
- **Impacts recognized:** Eugene Shoemaker and colleagues proved in 1960 that Meteor Crater in Arizona was made by an impact (finding the high-pressure silica mineral coesite); in 1980 Luis and Walter Alvarez and co-workers found an iridium-rich layer at the Cretaceous–Paleogene boundary and proposed an asteroid impact; the buried Chicxulub crater in Mexico was identified as the culprit in 1991.
- **Space age:** Sputnik (1957); Mariner 2 at Venus (1962, the first successful planetary flyby); Mariner 4's first close-up images of Mars (1965); first lunar landing (Apollo 11, 1969); Venera 7, the first spacecraft to transmit data from the surface of another planet (Venus, 1970); Pioneer 10 at Jupiter (1973); Mariner 10, the first spacecraft to use a gravity assist (from Venus, on its way to Mercury, 1974); Viking 1 on Mars (1976); Voyager 1 and 2 (1977) explored the outer planets — Voyager 1 entered interstellar space in 2012 and is the most distant human-made object (over 165 AU, approaching one light-day, about 173 AU, from Earth in late 2026).
- **Giant impact hypothesis** for the Moon: William Hartmann and Donald Davis (1975) and Alastair Cameron and William Ward (1976).
- **Exoplanets:** Aleksander Wolszczan and Dale Frail found planets orbiting a pulsar (1992); Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz found 51 Pegasi b around a Sun-like star (1995).

## 2. Scale of the Solar System

- **Astronomical unit (AU):** originally the mean Earth–Sun distance, now defined as exactly 149 597 870 700 m (~150 million km; light takes ~8 min 19 s to cross it).
- **Light-year:** ~9.46 × 10¹² km ≈ 63 241 AU. **Parsec:** ~3.26 light-years (the distance at which 1 AU subtends 1 arcsecond).
- If the Sun were a 1.4 m ball, Earth would be a 1.3 cm marble ~150 m away; Jupiter a 14 cm ball ~780 m away; Neptune ~4.5 km away; and the nearest star (Proxima Centauri, 4.24 light-years) would be ~40 000 km away.
- **Light-travel times** set the pace of spacecraft operations: about 43 minutes from the Sun to Jupiter, about 4.2 hours to Neptune, and between about 3 and 22 minutes between Earth and Mars depending on where the two planets are in their orbits. A rover on Mars therefore cannot be steered in real time; it must drive autonomously between commands.

## 3. Gravity and Orbits: The Physics That Organizes the Solar System

### 3.1 Kepler's three laws

1. **Law of ellipses:** each planet moves on an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. An ellipse is described by its **semi-major axis** $a$ (half the longest diameter) and its **eccentricity** $e$ (0 for a circle, approaching 1 for a very elongated orbit). The closest point to the Sun, **perihelion**, is at distance $q = a(1-e)$; the farthest, **aphelion**, is at $Q = a(1+e)$.
2. **Law of equal areas:** the line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out equal areas in equal times, so a planet moves fastest at perihelion and slowest at aphelion.
3. **Harmonic law:** the square of the orbital period is proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis, $P^2 \propto a^3$. With $P$ in years and $a$ in AU, $P^2 = a^3$ for anything orbiting the Sun. Check: Jupiter at 5.203 AU gives $P = 5.203^{3/2} = 11.87$ years, matching the observed 11.86 years.

Most planetary orbits are nearly circular: Earth's eccentricity is only 0.0167, so its distance from the Sun varies by just ±1.7%. Mercury (0.206) and Mars (0.093) have the most eccentric planetary orbits, which is why Kepler, who worked with Tycho's Mars data, could detect that orbits are not circles.

### 3.2 Deriving Kepler's laws from Newton's gravity

Newton's law of universal gravitation states that two masses $M$ and $m$ separated by a distance $r$ attract each other with a force

$$
F = \frac{G M m}{r^2}.
$$

**Second law from angular momentum.** Gravity always points along the line joining the two bodies, so it exerts no torque about the Sun, and the planet's angular momentum $\vec{L} = m\,\vec{r}\times\vec{v}$ is conserved. In a short time $dt$ the planet moves $\vec{v}\,dt$, and the thin triangle swept out has area $dA = \tfrac{1}{2}\lVert\vec{r}\times\vec{v}\,dt\rVert$. Therefore

$$
\frac{dA}{dt} = \frac{1}{2}\lVert\vec{r}\times\vec{v}\rVert = \frac{L}{2m} = \text{constant}.
$$

Equal areas in equal times is simply conservation of angular momentum in geometric form. It holds for any central force, not only gravity.

**Third law for a circular orbit.** For a planet of mass $m$ in a circular orbit of radius $a$ around a much heavier Sun, gravity provides the centripetal force:

1. Set gravitational force equal to mass times centripetal acceleration: $G M m / a^2 = m v^2 / a$.
2. Cancel $m$ and one factor of $a$: $v^2 = G M / a$, so the circular orbital speed is $v = \sqrt{GM/a}$.
3. The period is the circumference divided by the speed: $P = 2\pi a / v$.
4. Square and substitute: $P^2 = 4\pi^2 a^2 / v^2 = 4\pi^2 a^3/(GM)$.

The planet's own mass has cancelled, which is why all planets obey the same relation. When the second body is not negligible, both bodies orbit their common center of mass and the full solution of the two-body problem replaces $M$ by the total mass. The result holds for ellipses too, with $a$ the semi-major axis:

$$
P^2 = \frac{4\pi^2}{G\,(M+m)}\,a^3 .
$$

This is one of the most useful equations in astronomy because it turns two easily observed quantities (period and orbit size) into a **mass**. Every precise planetary mass known before spacecraft flybys was obtained from the orbits of that planet's moons. Expressed in years, AU and solar masses, the law becomes $P^2 = a^3/(M_{\text{total}}/M_\odot)$.

**First law (outline).** Solving Newton's equations with an inverse-square force shows that the possible paths are **conic sections** — circles, ellipses, parabolas and hyperbolas — with the attracting body at a focus. Bound orbits (negative total energy) are ellipses; a comet arriving from interstellar space with positive energy follows a hyperbola, which is how 1I/ʻOumuamua, 2I/Borisov and 3I/ATLAS were identified as interstellar visitors.

### 3.3 Orbital energy, the vis-viva equation and escape speed

For a circular orbit, the kinetic energy is $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2 = GMm/(2a)$ and the potential energy is $-GMm/a$, so the total energy is $E = -GMm/(2a)$. The same expression holds for an ellipse of semi-major axis $a$: the energy of an orbit depends only on its size, not its shape. Writing energy conservation at any distance $r$,

$$
\tfrac{1}{2} m v^2 - \frac{G M m}{r} = -\frac{G M m}{2a}
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
v^2 = G M \left( \frac{2}{r} - \frac{1}{a} \right).
$$

This is the **vis-viva equation**, the workhorse of spacecraft navigation. Setting the total energy to zero (the boundary between bound and unbound motion, equivalent to $a\to\infty$) gives the **escape speed** from distance $r$:

$$
v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}} = \sqrt{2}\,v_{\text{circ}} .
$$

For Earth's surface, $v_{\text{esc}} = 11.2$ km/s; for the Moon, 2.38 km/s; for Jupiter at its cloud tops, 59.5 km/s. A related quantity is the **surface gravity** $g = GM/R^2$, which sets the weight of a lander or an astronaut and the steepness of mountains a world can support.

### 3.4 Synodic periods: when planets line up again

The **sidereal period** is the time to orbit once relative to the stars; the **synodic period** $S$ is the time between successive identical alignments as seen from Earth (for example, between two oppositions of Mars). Each planet advances in angle at a rate $2\pi/P$, so the angle between them grows at the difference of these rates. One full lap of the faster planet over the slower one takes the synodic period:

$$
\frac{1}{S} = \left\lvert \frac{1}{P_1} - \frac{1}{P_2} \right\rvert .
$$

The same reasoning explains a planet's **solar day** (the time from noon to noon), which differs from its rotation period because the planet also moves along its orbit. For prograde rotation, $1/T_{\text{solar}} = 1/T_{\text{rot}} - 1/P_{\text{orb}}$. Mercury rotates in 58.65 days and orbits in 87.97 days, so its solar day is $1/(1/58.65 - 1/87.97) \approx 176$ days — two Mercury years. For a retrograde rotator such as Venus the minus sign becomes a plus (Practice Problem 11).

### Worked Example 1: The orbit of Halley's Comet

Halley's Comet returns about every 76 years and its orbital eccentricity is 0.967. Find the semi-major axis and the perihelion and aphelion distances.

1. Kepler's third law in solar units, with the comet's mass negligible: $a = P^{2/3}$.
2. $a = 76^{2/3}$ AU $= 17.9$ AU.
3. Perihelion: $q = a(1-e) = 17.9 \times 0.033 = 0.59$ AU — inside the orbit of Venus.
4. Aphelion: $Q = a(1+e) = 17.9 \times 1.967 = 35.3$ AU — beyond the orbit of Neptune.

**Answer:** $a \approx 17.9$ AU, $q \approx 0.59$ AU, $Q \approx 35$ AU. The comet spends most of its time far from the Sun, moving slowly (Kepler's second law), and races through the inner Solar System in a few months, which is when it grows a coma and tails.

### Worked Example 2: Weighing Jupiter with its moon Io

Io orbits Jupiter with a semi-major axis of 421 700 km and a period of 1.769 days. Find the mass of Jupiter.

1. Convert to SI units: $a = 4.217\times10^{8}$ m; $P = 1.769 \times 86\,400$ s $= 1.528\times10^{5}$ s.
2. Io's mass is less than 0.01% of Jupiter's, so use $M_J \approx 4\pi^2 a^3/(G P^2)$.
3. $a^3 = 7.499\times10^{25}\ \text{m}^3$ and $P^2 = 2.336\times10^{10}\ \text{s}^2$.
4. $M_J = \dfrac{39.48 \times 7.499\times10^{25}}{6.674\times10^{-11} \times 2.336\times10^{10}} = 1.90\times10^{27}$ kg.
5. Dividing by Earth's mass, $5.972\times10^{24}$ kg, gives about 318.

**Answer:** $M_J \approx 1.90\times10^{27}$ kg, about 318 Earth masses — in agreement with the accepted value. The same method applied to Earth's orbit (1 AU, 1 year) gives the Sun's mass, $1.989\times10^{30}$ kg.

### Worked Example 3: A Hohmann transfer from Earth to Mars

The most fuel-efficient way to travel between two circular orbits is a **Hohmann transfer**: an ellipse that touches the inner orbit at perihelion and the outer orbit at aphelion. Take Earth's orbit as 1.000 AU and Mars's as 1.524 AU.

1. Semi-major axis of the transfer ellipse: $a_t = (1.000 + 1.524)/2 = 1.262$ AU.
2. The trip is half an orbit: $t = \tfrac{1}{2} a_t^{3/2}$ years $= 0.5 \times 1.418 = 0.709$ yr $\approx 259$ days.
3. Earth's orbital speed: $v_E = \sqrt{GM_\odot/r_1} = \sqrt{1.327\times10^{20}/1.496\times10^{11}}$ m/s $= 29.78$ km/s.
4. Speed needed at the start of the transfer (vis-viva at $r = 1$ AU, $a = 1.262$ AU): $v_p = 32.73$ km/s.
5. Speed change relative to Earth's orbit: $\Delta v_1 = 32.73 - 29.78 = 2.95$ km/s (on top of escaping Earth's own gravity).
6. Arrival speed at 1.524 AU (vis-viva): $v_a = 21.48$ km/s, while Mars moves at $24.13$ km/s, so a further $\Delta v_2 = 2.65$ km/s is needed to match Mars's orbit.
7. Timing: Mars moves through $360^\circ \times 259/687 = 136^\circ$ during the trip, so at launch Mars must lead Earth by $180^\circ - 136^\circ \approx 44^\circ$.
8. This geometry repeats once per Earth–Mars synodic period: $1/S = 1/365.26 - 1/686.98$, giving $S = 780$ days.

**Answer:** a minimum-energy trip to Mars takes about 259 days (8.5 months), and launch windows open about every 780 days (26 months). Real missions follow slightly faster, non-ideal paths, but the 26-month rhythm of Mars launches is exactly this synodic period.

## 4. The Sun

| Property | Value |
|---|---|
| Mass | $1.989\times10^{30}$ kg (99.86% of the Solar System's mass) |
| Radius | 696 000 km (109 Earth radii) |
| Luminosity | $3.83\times10^{26}$ W |
| Surface (photosphere) temperature | ~5772 K |
| Core temperature | ~15.7 million K |
| Composition (by mass) | ~73% H, ~25% He, ~2% heavier elements ("metals") |
| Age | ~4.6 billion years |
| Spectral type | G2V (main-sequence yellow dwarf) |
| Rotation | ~25 days (equator) to ~35 days (poles) — differential rotation |

**Structure:** **core** (nuclear fusion via the proton–proton chain; ~600 million tonnes of hydrogen fused into helium each second), **radiative zone** (energy carried by photons in a random walk taking ~100 000+ years), **convective zone** (bubbling convection cells visible as **granulation**), **photosphere** (visible surface), **chromosphere**, and **corona** (the outer atmosphere, mysteriously heated to 1–3 million K, visible during total eclipses). The **solar wind** — a stream of charged particles at ~400–800 km/s — fills the **heliosphere**, whose boundary (the heliopause) lies ~120 AU from the Sun; Voyager 1 crossed it at about 122 AU in 2012 and Voyager 2 at about 119 AU in 2018.

**Solar activity:** sunspots (cooler regions, ~3500–4500 K, with intense magnetic fields), solar flares and **coronal mass ejections** follow an ~11-year cycle (22 years for the full magnetic cycle). Strong space weather disrupts satellites, GPS, radio and power grids, and causes vivid auroras (the 1859 Carrington event; the March 1989 storm that blacked out the Hydro-Québec grid for about nine hours; the May 2024 geomagnetic storm produced auroras seen at unusually low latitudes). Solar neutrinos confirm fusion in the core; helioseismology probes the interior.

### 4.1 The Sun's energy budget

The Sun's luminosity and surface temperature are linked by the **Stefan–Boltzmann law**: a sphere of radius $R$ radiating as a blackbody at temperature $T$ emits $L = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4$. Solving for $T$ with the nominal values $L_\odot = 3.828\times10^{26}$ W and $R_\odot = 6.957\times10^{8}$ m gives $T = 5772$ K — this is in fact how the "effective temperature" of the photosphere is defined.

Spreading the same luminosity over a sphere of radius 1 AU gives the **solar constant**:

$$
S_0 = \frac{L_\odot}{4\pi d^2} = \frac{3.828\times10^{26}\ \text{W}}{4\pi\,(1.496\times10^{11}\ \text{m})^2} \approx 1361\ \text{W/m}^2 .
$$

This single number drives Earth's climate, the energy balance of every planet (Section 10), and the design of solar panels on spacecraft. Because flux falls as $1/d^2$, a solar array at Jupiter (5.2 AU) receives only about 1/27 of the sunlight it would at Earth; the Juno spacecraft therefore carries very large solar arrays.

The energy comes from fusing four hydrogen nuclei into one helium-4 nucleus. Comparing atomic masses, $4 \times 1.007\,825$ u $= 4.031\,30$ u of hydrogen becomes $4.002\,60$ u of helium; the missing 0.028 70 u (0.71% of the original mass) is released as about 26.7 MeV of energy, according to $E = \Delta m\,c^2$.

### Worked Example 4: How fast does the Sun burn its fuel and lose mass?

1. Mass converted to energy per second: $\dot m = L_\odot/c^2 = 3.828\times10^{26} / (2.998\times10^{8})^2 = 4.26\times10^{9}$ kg/s — about 4 million tonnes every second.
2. Since only 0.71% of the hydrogen's mass becomes energy, the hydrogen fusion rate is $\dot m / 0.0071 \approx 6.0\times10^{11}$ kg/s — about 600 million tonnes per second, the figure quoted above.
3. Over 4.6 billion years ($4.6\times10^{9} \times 3.156\times10^{7}$ s $= 1.45\times10^{17}$ s), the mass converted to energy is $4.26\times10^{9} \times 1.45\times10^{17} \approx 6.2\times10^{26}$ kg.
4. As a fraction of the Sun's mass: $6.2\times10^{26}/1.989\times10^{30} \approx 3\times10^{-4}$, i.e. about 0.03%.

**Answer:** the Sun loses about 4 million tonnes of mass per second to radiation, yet over its whole life so far this amounts to only about 0.03% of its mass (assuming roughly constant luminosity; the young Sun was actually about 30% fainter). The Sun is long-lived because it is enormous, not because fusion is slow in an absolute sense.

## 5. Formation of the Solar System: The Nebular Hypothesis

Proposed by Immanuel Kant (1755) and Pierre-Simon Laplace (1796), refined by modern observations:
1. ~4.6 billion years ago, a cloud of gas and dust (a fragment of a giant molecular cloud, perhaps triggered by a nearby supernova) collapsed under gravity.
2. Conservation of angular momentum made it spin faster and flatten into a **protoplanetary disk** with the proto-Sun at the center. (This explains why planets orbit in nearly the same plane and the same direction as the Sun's rotation.)
3. The Sun ignited when its core reached fusion temperatures.
4. In the disk, dust grains stuck together into pebbles, then **planetesimals** (kilometer-sized), which collided and merged into **protoplanets** (accretion). The planetesimal picture was developed quantitatively by Viktor Safronov in the 1960s.
5. **The frost line** (~3–5 AU): inside it, only rock and metal could condense → small, rocky **terrestrial planets**. Beyond it, abundant ices allowed large cores (~10 Earth masses) that captured hydrogen and helium gas → **giant planets**.
6. The young Sun's strong solar wind (T Tauri phase) cleared remaining gas within a few million years.
7. Planetary migration (e.g. the "Nice model" and "Grand Tack" hypotheses) rearranged the giant planets, scattering small bodies — possibly causing the Late Heavy Bombardment (~4.1–3.8 billion years ago, though its reality is debated).

**Evidence:** the oldest solids (calcium–aluminum-rich inclusions in meteorites) date to 4.567 billion years; we observe protoplanetary disks around young stars (ALMA's 2014 image of HL Tauri showed gaps carved by forming planets).

### 5.1 Why the frost line falls where it does

A rough location for the frost line follows from energy balance (derived in Section 10.1). A small, dark grain at distance $d$ from the Sun, absorbing sunlight on its cross-section and radiating from its whole surface, settles at

$$
T \approx 278\ \text{K} \times \left(\frac{d}{1\ \text{AU}}\right)^{-1/2} .
$$

Water ice is stable in the low-pressure disk below roughly 150–170 K. Setting $T = 150$ K gives $d = (278/150)^2 \approx 3.4$ AU; setting $T = 170$ K gives about 2.7 AU. The young Sun was fainter, which pulls these estimates inward, while heating from accretion inside the dense disk pushes them outward, so the true frost line was somewhere near 3 AU and moved as the disk evolved. Today the asteroid belt straddles this boundary: inner-belt asteroids are mostly dry, stony S-types, while outer-belt C-types contain water-bearing minerals — a fossil record of the ancient frost line.

### 5.2 The angular momentum puzzle

The Sun holds 99.86% of the Solar System's mass but less than 1% of its angular momentum; Jupiter's orbit alone carries about 60% of the planets' total. A contracting cloud should have spun its central star up dramatically, so the young Sun must have shed angular momentum. The accepted explanation is **magnetic braking**: the young Sun's magnetic field was coupled to the surrounding disk and to its outflowing wind, which carried angular momentum away. Similar braking is observed today — young stars spin fast and old stars spin slowly.

### 5.3 Dating the Solar System

The ages quoted in this chapter come from **radiometric dating**. A radioactive parent isotope decays to a daughter at a fixed rate, so the ratio of daughter to parent in a closed sample measures the time since it solidified: $t = \dfrac{t_{1/2}}{\ln 2}\,\ln\!\left(1 + \dfrac{D}{P}\right)$, where $t_{1/2}$ is the half-life and $D/P$ the daughter-to-parent ratio. The uranium–lead system is especially powerful because two uranium isotopes (²³⁸U, half-life 4.47 billion years, and ²³⁵U, half-life 0.704 billion years) decay to two different lead isotopes, giving two independent clocks in the same mineral. Lead–lead dating of CAIs gives 4.567 billion years for the first solids, and lunar samples, Martian meteorites and the oldest Earth minerals (zircons about 4.4 billion years old from Western Australia) all fit within the following few hundred million years.

### Terrestrial (inner) planets
Rocky, dense, small, few or no moons, no rings, with differentiated interiors (iron cores, rocky mantles and crusts).

- **Mercury:** smallest planet; no significant atmosphere; heavily cratered; a very large iron core (~85% of its radius); a 3:2 spin–orbit resonance (rotates three times for every two orbits: $2 \times 87.97/3 = 58.65$ days). Water ice exists in permanently shadowed polar craters. Explored by Mariner 10, MESSENGER (orbited 2011–2015) and BepiColombo (arriving in orbit in late 2026). Because its orbit is so eccentric, Mercury receives $(1.206/0.794)^2 \approx 2.3$ times as much sunlight at perihelion as at aphelion.
- **Venus:** similar in size to Earth but radically different — a **runaway greenhouse effect** from a thick CO₂ atmosphere (96.5%) with sulfuric acid clouds raises the surface temperature to ~465 °C, hot enough to melt lead, and the pressure is 92 times Earth's. It rotates backward (retrograde) very slowly — a Venusian day is longer than its year (243 Earth days sidereal rotation vs. 225-day orbit). The surface is volcanic and geologically young (~300–700 million years). Soviet Venera landers survived up to ~2 hours (1970s–80s). Brightest planet in our sky.
- **Earth:** the only known world with life and stable surface liquid water; active plate tectonics; strong magnetic field; nitrogen–oxygen atmosphere (oxygen produced by photosynthesis); a large moon that stabilizes its axial tilt.
- **Mars:** the "Red Planet" (iron oxide dust). Thin CO₂ atmosphere (~0.6% of Earth's surface pressure); average temperature about −60 °C. Hosts **Olympus Mons**, the largest volcano in the Solar System (~22 km high, ~600 km wide), and **Valles Marineris**, a canyon system ~4000 km long. Low surface gravity (about 38% of Earth's) is part of the reason such tall volcanoes can stand. Evidence of ancient rivers, lakes and deltas (e.g. Jezero Crater, explored by Perseverance) shows Mars was once warmer and wetter; it lost much of its atmosphere after its magnetic field died (solar wind stripping, studied by MAVEN). Water ice in polar caps and underground. Two small moons, Phobos and Deimos (probably captured asteroids or debris from an impact). Rovers: Sojourner (1997), Spirit and Opportunity (2004), Curiosity (2012), Perseverance with the Ingenuity helicopter (2021 — the first powered flight on another world), China's Zhurong (2021).

### Giant (outer) planets
Large, low-density, composed mostly of hydrogen and helium (gas giants) or water, ammonia and methane ices (ice giants); all have rings and many moons; rapid rotation; no solid surface.

- **Jupiter:** more than twice the mass of all other planets combined. Banded cloud belts and zones; the **Great Red Spot** is a storm larger than Earth, observed since at least 1830 (possibly 1665) and slowly shrinking. Interior: molecular hydrogen, then **metallic hydrogen** generating a powerful magnetic field (~20 000 times Earth's magnetic moment) and intense radiation belts. Radiates more heat than it receives from the Sun (Practice Problem 6). Its gravity may have shaped the asteroid belt and deflected or attracted comets (Shoemaker–Levy 9 struck Jupiter in 1994). Missions: Pioneer, Voyager, Galileo (1995–2003), Juno (since 2016), JUICE and Europa Clipper (en route).
  - **Galilean moons:** **Io** (most volcanically active body known, heated by tidal flexing), **Europa** (ice shell over a global salty ocean — a prime target in the search for life), **Ganymede** (the largest moon in the Solar System, bigger than Mercury, with its own magnetic field and a subsurface ocean), **Callisto** (ancient, heavily cratered). Io, Europa and Ganymede are locked in a **Laplace resonance**: their periods (1.769, 3.551 and 7.155 days) are almost exactly in the ratio 1 : 2 : 4. The repeated, regular tugs keep their orbits slightly eccentric, so the tides they feel keep changing — this ongoing flexing is what heats Io's volcanoes and Europa's ocean.
- **Saturn:** least dense planet (would float in a large enough bathtub). Its **rings** — made of countless water-ice particles from micrometers to meters across — span ~280 000 km but are typically only ~10 meters thick. They may be relatively young (perhaps a few hundred million years) and are slowly raining onto the planet. Hexagonal jet stream at the north pole. The Cassini–Huygens mission (2004–2017) ended by diving into Saturn.
  - **Titan:** the second-largest moon; the only moon with a thick atmosphere (mostly nitrogen, 1.5 bar) and stable surface liquids — lakes and seas of **liquid methane and ethane**, with a methane "hydrological cycle". The Huygens probe landed in 2005 (the most distant landing ever). NASA's Dragonfly rotorcraft is planned to arrive in the 2030s.
  - **Enceladus:** small icy moon with **geysers** of water vapor and ice jetting from "tiger stripe" fractures near its south pole, fed by a subsurface ocean containing salts, silica, organic molecules and molecular hydrogen (suggesting hydrothermal activity) — and phosphates detected in 2023.
- **Uranus:** an ice giant tilted 98°, perhaps from an ancient giant impact; each pole experiences ~42 years of continuous daylight followed by ~42 years of darkness. Blue-green color from methane absorbing red light. Faint rings (discovered 1977). Visited only by Voyager 2 (1986).
- **Neptune:** deep blue; the windiest planet; the Great Dark Spot (observed by Voyager 2 in 1989) was a transient storm. Its largest moon **Triton** orbits in the opposite direction to Neptune's rotation (retrograde), indicating it is a captured Kuiper belt object; it has nitrogen geysers and is slowly spiraling inward.

## 7. Earth's Moon

- Diameter 3474 km (27% of Earth's — the largest moon relative to its planet, except Pluto–Charon); mass 1/81 of Earth's; surface gravity 1/6 of Earth's; mean distance 384 400 km.
- **Synchronous rotation (tidal locking):** the Moon rotates once per orbit (27.3 days sidereal), always showing the same face. The far side is not "dark" — it receives as much sunlight as the near side. Tidal locking is the end state of tidal friction: Earth's gravity raised bulges in the young, faster-spinning Moon, and the torque on those bulges slowed its spin until rotation and orbit matched. Most large moons in the Solar System are locked in the same way.
- **Phases** (29.5-day synodic cycle): new → waxing crescent → first quarter → waxing gibbous → full → waning gibbous → third quarter → waning crescent. Phases result from our changing view of the Moon's sunlit half, **not** from Earth's shadow. The synodic month is longer than the sidereal month because Earth moves about 29° along its orbit each month, so the Moon must travel a little more than one full circle to return to the same Sun–Earth–Moon geometry: $1/29.53 \approx 1/27.32 - 1/365.26$.
- **Surface:** dark **maria** (basaltic plains from ancient lava flows, ~3–3.9 billion years old) and bright, heavily cratered **highlands**; a powdery **regolith**; no atmosphere, so craters persist for billions of years. Water ice exists in permanently shadowed polar craters (target of NASA's Artemis program and other missions). Counting craters is a dating tool: older surfaces have accumulated more craters, and calibrating crater density against the radiometric ages of Apollo and Luna samples lets geologists estimate ages for unsampled surfaces on the Moon, Mars and Mercury.
- **Origin: the giant impact hypothesis.** About 4.5 billion years ago, a Mars-sized protoplanet (**Theia**) collided with the proto-Earth; debris from both formed a disk that coalesced into the Moon. Evidence: the Moon's small iron core (the impact flung out mainly mantle rock), depletion of volatile elements (lost in the hot debris disk), and the system's high angular momentum. The Moon's oxygen isotope ratios are nearly identical to Earth's; this shows the Moon formed largely from Earth-like material, but it is also a puzzle, because simple impact simulations predict a Moon made mostly of Theia. More energetic impacts, thorough mixing in the debris, or a Theia that formed close to Earth have all been proposed to explain it.
- **Exploration:** Luna 2 (first impact, 1959), Luna 3 (first far-side images, 1959), Apollo 11 (Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin, 20 July 1969); twelve astronauts walked on the Moon (1969–1972) and returned 382 kg of samples. China's Chang'e 4 made the first far-side landing (2019), and Chang'e 6 returned the first far-side samples (2024). India's Chandrayaan-3 landed near the south pole (2023).

### 7.1 Tides
The Moon's gravity (and to a lesser extent the Sun's) produces two tidal bulges on Earth, giving most coasts two high tides per day (every ~12 h 25 min). **Spring tides** (largest range) at new and full moon; **neap tides** at quarter phases. Tidal friction slows Earth's rotation (the day lengthens ~2 ms per century; ~400 million years ago a year had ~400 days, as recorded in coral growth bands) and pushes the Moon outward by ~3.8 cm per year (measured by laser ranging to Apollo retroreflectors).

**Why two bulges?** Tides are caused not by the Moon's pull itself but by the *difference* in its pull across Earth. The side of Earth facing the Moon is about one Earth radius closer than Earth's center and is pulled more strongly; the far side is pulled less strongly. Relative to Earth's center, the near-side water is pulled toward the Moon and the far-side water is "left behind" — two bulges on opposite sides. Expanding the Moon's acceleration $GM/r^2$ about the center-to-Moon distance $d$:

$$
\Delta a = \frac{GM}{(d-R)^2} - \frac{GM}{d^2} \approx \frac{GM}{d^2}\left(1 + \frac{2R}{d}\right) - \frac{GM}{d^2} = \frac{2\,G M R}{d^3} .
$$

The key feature is the $1/d^3$ dependence: tidal effects fall off faster than gravity itself. As Earth rotates under the two bulges, a coast passes through both each day. Because the Moon advances in its orbit, the cycle repeats every 24 h 50 min rather than every 24 h, so successive high tides are ~12 h 25 min apart. Real ocean tides are strongly shaped by coastlines and basin resonances — the Bay of Fundy in Canada has a range of over 15 m, while parts of the Mediterranean have ranges of a few tens of centimeters.

**Tidal friction and the receding Moon.** Earth spins faster than the Moon orbits, so friction drags the tidal bulges slightly ahead of the Earth–Moon line. The Moon's gravity pulls back on the leading bulge, slowing Earth's spin; the bulge in turn pulls the Moon forward, adding orbital energy and raising its orbit. Total angular momentum of the Earth–Moon system is conserved: what Earth's spin loses, the Moon's orbit gains.

### Worked Example 5: Comparing the tides of the Moon and the Sun

The Sun's gravitational pull on Earth is far stronger than the Moon's. Which body raises the larger tide?

1. Direct pull of each body at Earth's center: Moon, $GM_{\text{Moon}}/d^2 = 6.674\times10^{-11} \times 7.342\times10^{22}/(3.844\times10^{8})^2 = 3.3\times10^{-5}$ m/s²; Sun, $GM_\odot/(1\ \text{AU})^2 = 5.9\times10^{-3}$ m/s². The Sun pulls about 180 times harder.
2. Tidal acceleration from the Moon: $2GM_{\text{Moon}}R_\oplus/d^3 = 2 \times 6.674\times10^{-11} \times 7.342\times10^{22} \times 6.371\times10^{6}/(3.844\times10^{8})^3 = 1.10\times10^{-6}$ m/s².
3. Tidal acceleration from the Sun: $2GM_\odot R_\oplus/(1\ \text{AU})^3 = 5.05\times10^{-7}$ m/s².
4. Ratio: $1.10\times10^{-6}/5.05\times10^{-7} = 2.18$. Equivalently, $(M_{\text{Moon}}/M_\odot)(d_\odot/d_{\text{Moon}})^3 = 3.69\times10^{-8} \times 389^3 = 2.18$.
5. At spring tide the effects add; at neap tide they partly cancel. The ratio of the idealized tidal ranges is $(1 + 0.46)/(1 - 0.46) \approx 2.7$.

**Answer:** the Moon's tidal effect is about 2.2 times the Sun's (the solar tide is about 46% of the lunar tide), even though the Sun's overall pull is about 180 times stronger. The $1/d^3$ dependence favors the nearby Moon. Note also how tiny the tidal acceleration is: about $10^{-7}$ of $g$, yet acting on whole ocean basins it moves enormous volumes of water.

### 7.2 Eclipses
- **Solar eclipse:** the Moon passes between the Sun and Earth (at new moon). A **total** eclipse occurs because the Moon and Sun have nearly the same angular size (the Sun is ~400 times larger and ~400 times farther) — a cosmic coincidence that will not last forever, as the Moon recedes. **Annular** eclipses occur when the Moon is near apogee. Totality reveals the corona and lasts at most ~7.5 minutes.
- **Lunar eclipse:** Earth's shadow falls on the Moon (at full moon); during totality the Moon turns coppery red because Earth's atmosphere refracts red sunlight into the shadow (the light of all the world's sunrises and sunsets).
- Eclipses don't happen every month because the Moon's orbit is tilted ~5° to the ecliptic; they occur only when new or full moon coincides with the Moon crossing the ecliptic (eclipse seasons). The **Saros cycle** (~18 years 11 days) predicts recurring eclipses.

The numbers behind the coincidence: an object of radius $r$ at distance $d$ has angular diameter $\theta \approx 2r/d$ (in radians). The Moon's angular diameter ranges from about 0.49° at apogee (405 500 km) to about 0.55° at perigee (363 300 km); the Sun's ranges from about 0.524° in early July (aphelion) to about 0.542° in early January (perihelion). When the Moon appears larger than the Sun the eclipse can be total; when smaller, the result is an annular "ring of fire". Because the Moon recedes, total eclipses will eventually become impossible — on a crude estimate using today's recession rate, in several hundred million years.

### 7.3 The Roche limit and planetary rings

A moon held together only by its own gravity is torn apart if it comes too close to its planet, where the planet's tidal stretching exceeds the moon's self-gravity. A simple estimate considers a loose particle on the surface of a moon of radius $r$ and mass $m$, on the side facing a planet of mass $M$ and radius $R$, at distance $d$:

1. The moon's gravity holds the particle with acceleration $Gm/r^2$.
2. The tidal acceleration pulling it away is $2GMr/d^3$ (from the tidal formula above).
3. Disruption begins when they are equal: $d^3 = 2Mr^3/m$.
4. Writing $M = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3 \rho_M$ and $m = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho_m$ gives the rigid-body result $d \approx 1.26\,R\,(\rho_M/\rho_m)^{1/3}$.

A fluid moon deforms into an elongated shape, which makes it easier to disrupt; Roche's detailed calculation gives

$$
d_{\text{Roche}} \approx 2.44\,R\left(\frac{\rho_M}{\rho_m}\right)^{1/3}.
$$

For Saturn ($R = 60\,268$ km, $\rho = 687$ kg/m³) and icy material ($\rho \approx 920$ kg/m³), the fluid Roche limit is about $2.44 \times 60\,268 \times 0.907 \approx 133\,000$ km. Saturn's bright main rings end at about 137 000 km — essentially at the Roche limit — while large moons orbit farther out. This is why ring material does not clump into a moon: inside the limit, tides pull aggregates apart as fast as they gather. Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 was a real example: a close pass by Jupiter in 1992 tore it into a string of more than 20 fragments, which struck the planet two years later. (Real rocky or icy bodies have material strength, so small solid moons and spacecraft can orbit safely inside the Roche limit.)

## 8. Seasons

Seasons are caused by Earth's **axial tilt (23.44°)**, not by changes in distance from the Sun (Earth is actually closest to the Sun — perihelion — in early January, during the Northern Hemisphere winter).
- When a hemisphere tilts toward the Sun, sunlight strikes more directly (more energy per unit area) and days are longer → summer.
- **Solstices** (~21 June and ~21 December): the Sun reaches its farthest north (Tropic of Cancer, 23.44° N) or south (Tropic of Capricorn). **Equinoxes** (~20 March and ~22–23 September): day and night are roughly equal everywhere.
- The **Arctic and Antarctic Circles** (66.56°) experience at least one day of midnight sun and polar night.
- Over ~26 000 years, the axis precesses; tilt and orbital eccentricity also vary in **Milankovitch cycles** (41 000-year tilt cycle; ~100 000-year eccentricity cycles), pacing ice ages.

### 8.1 The geometry of sunlight

Two effects combine. First, a beam of sunlight of flux $S$ striking the ground at altitude angle $h$ above the horizon spreads over a larger area when the Sun is low, so the energy per unit horizontal area is $S \sin h$. Second, the length of daylight changes with the Sun's **declination** $\delta$ (its angle north or south of the celestial equator, ranging from +23.44° in June to −23.44° in December). At latitude $\varphi$ in the Northern Hemisphere the noon altitude of the Sun is

$$
h_{\text{noon}} = 90^\circ - \varphi + \delta ,
$$

and sunrise and sunset occur at hour angle $H_0$ (measured from noon, 15° per hour) given by $\cos H_0 = -\tan\varphi\,\tan\delta$, so the day length is $2H_0/15^\circ$ hours. When $\tan\varphi\tan\delta > 1$ there is no solution — the Sun never sets (or never rises), which happens poleward of $90^\circ - 23.44^\circ = 66.56^\circ$, the polar circles.

### Worked Example 6: Summer versus winter sunlight at 40° N

1. June solstice: $h_{\text{noon}} = 90^\circ - 40^\circ + 23.44^\circ = 73.44^\circ$, so $\sin h = 0.959$.
2. December solstice: $h_{\text{noon}} = 90^\circ - 40^\circ - 23.44^\circ = 26.56^\circ$, so $\sin h = 0.447$.
3. At noon, the energy per square meter of ground in June is $0.959/0.447 = 2.1$ times that in December.
4. Day length in June: $\cos H_0 = -\tan 40^\circ \tan 23.44^\circ = -0.364$, so $H_0 = 111.3^\circ$ and daylight lasts $2 \times 111.3/15 = 14.8$ hours. In December, $H_0 = 68.7^\circ$ and daylight lasts 9.2 hours.
5. Integrating $S \sin h$ over each day gives a total daily insolation about 3.3 times larger at the June solstice than at the December solstice.
6. Compare the distance effect: Earth's eccentricity of 0.0167 changes the Sun's flux between aphelion and perihelion by a factor of $\left(\frac{1+e}{1-e}\right)^2 = 1.069$, only about 7%.

**Answer:** at 40° N, a summer day delivers more than three times the solar energy of a winter day, mainly because of the tilt-driven changes in Sun angle and day length; the 7% distance effect is much smaller and works in the "wrong" direction for the Northern Hemisphere (perihelion falls in northern winter).

## 9. Small Bodies

### Dwarf planets
In 2006 the International Astronomical Union defined a **planet** as a body that (1) orbits the Sun, (2) is massive enough to be nearly round (hydrostatic equilibrium), and (3) has **cleared its orbital neighborhood**. A **dwarf planet** meets (1) and (2) but not (3). Recognized dwarf planets: **Ceres** (largest object in the asteroid belt, ~940 km; visited by Dawn, which found bright salt deposits), **Pluto**, **Eris** (slightly smaller than Pluto but more massive; its discovery in 2005 triggered the reclassification), **Haumea** (rapidly spinning, elongated, with rings), **Makemake**.

Criterion (2) has a physical basis. A body becomes round when its self-gravity can deform rock or ice — when the pressure at its center exceeds the material's strength. For icy bodies this happens at diameters of a few hundred kilometers; rocky bodies need to be somewhat larger. That is why Ceres, about 940 km across and rich in ice, is round, while the rocky asteroid Vesta, a little over 500 km across, is not quite round (its shape was also battered by a huge impact near its south pole).

**Pluto** (diameter 2377 km — smaller than Earth's Moon) has five known moons; the largest, **Charon**, is half Pluto's diameter (the two orbit a point between them — a binary system). NASA's **New Horizons** flyby (July 2015) revealed a surprisingly active world: nitrogen-ice glaciers in the heart-shaped Sputnik Planitia, water-ice mountains, a thin hazy atmosphere, and possibly a subsurface ocean. New Horizons then flew past **Arrokoth** (2019), a pristine "contact binary" Kuiper belt object. Pluto's orbit (semi-major axis 39.5 AU, period 248 years) is in a 3:2 resonance with Neptune's: Pluto completes two orbits for every three of Neptune, and this protects it from close encounters even though its eccentric orbit crosses inside Neptune's.

### Asteroids
Rocky and metallic bodies, mostly in the **main asteroid belt** between Mars and Jupiter (~2.1–3.3 AU), where Jupiter's gravity prevented a planet from forming. Over a million known; total mass only ~3% of the Moon's. Types: carbonaceous (C-type, dark, primitive), silicate (S-type), metallic (M-type, e.g. 16 Psyche, target of NASA's Psyche mission). **Kirkwood gaps** are cleared by orbital resonances with Jupiter. **Trojan asteroids** share Jupiter's orbit at its L4 and L5 points (explored by NASA's Lucy mission). **Near-Earth asteroids** are tracked for impact hazards; NASA's **DART** mission (2022) deliberately crashed into the moonlet Dimorphos, shortening its orbit around Didymos by ~32 minutes — the first demonstration of planetary defense. Sample-return missions: Hayabusa (Itokawa), Hayabusa2 (Ryugu, 2020), OSIRIS-REx (Bennu, 2023) — finding amino acids and other organic molecules.

**Locating the Kirkwood gaps.** An asteroid whose period is an exact fraction of Jupiter's receives Jupiter's tug at the same point of its orbit again and again, and the repeated kicks pump up its eccentricity until it is thrown out of the region. Kepler's third law locates these resonances: an orbit with period $P_J\,(q/p)$ has semi-major axis $a = a_J\,(q/p)^{2/3}$. With $a_J = 5.203$ AU, the 3:1 resonance (three asteroid orbits per Jupiter orbit) lies at $5.203 \times (1/3)^{2/3} = 2.50$ AU, the 5:2 at 2.82 AU, the 7:3 at 2.96 AU and the 2:1 at 3.28 AU — exactly where the major gaps are observed. The 2:1 resonance roughly marks the outer edge of the main belt.

**The belt is mostly empty space.** Despite the dramatic images in films, the average distance between asteroids larger than about 1 km is on the order of a million kilometers. Spacecraft cross the belt routinely without maneuvering to avoid anything; visiting a particular asteroid requires careful targeting.

### Comets
Icy bodies ("dirty snowballs" — or "icy dirtballs") of ice, dust and organic compounds, typically a few kilometers across. When near the Sun, ices sublimate, forming a glowing **coma** and two **tails** that point away from the Sun: a straight blue **ion tail** (pushed by the solar wind) and a curved **dust tail** (pushed by radiation pressure).
- **Short-period comets** have periods under 200 years. **Jupiter-family comets** (periods below about 20 years, such as Comet 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko, studied by ESA's **Rosetta** and the Philae lander in 2014–2016) originate mainly in the **Kuiper belt** and the related scattered disk (the Kuiper belt is a disk of icy bodies beyond Neptune at roughly 30–50 AU, including Pluto). **Halley-type comets** (periods of about 20–200 years, such as Halley's Comet, 76 years, next return 2061) have orbits with a wide range of inclinations — Halley's is even retrograde — and are thought to come mostly from the Oort cloud.
- **Long-period comets** come from the **Oort cloud**, a hypothesized spherical shell of trillions of icy bodies at ~2000–100 000 AU, perturbed inward by passing stars and galactic tides.
- Comets and asteroids may have delivered water and organic compounds to early Earth. The deuterium-to-hydrogen (D/H) ratio of water is a test of where Earth's water came from: Rosetta's measurements of gas around 67P gave roughly three times the D/H ratio of Earth's oceans, suggesting that comets like it supplied little of Earth's water (later analyses have argued that dust can bias such measurements), while water-bearing carbonaceous asteroids match Earth's ratio well.
- **Interstellar objects:** 1I/ʻOumuamua (2017), 2I/Borisov (2019) and 3I/ATLAS (2025) passed through the Solar System from other star systems.

### Meteoroids, meteors and meteorites
- **Meteoroid:** small rock in space. **Meteor:** the streak of light as it burns up in the atmosphere ("shooting star"). **Meteorite:** a piece that reaches the ground.
- **Meteor showers** (Perseids in August, Geminids in December, Leonids in November) occur when Earth passes through debris trails left by comets (the Geminids come from asteroid 3200 Phaethon).
- Types of meteorites: stony (chondrites — the most primitive material in the Solar System, containing chondrules and CAIs; carbonaceous chondrites contain amino acids, as convincingly established for the Murchison meteorite that fell in Australia in 1969), iron, and stony-iron. Some meteorites come from Mars and the Moon, blasted off by impacts; Martian meteorites are identified by gas trapped in them whose composition matches the atmosphere measured by the Viking landers.
- **Impacts:** ~100 tonnes of material hit Earth daily. The Chelyabinsk meteor (2013, ~20 m) injured ~1500 people; the Tunguska event (1908) flattened ~2000 km² of Siberian forest; the Chicxulub impact (66 Mya, ~10 km) caused the extinction of non-avian dinosaurs. Meteor Crater in Arizona (~50 000 years old, 1.2 km wide) is a well-preserved example.
- **Why impacts are so destructive:** objects strike Earth at speeds of at least 11 km/s (Earth's escape speed — the minimum speed gained by falling from far away) and typically about 20 km/s. At 20 km/s the kinetic energy per kilogram is $\tfrac{1}{2}v^2 = 2\times10^{8}$ J/kg, roughly 50 times the chemical energy released by a kilogram of TNT (about $4.2\times10^{6}$ J/kg). An impactor therefore behaves like an explosive many times its own mass.

## 10. Planetary Atmospheres and Habitability

An atmosphere's composition and retention depend on the planet's mass (escape velocity), temperature, magnetic field and geological activity.
- **Greenhouse effect:** gases transparent to visible light but absorbing infrared (CO₂, H₂O, CH₄) warm the surface. Venus: extreme runaway greenhouse (~465 °C vs. ~−40 °C without it); Earth: natural greenhouse effect raises the average from ~−18 °C to ~+15 °C; Mars: very thin atmosphere, little warming.
- **Habitable zone ("Goldilocks zone"):** the range of orbital distances where a planet could maintain liquid water on its surface. For the Sun, roughly 0.95–1.4 AU (estimates vary). Venus lies just inside the inner edge, Mars near the outer edge.
- **Subsurface oceans** in icy moons (Europa, Enceladus, Ganymede, Titan, possibly Callisto and others) expand the possible "habitable" real estate far beyond the classical habitable zone, heated by tidal forces.
- **Astrobiology** searches for biosignatures: Mars missions caching samples for future return; studies of Venus's clouds (a controversial 2020 claim of phosphine); missions to Europa and Enceladus' plumes.

| World | Surface pressure | Main gases | Mean surface temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Venus | 92 bar | 96.5% CO₂, 3.5% N₂ | ~465 °C |
| Earth | 1.013 bar | 78% N₂, 21% O₂, 0.9% Ar, ~0.04% CO₂ | ~15 °C |
| Mars | ~0.006 bar (varies with season) | ~95% CO₂, ~2.6% N₂, ~1.9% Ar | ~−63 °C |
| Titan | 1.5 bar | ~95% N₂, ~5% CH₄ near the surface | ~−179 °C (94 K) |

### 10.1 Energy balance and equilibrium temperature

The simplest model of a planet's temperature balances absorbed sunlight against emitted infrared radiation.

1. The flux of sunlight at distance $d$ is $L_\odot/(4\pi d^2)$.
2. The planet intercepts sunlight over its cross-sectional area $\pi R^2$ and reflects a fraction $A$, its **Bond albedo**. Absorbed power: $P_{\text{in}} = (1-A)\,\dfrac{L_\odot}{4\pi d^2}\,\pi R^2$.
3. A planet that spins fast enough (or has an atmosphere and ocean that spread heat around) radiates from its whole surface, $4\pi R^2$, as a blackbody: $P_{\text{out}} = 4\pi R^2 \sigma T^4$.
4. Setting $P_{\text{in}} = P_{\text{out}}$, the radius cancels:

$$
T_{\text{eq}} = \left[\frac{(1-A)\,L_\odot}{16\pi\sigma d^2}\right]^{1/4} = \left[\frac{(1-A)\,S_0}{4\sigma}\right]^{1/4}\left(\frac{d}{1\ \text{AU}}\right)^{-1/2} .
$$

The factor of 4 is the ratio of the emitting area to the intercepting area. The temperature falls only as the square root of distance and depends only weakly on albedo (the fourth root). The difference between the actual mean surface temperature and $T_{\text{eq}}$ measures the greenhouse effect (or, for the giant planets, an internal heat source).

| Planet | Bond albedo (approx.) | Equilibrium temperature | Actual mean surface temperature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Venus | 0.76 | ~229 K (−44 °C) | ~738 K (465 °C) |
| Earth | 0.30 | ~255 K (−18 °C) | ~288 K (15 °C) |
| Mars | 0.25 | ~210 K (−63 °C) | ~210 K (−63 °C) |
| Jupiter | 0.34 | ~110 K | effective temperature ~125 K (internal heat) |

The comparison is striking. Venus reflects so much sunlight from its bright clouds that its equilibrium temperature is actually *lower* than Earth's, yet its surface is about 500 K hotter because its massive CO₂ atmosphere traps outgoing infrared radiation. Mars, with almost no atmosphere, sits close to its equilibrium temperature.

### Worked Example 7: Earth's equilibrium temperature and the size of the greenhouse effect

1. Solar flux at Earth: $S_0 = 1361$ W/m². Bond albedo: $A = 0.30$.
2. Average absorbed flux per square meter of Earth's surface: $(1-A)S_0/4 = 0.70 \times 1361/4 = 238$ W/m².
3. Set equal to $\sigma T^4$: $T^4 = 238/5.670\times10^{-8} = 4.20\times10^{9}$ K⁴.
4. Take the fourth root: $T_{\text{eq}} = 255$ K $= -18$ °C.
5. The observed global mean surface temperature is about 288 K, so the greenhouse effect contributes about $288 - 255 = 33$ K of warming.
6. Repeating for Mars ($d = 1.524$ AU, $A = 0.25$): absorbed flux $= 0.75 \times 1361/(4 \times 1.524^2) = 110$ W/m², giving $T_{\text{eq}} = 210$ K, close to Mars's actual mean.

**Answer:** without its greenhouse gases Earth would average about −18 °C, below the freezing point of water; the natural greenhouse effect of water vapor and CO₂ raises it by about 33 K to a habitable +15 °C. Mars's thin atmosphere adds almost nothing.

### 10.2 Why some worlds keep their air: thermal escape

Gas molecules in an atmosphere move with a range of speeds set by temperature. By the **equipartition theorem**, the average translational kinetic energy per molecule is $\tfrac{3}{2}k_B T$, so the root-mean-square speed is

$$
v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{\frac{3 k_B T}{m}} ,
$$

where $m$ is the molecular mass. Light molecules move fastest. A small fraction of molecules in the high-speed tail of the Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution exceed the escape speed and, if they are high enough in the atmosphere (in the **exosphere**, where collisions are rare), leak away into space. This is **Jeans escape**. A widely used rule of thumb is that a planet keeps a gas for billions of years if its escape speed exceeds about six times the gas's rms speed at the exosphere temperature; gases with a smaller ratio are gradually lost.

### Worked Example 8: Can Earth, the Moon and Titan keep their atmospheres?

Use $m = (\text{molar mass in u}) \times 1.6605\times10^{-27}$ kg and $k_B = 1.381\times10^{-23}$ J/K.

1. Earth's escape speed is 11.2 km/s. Its exosphere is hot — about 1000 K on average, varying with solar activity.
2. Hydrogen molecules (H₂, 2.016 u) at 1000 K: $v_{\text{rms}} = \sqrt{3 \times 1.381\times10^{-23} \times 1000/(2.016 \times 1.6605\times10^{-27})} = 3.52$ km/s. Ratio $11.2/3.52 = 3.2$, well below 6: **hydrogen escapes**. (Helium, at 2.50 km/s, gives a ratio of 4.5 and also leaks away, which is why helium is rare in air.)
3. Nitrogen (N₂, 28.01 u) at 1000 K: $v_{\text{rms}} = 0.94$ km/s. Ratio $11.2/0.94 = 11.9$: **nitrogen is retained**. Oxygen and CO₂ are heavier and are retained as well.
4. The Moon: escape speed 2.38 km/s. In daytime sunlight its surface reaches about 390 K, where N₂ has $v_{\text{rms}} = 0.59$ km/s. Ratio $2.38/0.59 = 4.0$: even nitrogen leaks away over geological time, consistent with the Moon being airless.
5. Titan: escape speed only 2.64 km/s — similar to the Moon's — but its atmosphere is cold. Even in its upper atmosphere, where escape happens, temperatures are only around 150 K, so N₂ has $v_{\text{rms}} \approx 0.37$ km/s, a ratio of about 7.

**Answer:** Earth keeps N₂ and O₂ but loses hydrogen and helium; the Moon cannot keep even nitrogen; Titan, though small, holds a thick nitrogen atmosphere largely because it is so cold. Mass and temperature together decide which gases a world can keep. (Real atmospheres also lose gas by non-thermal processes such as solar-wind stripping and photochemical escape, which were important for Mars once its magnetic field had faded.)

### 10.3 Scaling the habitable zone to other stars

The habitable zone is defined by the stellar flux a planet receives, so its distance scales with the star's luminosity. Setting $L/(4\pi d^2)$ equal to the flux at a given edge of the Sun's habitable zone gives

$$
d_{\text{HZ}} = d_{\text{HZ},\odot}\,\sqrt{\frac{L}{L_\odot}} .
$$

A star with 4% of the Sun's luminosity has a habitable zone $\sqrt{0.04} = 0.2$ times as far out, roughly 0.19–0.28 AU. For dim red dwarfs the habitable zone is so close in that planets there are probably tidally locked and exposed to strong flares — factors that complicate their habitability (Practice Problem 10).

## 11. Exoplanets

The first exoplanet around a Sun-like star, **51 Pegasi b**, was discovered in 1995 by Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz (Nobel 2019) — a "hot Jupiter" orbiting its star every 4.2 days. (Planets around a pulsar had been found in 1992.) As of 2025, over **6000** exoplanets are confirmed.

### Detection methods
- **Radial velocity (Doppler wobble):** a planet's gravity makes its star wobble, shifting its spectral lines; gives minimum planet mass. Jupiter makes the Sun wobble at ~12.5 m/s; Earth only ~0.09 m/s.
- **Transit method:** a planet passing in front of its star dims it slightly (Earth transiting the Sun: ~0.008%; Jupiter: ~1%). Gives planet radius and, with radial velocity, density. NASA's **Kepler** (2009–2018) found ~2700 confirmed planets this way; **TESS** (since 2018) surveys bright nearby stars. **Transit spectroscopy** reveals atmospheric composition (JWST has detected H₂O, CO₂, CH₄, SO₂ and more).
- **Direct imaging:** blocking starlight to photograph planets (HR 8799's four giant planets).
- **Gravitational microlensing:** a planet's gravity briefly magnifies a background star.
- **Astrometry** (Gaia) and **transit timing variations**.

| Method | What it measures | Easiest to find |
|---|---|---|
| Radial velocity | Minimum mass, period, eccentricity | Massive planets close to their stars |
| Transit | Radius, period; atmosphere via spectroscopy | Large planets on short orbits around small stars |
| Direct imaging | Brightness, colors, orbit on the sky | Young, hot giant planets far from their stars |
| Microlensing | Mass ratio and projected separation | Planets at a few AU, even around faint stars |
| Astrometry | Mass and full orbit | Massive planets on wide orbits around nearby stars |

### The physics of the two main methods

**Transit depth.** When a planet of radius $R_p$ crosses the disk of a star of radius $R_*$, it blocks a fraction of the light equal to the ratio of their areas (ignoring the darkening of the star's limb):

$$
\frac{\Delta F}{F} = \left(\frac{R_p}{R_*}\right)^2 .
$$

For Earth and the Sun this is $(6371/695\,700)^2 = 8.4\times10^{-5}$ (84 parts per million); for Jupiter, $(69\,911/695\,700)^2 = 1.0\%$. A transit is only seen if the orbit is nearly edge-on; the chance of the right alignment is roughly $R_*/a$, about 0.5% for an Earth analog, which is why Kepler had to monitor about 150 000 stars at once.

**Radial-velocity amplitude.** The star and planet orbit their common center of mass, so their momenta are equal and opposite: $M_* v_* = m_p v_p$. For a circular orbit, the relative orbital speed follows from Kepler's third law, $v = 2\pi a/P = (2\pi G M_{\text{tot}}/P)^{1/3}$, and the star's share of the motion is $m_p/M_{\text{tot}}$. Including the orbital inclination $i$ (only the line-of-sight component is measured):

$$
K = \left(\frac{2\pi G}{P}\right)^{1/3}\frac{m_p \sin i}{(M_* + m_p)^{2/3}} .
$$

Because only $m_p \sin i$ appears, radial velocities alone give a minimum mass; combining with a transit (which requires $i \approx 90^\circ$) gives the true mass, and with the transit radius, the planet's density — the key clue to whether it is rocky, watery or gaseous.

### Worked Example 9: The signals of a hot Jupiter

A planet of one Jupiter mass ($1.898\times10^{27}$ kg) and 1.2 Jupiter radii (radius of Jupiter $6.991\times10^{7}$ m) orbits a Sun-like star every 4.0 days, edge-on. Find its orbital distance, the star's radial-velocity amplitude, the transit depth and the approximate transit duration.

1. Period in seconds: $P = 4.0 \times 86\,400 = 3.456\times10^{5}$ s.
2. Kepler's third law: $a = \left[G(M_\odot + M_J)P^2/4\pi^2\right]^{1/3} = 7.38\times10^{9}$ m $= 0.049$ AU — about one-eighth of Mercury's distance from the Sun.
3. Radial-velocity amplitude with $\sin i = 1$: $K = (2\pi G/P)^{1/3}\,M_J/(M_\odot+M_J)^{2/3} \approx 128$ m/s, about ten times Jupiter's effect on the Sun and easily measurable with modern spectrographs.
4. Transit depth: $(1.2 \times 6.991\times10^{7}/6.957\times10^{8})^2 = 0.0145$, i.e. a 1.45% dip.
5. Transit duration for a central crossing: the planet's orbital speed is $2\pi a/P = 134$ km/s, and it must travel about $2(R_* + R_p) = 1.56\times10^{9}$ m across the star, taking about $1.16\times10^{4}$ s $\approx 3.2$ hours.

**Answer:** $a \approx 0.049$ AU, $K \approx 128$ m/s, a transit depth of about 1.5% lasting about 3 hours every 4 days. Large, frequent and deep signals like these are why hot Jupiters were the first planets found around Sun-like stars even though they are intrinsically rare — an example of a **selection effect**.

### Findings
- Planetary systems are common: most stars have planets.
- **Diversity:** hot Jupiters, **super-Earths** and **mini-Neptunes** (the most common type, with no Solar System analog), lava worlds, water worlds, and planets orbiting two stars (circumbinary, like "Tatooine" — Kepler-16b).
- **Proxima Centauri b** (2016): an Earth-mass planet in the habitable zone of the nearest star (4.24 light-years), though its red dwarf host emits strong flares.
- **TRAPPIST-1** (2017): seven roughly Earth-sized planets orbiting a cool red dwarf ~40 light-years away, three or four in the habitable zone.
- Estimates suggest roughly one in five Sun-like stars may host an Earth-sized planet in the habitable zone (with large uncertainties) — implying billions of potentially habitable worlds in the Milky Way.

## 12. Applications

- **Spacecraft navigation.** Every interplanetary mission is planned with Kepler's laws, the vis-viva equation and Hohmann-type transfers (Worked Example 3). **Gravity assists** — flying close behind a moving planet to gain speed relative to the Sun — made the Voyager tour of all four giant planets possible; it exploited an alignment of the outer planets that occurs only about once every 175 years.
- **Space weather forecasting.** Monitoring the Sun from spacecraft such as SOHO and from ground observatories gives hours to days of warning of coronal mass ejections. Grid operators can then reconfigure transformers, satellite operators can put spacecraft into safe modes, and airlines can reroute polar flights to reduce radiation exposure for crews and passengers and to avoid radio blackouts.
- **Planetary defense.** Sky surveys catalog near-Earth asteroids and compute their future orbits; the DART test showed that a kinetic impactor can change an asteroid's orbit, provided the threat is found years in advance.
- **Tidal energy and navigation.** Tide prediction, based on the lunar and solar tidal periods, is essential for shipping and coastal engineering. Tidal power stations such as La Rance in France (240 MW, operating since 1966) and Sihwa Lake in South Korea (254 MW, 2011) convert tidal flows into electricity — ultimately drawing on Earth's rotational energy.
- **Timekeeping and calendars.** The day, month and year are astronomical; leap years correct for the extra quarter day in the orbital period, and leap seconds have been inserted because tidal braking and other effects make Earth's rotation slightly irregular compared with atomic clocks.
- **Climate science.** Comparative planetology — Venus's runaway greenhouse, Mars's dust storms and lost atmosphere — tests the climate models used for Earth, and Milankovitch theory explains the timing of past ice ages.
- **Public health from planetary science.** Clair Patterson's precise lead measurements, developed to date meteorites, revealed how much industrial lead (from leaded gasoline) contaminated the environment; his advocacy contributed to the phasing out of leaded fuel, a major public-health gain.
- **Materials and archaeology.** Before iron smelting, people worked meteoritic iron; analyses published in 2016 showed that an iron dagger buried with Tutankhamun has the nickel content characteristic of meteorites.
- **Solar energy engineering.** The solar constant and the geometry of sunlight (Section 8.1) determine how solar panels are tilted and how much energy they deliver at a given latitude and season.

## 13. Connections to Other Fields

- **Physics:** Newtonian mechanics and conservation laws (orbits, tides, angular momentum of the forming disk); thermodynamics and blackbody radiation (equilibrium temperatures, the greenhouse effect); kinetic theory (atmospheric escape); electromagnetism (planetary dynamos, auroras, the solar wind); nuclear physics (solar fusion, radiometric dating); fluid dynamics (zonal jets, the Great Red Spot, convection in the Sun and planetary interiors).
- **Chemistry:** the condensation sequence of minerals and ices in the solar nebula; isotope geochemistry (oxygen isotopes for the Moon's origin, D/H ratios for the origin of water); atmospheric photochemistry (Titan's organic haze, ozone on Earth); spectroscopy for identifying molecules in planetary and exoplanet atmospheres.
- **Geology and Earth science:** plate tectonics, volcanism and impact cratering seen in comparative context; crater counting for surface ages; seismology applied to the Moon (Apollo seismometers) and Mars (InSight, 2018–2022).
- **Biology:** astrobiology and the search for life in subsurface oceans; extremophiles on Earth (organisms living near hydrothermal vents, in acidic springs or in Antarctic ice) as analogs for possible life elsewhere; tidal rhythms that govern intertidal organisms and the spawning of some fish and corals; the dependence of photosynthesis and climate on the solar constant.
- **Mathematics:** conic sections (studied by Apollonius of Perga around 200 BCE, nearly two thousand years before Kepler needed them); calculus, which Newton developed partly to solve orbital problems; the three-body problem, whose study by Henri Poincaré in the 1880s–1890s revealed chaotic dynamics; Lagrange points (Joseph-Louis Lagrange, 1772), now used for spacecraft such as JWST at the Sun–Earth L2 point; resonances and continued fractions in celestial mechanics.
- **Other astronomy topics:** the Sun is a typical main-sequence star (stellar evolution); exoplanet demographics connect to galactic structure and the chemical enrichment of the Milky Way by earlier generations of stars.

## 14. Common Misconceptions

- **"Seasons happen because Earth is closer to the Sun in summer."** Earth is closest to the Sun in early January, during northern winter, and the two hemispheres have opposite seasons at the same time — impossible if distance were the cause. The distance effect changes sunlight by only about 7%, while tilt changes daily insolation at mid-latitudes by a factor of about three (Worked Example 6).
- **"The phases of the Moon are caused by Earth's shadow."** Earth's shadow falls on the Moon only during lunar eclipses, which happen at full moon. Phases come from viewing the half-lit Moon from different angles as it orbits.
- **"The far side of the Moon is permanently dark."** It is the side we never see, not the side never lit. At new moon, when the near side faces away from the Sun, the far side is in full sunlight.
- **"Tides happen because the Moon pulls the water toward it."** If that were all, there would be one high tide per day. Tides come from the difference in the Moon's pull across Earth, which produces two bulges. And although the Sun pulls on Earth about 180 times more strongly than the Moon, its tidal effect is less than half the Moon's, because tides depend on $1/d^3$ (Worked Example 5).
- **"Astronauts on the Space Station float because there is no gravity in space."** At the station's altitude of about 400 km, gravity is still about 90% of its surface value. Astronauts feel weightless because they and the station are falling together around Earth in orbit.
- **"Mercury, being closest to the Sun, is the hottest planet."** Venus is hotter, everywhere and at all times, because of its thick CO₂ greenhouse atmosphere. Mercury's night side falls to about −180 °C because it has no atmosphere to retain heat.
- **"The asteroid belt is a crowded field of tumbling rocks."** Asteroids are typically separated by about a million kilometers; spacecraft pass through without danger.
- **"A comet's tail streams out behind it, like smoke behind a train."** The tails point away from the Sun, pushed by the solar wind and sunlight. When a comet moves away from the Sun after perihelion, its tail actually leads it.
- **"Pluto was demoted because it is too small."** Size alone was not the criterion; Pluto does not dominate its orbital zone, which it shares with many other Kuiper belt objects. Its status changed through the 2006 definition of "planet", not through any change in Pluto.
- **"Jupiter is a failed star."** Sustained hydrogen fusion requires roughly 80 times Jupiter's mass, and even deuterium fusion requires about 13 Jupiter masses. Jupiter is a planet; its excess heat comes from slow contraction (and possibly the settling of helium in its interior), not from fusion.
- **"The Sun is burning like a fire."** Combustion is a chemical reaction needing oxygen; the Sun shines by nuclear fusion, which releases roughly ten million times more energy per kilogram of fuel than burning gasoline. (Its light is also essentially white; it looks yellow near the horizon because the atmosphere scatters away blue light.)
- **"Meteorites arrive red-hot."** Only a thin outer layer melts during the few seconds of bright flight, and it is stripped away as it forms. The interior keeps the cold of space, and freshly fallen small meteorites are often only warm or even cool to the touch.
- **"Saturn would float in water, so its rings and atmosphere would too."** The bathtub image only illustrates that Saturn's *average* density is below 1 g/cm³. Its interior is compressed to much higher densities at depth, and no real planet could be placed in water.

### Solutions

**1.** In the protoplanetary disk, temperature decreased with distance from the young Sun. Inside the frost line (about 3 AU), it was too warm for water, ammonia and methane to freeze, so only metals and silicate rock — a small fraction of the disk's mass — could form solids; the resulting planets were small and rocky. Beyond the frost line, ices were abundant and several times more plentiful than rock, so solid cores grew to about 10 Earth masses, massive enough to capture the surrounding hydrogen and helium gas before the young Sun's wind cleared it. The dividing quantity is **temperature**, specifically whether it lies below the condensation temperature of water ice (~150–170 K in the disk).

**2.**
1. Kepler's third law in solar units: $P = a^{3/2}$.
2. $P = 44^{3/2} = 44 \times \sqrt{44} = 44 \times 6.633 = 292$ years.

**Answer:** about 292 years.

**3.**
1. Light takes 499.0 s to travel 1 AU.
2. One-way time: $29.07 \times 499.0$ s $= 1.45\times10^{4}$ s $= 4.03$ hours.
3. A response returns over the same distance, so the round trip takes about 8.06 hours.

**Answer:** about 4.0 hours one way; about 8.1 hours before any response is seen. This is why spacecraft at Neptune must be largely autonomous.

**4.** The Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5° to the plane of Earth's orbit (the ecliptic). At most new moons the Moon passes a little above or below the Sun as seen from Earth, and at most full moons it passes above or below Earth's shadow. Eclipses occur only when a new or full moon happens close to one of the two points (nodes) where the Moon's orbit crosses the ecliptic. The Sun lines up with the nodes about twice a year, creating **eclipse seasons**, so there are at least two (and at most five) solar eclipses each year somewhere on Earth.

**5.**
1. Surface gravity scales as $g \propto M/R^2$.
2. $g_{\text{Mars}} = 9.81 \times 0.107/0.532^2 = 9.81 \times 0.378 = 3.71$ m/s².
3. Rover's weight on Mars: $899 \times 3.71 = 3.34\times10^{3}$ N.
4. On Earth: $899 \times 9.81 = 8.82\times10^{3}$ N.

**Answer:** $g_{\text{Mars}} \approx 3.7$ m/s², about 38% of Earth's; the rover weighs about 3.3 kN on Mars versus 8.8 kN on Earth. Its *mass* is the same on both worlds.

**6.**
1. $T_{\text{eq}} = 278.3\ \text{K} \times (1 - 0.34)^{1/4}/\sqrt{5.20}$, where 278.3 K is the zero-albedo value at 1 AU.
2. $(0.66)^{1/4} = 0.901$ and $\sqrt{5.20} = 2.28$, so $T_{\text{eq}} = 278.3 \times 0.901/2.28 \approx 110$ K.
3. Emitted power scales as $T^4$; absorbed power equals the emission of a body at $T_{\text{eq}}$. Ratio: $(125/110)^4 \approx 1.67$.

**Answer:** $T_{\text{eq}} \approx 110$ K, and Jupiter radiates about 1.6–1.7 times as much energy as it absorbs. It must have an internal heat source — primordial heat from its formation, released as the planet slowly contracts, possibly supplemented by energy from helium droplets sinking through its hydrogen interior.

1. $a = 3.844\times10^{8}$ m; $P = 27.32 \times 86\,400 = 2.361\times10^{6}$ s.

**Answer:** about $6.03\times10^{24}$ kg, within 0.3% of the accepted $6.05\times10^{24}$ kg. Note that the method gives the *sum* of the masses; separating them requires another measurement, such as the wobble of Earth about the Earth–Moon center of mass.

1. Transit depth $= (R_p/R_*)^2 = \left(\dfrac{2 \times 6371\ \text{km}}{0.5 \times 695\,700\ \text{km}}\right)^2$.
2. $= (12\,742/347\,850)^2 = (0.0366)^2 = 1.34\times10^{-3}$.
3. Earth's transit of the Sun: $8.4\times10^{-5}$. Ratio: $1.34\times10^{-3}/8.4\times10^{-5} = 16$, which equals $2^2 \times 2^2$ (doubling the planet radius and halving the star radius each multiply the depth by 4).

**Answer:** a dip of about 0.13% (1340 ppm), 16 times deeper than Earth's transit of the Sun — one reason small stars are favored targets for finding small planets.

**9.** Venus's surface temperature is not set by the sunlight it receives but by how efficiently that energy can escape. Its 92-bar CO₂ atmosphere, together with sulfuric acid clouds and traces of water vapor and sulfur dioxide, is nearly opaque to infrared radiation, so the surface must become extremely hot (~738 K) before the energy it radiates can work its way out to space. Thick air and fast winds also spread the heat evenly, so night and day, poles and equator are all about equally hot. Mercury has no atmosphere: its day side reaches ~700 K under the direct Sun but its night side cools to ~90 K. The comparison shows the dominant role of the greenhouse effect; in fact, because its clouds reflect most sunlight, Venus absorbs *less* solar energy per square meter of surface than Earth does.

**10.**
1. (a) Habitable-zone distances scale as $\sqrt{L/L_\odot} = \sqrt{0.04} = 0.20$. Inner edge: $0.95 \times 0.20 = 0.19$ AU; outer edge: $1.4 \times 0.20 = 0.28$ AU.
2. (b) Kepler's third law with $M = 0.5\,M_\odot$: $P = \sqrt{a^3/M} = \sqrt{0.20^3/0.5} = \sqrt{0.016} = 0.126$ yr $\approx 46$ days.
3. (c) A planet this close to its star is likely to be tidally locked, with a permanent day side and night side (heat transport by an atmosphere may moderate this). Red dwarfs also produce frequent, powerful flares and strong ultraviolet and X-ray emission, especially when young, which can erode atmospheres and damage surface chemistry. In addition, such stars are very luminous during their long pre-main-sequence contraction, so planets now in the habitable zone may have lost water early on.

**Answer:** about 0.19–0.28 AU; a period of about 46 days; tidal locking and stellar activity are the main concerns.

**11.**
1. For retrograde rotation, the planet's spin and orbital motion combine, so the Sun's apparent motion across the sky is faster: $1/T_{\text{solar}} = 1/T_{\text{rot}} + 1/P_{\text{orb}}$.
2. $1/T_{\text{solar}} = 1/243.0 + 1/224.7 = 0.004\,115 + 0.004\,450 = 0.008\,565$ per day.
3. $T_{\text{solar}} = 116.8$ days.

**Answer:** a solar day on Venus lasts about 117 Earth days, so the Sun rises in the west and sets in the east about twice per Venusian year.

**12.**
1. (a) $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2GM/R} = \sqrt{2 \times 6.674\times10^{-11} \times 9.38\times10^{20}/4.70\times10^{5}}$ m/s $= 516$ m/s, about 0.52 km/s.
2. (b) With $M = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \rho$: $v_{\text{esc}} = \sqrt{2G \cdot \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3\rho / r} = \sqrt{\tfrac{8}{3}\pi G \rho\, r^2} = r\sqrt{8\pi G\rho/3}$.
3. For $r = 1000$ m and $\rho = 2000$ kg/m³: $\sqrt{8\pi \times 6.674\times10^{-11} \times 2000/3} = 1.06\times10^{-3}$ s⁻¹, so $v_{\text{esc}} = 1000 \times 1.06\times10^{-3} = 1.06$ m/s.
4. (c) A jump at 3 m/s is far below Ceres's 516 m/s, but nearly three times the small asteroid's escape speed.

**Answer:** about 0.52 km/s for Ceres and about 1.1 m/s for the small asteroid; an astronaut could easily jump off the small asteroid into space, but not off Ceres. (This is why landers on small asteroids, such as Philae on comet 67P, have had trouble staying on the surface.) Note that escape speed for a body of fixed density is proportional to its radius.
