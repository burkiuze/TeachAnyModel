# Galaxies and Cosmology: The Structure, Origin and Fate of the Universe

**Cosmology** studies the universe as a whole — its structure, origin, evolution and ultimate fate. In just one century, we went from not knowing whether other galaxies existed to measuring the age of the universe to within about 1%. The modern picture — the hot **Big Bang** model with **dark matter**, **dark energy** and **inflation** — is supported by multiple independent lines of evidence, yet leaves profound questions open: we do not know what 95% of the universe is made of.

This chapter moves outward in scale: from our own galaxy, to galaxies in general, to the evidence for dark matter, and then to the universe as a whole — its expansion, its hot beginning, and the equations that govern its history. Along the way, key results are derived step by step and applied in worked examples, so that each number quoted can be traced back to physics you can check yourself.

## 1. Historical Development

### 1.1 From the Milky Way to "island universes" (1610–1925)

Galileo turned his telescope on the Milky Way in 1610 and found that its glow is the combined light of countless faint stars. In 1750 Thomas Wright suggested that the Milky Way is a flattened system of stars seen from inside, and in 1755 Immanuel Kant went further: the faint elliptical "nebulae" might be other such systems, "island universes" at enormous distances. William Herschel counted stars in different directions (1785) and drew the Milky Way as a flattened disk with the Sun near its middle, and in 1845 William Parsons (Lord Rosse) used his giant 72-inch reflector to see spiral structure in the nebula M51.

Measuring distances was the obstacle. The breakthrough came from **Henrietta Swan Leavitt** at Harvard, who studied variable stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud. Because all of them are at nearly the same distance, she could compare their apparent brightnesses directly, and in 1912 she published the **period–luminosity relation** of Cepheid variables: the longer the pulsation period, the more luminous the star. Once calibrated, a Cepheid's period reveals its luminosity, and comparing luminosity with apparent brightness gives its distance. Meanwhile **Vesto Slipher** (from 1912) measured the spectra of spiral nebulae and found that most showed large redshifts. Whether the spiral nebulae were small clouds inside the Milky Way or distant galaxies remained unresolved — it was the subject of the 1920 "Great Debate" between Harlow Shapley and Heber Curtis — until **Edwin Hubble** found Cepheids in the Andromeda Nebula in 1923–1924, placing it far outside the Milky Way.

### 1.2 The expanding universe and the Big Bang (1915–1965)

Einstein's general relativity (1915) made it possible to treat the universe as a whole as a physical system. In 1917 Einstein built a static model by adding a **cosmological constant**, while Willem de Sitter found a model with no matter in which test particles fly apart. **Alexander Friedmann** (1922, 1924) showed that the equations naturally describe expanding or contracting universes, and **Georges Lemaître** (1927) independently derived the expansion, connected it to the observed redshifts and estimated the expansion rate. Hubble's 1929 data on 24 galaxies with known distances showed the linear velocity–distance relation, though his value of the constant (about 500 km/s/Mpc) was far too large because his distance scale was wrong. Walter Baade showed in 1952 that there are two types of Cepheid, which roughly doubled extragalactic distances, and by 1958 Allan Sandage had brought the Hubble constant down to about 75 km/s/Mpc.

In the late 1940s George Gamow, Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman worked out the consequences of a hot, dense beginning, predicting both light-element production and a relic radiation of a few kelvin. The rival **Steady State** theory (Hermann Bondi, Thomas Gold and Fred Hoyle, 1948) held that the universe is unchanging on large scales, with matter continuously created as it expands. The discovery of quasars (1963), which are common at large distances but absent nearby, and above all the cosmic microwave background (1965), showed that the universe has changed over time and decided the contest in favor of the Big Bang.

## 2. Units, Constants and the Cosmic Distance Ladder

### 2.1 Units used in this chapter

Astronomical distances are measured in **light-years** (ly) and **parsecs** (pc). One parsec is the distance at which 1 AU subtends an angle of one arcsecond. Galaxies are typically kiloparsecs (kpc) across, and intergalactic distances are measured in megaparsecs (Mpc).

| Light-year | $9.461\times10^{15}$ m |
| Parsec | $3.086\times10^{16}$ m $= 3.262$ ly $= 206\,265$ AU |
| Megaparsec | $3.086\times10^{22}$ m $= 3.262$ million ly |
| Solar mass $M_\odot$ | $1.989\times10^{30}$ kg |
| Solar luminosity $L_\odot$ | $3.828\times10^{26}$ W |

| One billion years (Gyr) | $3.156\times10^{16}$ s |
| $H_0 = 67.4$ km/s/Mpc | $2.184\times10^{-18}$ s⁻¹ |

### 2.2 Standard candles and the distance modulus

Most extragalactic distances come from **standard candles** — objects of known luminosity $L$. Light spreads over a sphere, so the measured flux is $F = L/(4\pi d^2)$. Astronomers express brightness on the logarithmic magnitude scale, where a factor of 100 in flux corresponds to 5 magnitudes: $m_1 - m_2 = -2.5\log_{10}(F_1/F_2)$. The **absolute magnitude** $M$ is defined as the apparent magnitude a source would have at 10 pc. Comparing the same source at its true distance $d$ and at 10 pc:
$$m - M = -2.5\log_{10}\frac{F(d)}{F(10\ \text{pc})} = -2.5\log_{10}\left(\frac{10\ \text{pc}}{d}\right)^2 = 5\log_{10}\frac{d}{10\ \text{pc}}$$
The quantity $\mu = m - M$ is the **distance modulus**; inverting gives $d = 10^{(\mu + 5)/5}$ pc. Interstellar dust dims light, so in practice an extinction term is subtracted from $m$ first.

### 2.3 The rungs of the ladder

No single method works at all distances, so each rung is calibrated using the one below it:

| Rung | Principle | Approximate reach |
|---|---|---|
| Radar ranging | Echo time of radio pulses | Solar System (fixes the AU) |
| Trigonometric parallax | Geometry: $d$ (pc) $= 1/p$ (arcsec); Gaia satellite (launched 2013) | A few kpc |
| Eclipsing binaries, main-sequence fitting | Physical sizes and colors of stars | Magellanic Clouds, nearby galaxies |
| Cepheid variables | Period–luminosity relation | ~40 Mpc |
| Tip of the red giant branch | The brightest red giants have a nearly fixed luminosity (helium ignition) | ~20 Mpc, farther with JWST |
| Tully–Fisher relation | Spiral luminosity correlates with rotation speed | Hundreds of Mpc |
| Type Ia supernovae | Standardizable candles, peak $M \approx -19.3$ | Several Gpc |
| Hubble's law | $d = v/H_0$ | Beyond ~100 Mpc, where peculiar velocities are small by comparison |
| Gravitational-wave "standard sirens" | The waveform gives the luminosity distance directly | Hundreds of Mpc so far |

Errors propagate upward: a 2% error in the Cepheid calibration becomes a 2% error in every supernova distance, which is why the calibration of the lower rungs lies at the heart of the Hubble tension (Section 6.5).

### Worked Example 1: The distance to a galaxy from a Cepheid

The Hubble Space Telescope finds a Cepheid in a distant spiral galaxy with a period of 30.0 days and a mean apparent visual magnitude of $m_V = 25.0$. Use the approximate calibration $M_V = -4.05 - 2.43(\log_{10}P - 1)$, with $P$ in days, and ignore dust.

1. $\log_{10} 30.0 = 1.477$, so $\log_{10}P - 1 = 0.477$.
2. Absolute magnitude: $M_V = -4.05 - 2.43 \times 0.477 = -4.05 - 1.16 = -5.21$.
3. Distance modulus: $\mu = m_V - M_V = 25.0 - (-5.21) = 30.21$.
4. Distance: $d = 10^{(30.21 + 5)/5}$ pc $= 10^{7.042}$ pc $= 1.10\times10^{7}$ pc.
5. Converting: $11.0$ Mpc $\times 3.262$ million ly/Mpc $\approx 36$ million ly.

**Answer:** about 11 Mpc (36 million light-years). The Cepheid's luminosity is $10^{0.4(4.83 + 5.21)} \approx 10^{4}$ times the Sun's in visual light ($M_{V,\odot} = 4.83$), which is why single Cepheids are visible in other galaxies. Dust dims the star, so ignoring it makes the galaxy seem farther away than it is: this estimate is an upper limit, and correcting for extinction reduces it.

## 3. The Milky Way

Our galaxy is a **barred spiral galaxy**, ~100 000 light-years (~30 kpc) across, containing ~100–400 billion stars, plus gas, dust and dark matter. Its stars total roughly $6\times10^{10}$ solar masses, while its total mass, dominated by dark matter, is estimated at roughly $10^{12}$ solar masses (estimates differ by up to a factor of about two).

**Structure:**
- **Disk:** thin (~1000 ly thick), containing young stars, gas and dust in **spiral arms**, rotating around the center. The Sun lies ~26 700 light-years (~8.2 kpc) from the center, in a minor arm (the Orion Spur), and orbits at ~230 km/s, completing one **galactic year** in ~220–250 million years.
- **Bulge and bar:** a central concentration of older stars.
- **Halo:** a roughly spherical region containing old stars, ~150–160 **globular clusters**, and most of the galaxy's mass in the form of **dark matter** (extending far beyond the visible disk).
- **Galactic center:** **Sagittarius A***, a supermassive black hole of ~4.3 million solar masses, established by decades of tracking stars (such as S2, with a 16-year orbit) at close range (Genzel and Ghez, Nobel 2020) and imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope (2022).

**Spiral arms are patterns, not fixed structures.** Stars near the center complete an orbit faster than stars farther out (differential rotation). If the arms were made of the same stars forever, they would wind up tightly within a few rotations — the **winding problem**. The **density-wave theory** (C. C. Lin and Frank Shu, 1964) explains arms as slowly rotating compression waves: stars and gas pass through them, like cars through a traffic jam. Gas compressed in an arm collapses into new stars; the brightest, short-lived blue stars die before leaving the arm, which is why arms look blue and are lined with emission nebulae.

**History of discovery:** Galileo resolved the Milky Way's band into stars (1610). William Herschel mapped it as a flattened disk (1785) with the Sun near the center (wrong, due to dust obscuration). **Harlow Shapley** (1918) used globular clusters to show the Sun is far from the center. The **"Great Debate"** (Shapley vs. Heber Curtis, 1920) concerned whether spiral nebulae were nearby clouds or distant "island universes". **Edwin Hubble** settled it in 1924 by finding Cepheid variables in the Andromeda Nebula, showing it lay far beyond the Milky Way — the universe contains countless galaxies.

The Milky Way has many satellite galaxies (the Large Magellanic Cloud at about 160 000 ly, the Small Magellanic Cloud at about 200 000 ly, the Sagittarius Dwarf, and dozens of faint dwarfs). It has grown by merging with smaller galaxies — Gaia revealed the remnants of the **Gaia–Enceladus/Sausage** merger ~8–11 billion years ago.

### Worked Example 2: Weighing the black hole at the Galactic Center

The star S2 orbits Sagittarius A* with a period of 16.05 years on an ellipse with eccentricity 0.884 and an angular semi-major axis of 0.1255 arcsec. The distance to the Galactic Center is 8.18 kpc. Find the black hole's mass, the star's closest approach, and compare it with the black hole's Schwarzschild radius.

1. Convert the angle to a length. By the definition of the parsec, 1 arcsec at 1 pc corresponds to 1 AU, so $a = 0.1255 \times 8178$ AU $= 1026$ AU.
2. Kepler's third law in solar units ($a$ in AU, $P$ in years, $M$ in solar masses, with the star's mass negligible): $M = a^3/P^2$.
3. $M = 1026^3/16.05^2 = 1.080\times10^{9}/257.6 = 4.20\times10^{6}\ M_\odot$.
4. Pericenter distance: $r_p = a(1 - e) = 1026 \times 0.116 = 119$ AU, about 16.5 light-hours — only about four times the Sun–Neptune distance.
5. Schwarzschild radius for $4.3\times10^{6}\ M_\odot$: $R_s = 2GM/c^2 = 1.27\times10^{10}$ m $= 0.085$ AU, about 18 solar radii.
6. Speed at pericenter from the vis-viva equation, $v^2 = GM(2/r_p - 1/a)$: $v \approx 7.7\times10^{3}$ km/s, about 2.6% of the speed of light.

**Answer:** about $4.2\times10^{6}\ M_\odot$, consistent with the accepted $4.3\times10^{6}\ M_\odot$ (the small difference comes from the adopted distance, since $M \propto d^3$). S2 passes within 119 AU, about 1400 Schwarzschild radii, so four million solar masses are packed into a region only a few times the size of Neptune's orbit. No cluster of stars or stellar remnants could be that compact and stable; a black hole is the only known explanation. Its predicted shadow spans roughly 50 microarcseconds, consistent with the ring of about 52 microarcseconds imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope.

## 4. Galaxies

There are perhaps **~200 billion to 2 trillion galaxies** in the observable universe (estimates depend on how many faint dwarfs are counted).

### Hubble's classification (1926; the "tuning fork" diagram, 1936)
- **Elliptical galaxies (E0–E7):** smooth, featureless, ellipsoidal; old red stars; little gas or ongoing star formation; range from dwarf ellipticals to giant ellipticals (the largest galaxies, e.g. M87 at the center of the Virgo Cluster) often formed through mergers. The number after E is $10(1 - b/a)$, where $b/a$ is the apparent axis ratio, so E0 looks round and E7 is the most flattened.
- **Spiral galaxies (Sa–Sd) and barred spirals (SBa–SBd):** a disk with spiral arms, a central bulge, ongoing star formation in arms (blue young stars, emission nebulae), gas and dust. Sa have tightly wound arms and large bulges; Sd have loose arms and small bulges. ~2/3 of spirals have bars, including the Milky Way.
- **Lenticular (S0):** disk without spiral arms; intermediate.
- **Irregular galaxies:** no regular shape (Magellanic Clouds), often gas-rich and star-forming, sometimes disturbed by interactions.
(Hubble initially thought the sequence was evolutionary; it is not, although galaxies do evolve through mergers and gas consumption. His terms "early type" for ellipticals and "late type" for spirals survive, but they say nothing about age.)

| Type | Structure | Stellar population and color | Cold gas and dust | Dominant motions |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Elliptical | Smooth ellipsoid, no disk | Old, red | Very little (giants contain hot X-ray gas) | Random orbits in all directions |
| Lenticular (S0) | Disk and bulge, no arms | Mostly old | Little | Rotation plus random motions |
| Spiral and barred spiral | Disk, bulge, arms, often a bar | Old bulge, young blue arms | Abundant in the disk | Ordered rotation of the disk |
| Irregular | No symmetry | Many young stars | Gas-rich | Disordered, often weak rotation |

The table reflects a deep physical division: disks form when gas cools and settles while conserving angular momentum, so they rotate in an orderly way; ellipticals are dominated by random stellar orbits, as expected when mergers scramble disks. Galaxy surveys show a "red sequence" of mostly elliptical galaxies that have stopped forming stars and a "blue cloud" of star-forming spirals and irregulars.

### Galaxy interactions and mergers
Galaxies collide frequently on cosmic timescales. Stars rarely hit each other (vast distances between them), but gas clouds collide and trigger **starbursts**, tidal forces create tails and bridges (the Antennae Galaxies, the Mice), and mergers transform spirals into ellipticals. The **Andromeda Galaxy** (M31, 2.5 million light-years away, slightly larger than the Milky Way with ~1 trillion stars) is approaching at ~110 km/s; the two will likely merge in ~4–5 billion years (recent analyses suggest the probability is around 50% within 10 billion years), forming a giant elliptical sometimes nicknamed "Milkomeda".

Why stars almost never collide: in the solar neighborhood, the typical distance between stars is a parsec or so, some 20–30 million times the Sun's diameter. A star crossing a whole galaxy has a negligible chance of striking another — gravity, not contact, does the work in a galaxy merger.

### Groups, clusters and superclusters
- **Local Group:** the Milky Way, Andromeda, Triangulum (M33, about 2.7–3.0 million ly away) and ~80+ dwarf galaxies, spanning ~10 million light-years.
- **Galaxy clusters:** hundreds to thousands of galaxies bound by gravity (Virgo Cluster ~54 million ly away; Coma Cluster). Most of a cluster's ordinary matter is hot (10⁷–10⁸ K) intracluster gas emitting X-rays. The same gas scatters CMB photons and leaves a small distortion in the microwave sky (the **Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect**), which lets surveys find clusters at great distances.
- **Superclusters:** the Local Group belongs to the Virgo Supercluster, part of the larger **Laniakea Supercluster** (~520 million ly across, ~100 000 galaxies; defined in 2014 from galaxy motions).
- **Large-scale structure:** on the largest scales, galaxies form a **cosmic web** of filaments and walls surrounding enormous **voids** (up to hundreds of millions of light-years across), mapped by redshift surveys (CfA, 2dF, SDSS, DESI). Above ~300 million light-years, the universe looks statistically homogeneous and isotropic (the **cosmological principle**).

### Active galactic nuclei and quasars
Some galaxies have extraordinarily luminous cores powered by gas accreting onto supermassive black holes:
- **Quasars** (quasi-stellar radio sources): discovered in 1963 (Maarten Schmidt measured 3C 273's redshift, z = 0.158, implying enormous luminosity — ~4 trillion Suns — from a region the size of the Solar System). The most luminous objects in the universe, mostly seen at great distances (in the early universe). Some are powered by black holes of billions of solar masses.
- **Seyfert galaxies, radio galaxies, blazars** (jets pointed at us) are related types, unified by viewing angle and accretion rate.
- **Relativistic jets** carry energy far beyond the host galaxy: M87's famous optical jet is about 5000 light-years long, and the lobes of the largest radio galaxies span more than 10 million light-years.
- **Black hole–galaxy coevolution:** supermassive black hole masses correlate with their host bulges' masses (~0.1–0.5%), suggesting feedback from accretion regulates star formation.

**Why accretion is so powerful.** Matter spiraling into a black hole through an accretion disk heats up and radiates a fraction $\varepsilon$ of its rest-mass energy, $L = \varepsilon\,\dot m c^2$, with $\varepsilon \approx 0.06$–0.4 depending on the black hole's spin (a value of 0.1 is typical). Nuclear fusion of hydrogen releases only 0.7% of the rest mass, so accretion is roughly ten times more efficient than the process that powers stars.

**The Eddington limit.** Radiation pushes outward on the infalling gas. The force acts mainly on free electrons, through Thomson scattering, while gravity acts mainly on protons (the electrostatic attraction keeps them together). At distance $r$, the radiation force on one electron is the momentum flux $L/(4\pi r^2 c)$ times $\sigma_T$; gravity on one proton is $GMm_p/r^2$. Setting them equal, the distance cancels:
$$\frac{L\sigma_T}{4\pi r^2 c} = \frac{GMm_p}{r^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad L_{\text{Edd}} = \frac{4\pi G M m_p c}{\sigma_T} \approx 1.26\times10^{31}\ \text{W}\times\frac{M}{M_\odot}$$
A steadily accreting object cannot shine much brighter than this, so a measured luminosity sets a **minimum mass** for the black hole.

### Worked Example 3: How massive is the engine of a quasar?

The quasar 3C 273 has a luminosity of about $4\times10^{12}\ L_\odot$. Find the minimum black hole mass and the accretion rate needed, assuming a radiative efficiency of 10%.

1. Luminosity in watts: $L = 4\times10^{12} \times 3.828\times10^{26}$ W $= 1.53\times10^{39}$ W.
2. Eddington luminosity per solar mass: $1.26\times10^{31}$ W, about $3.3\times10^{4}\ L_\odot$.
3. Minimum mass: $M \ge 1.53\times10^{39}/1.26\times10^{31}\ M_\odot = 1.2\times10^{8}\ M_\odot$.
4. Accretion rate: $\dot m = L/(\varepsilon c^2) = 1.53\times10^{39}/(0.1 \times 8.99\times10^{16})$ kg/s $= 1.7\times10^{23}$ kg/s.
5. Per year: $1.7\times10^{23} \times 3.156\times10^{7}/1.989\times10^{30} \approx 2.7\ M_\odot$ per year.

**Answer:** the black hole must have at least about $10^{8}$ solar masses and swallow roughly three solar masses of gas per year. Its brightness varies over months, so the emitting region can be at most light-months across — tiny compared with a galaxy 100 000 light-years wide. Packing that much power into so small a region is what forced astronomers to accept accreting black holes as the energy source after Schmidt's 1963 redshift.

## 5. Dark Matter

### Evidence
1. **Galaxy rotation curves:** if most mass were in the visible stars and gas, orbital speeds would decline with distance from the center (Keplerian falloff, $v \propto r^{-1/2}$). Instead, rotation curves stay **flat** far beyond the visible disk — **Vera Rubin** and Kent Ford's optical measurements in the 1970s, together with radio observations of the 21-cm line of neutral hydrogen that reach far beyond the stars, showed galaxies must be embedded in massive, invisible halos. (Horace Babcock had already found unexpectedly high outer speeds in Andromeda in 1939.)
2. **Galaxy clusters:** **Fritz Zwicky** (1933) found that galaxies in the Coma Cluster move far too fast to be held together by visible matter — he called the missing mass *dunkle Materie*. X-ray gas temperatures confirm it.
3. **Gravitational lensing:** mass maps of clusters from strong and weak lensing show far more mass than visible matter.
4. **The Bullet Cluster** (1E 0657-56): two colliding clusters where the hot gas (most of the ordinary matter, seen in X-rays) was slowed by the collision, while the lensing mass passed through — separating dark matter from ordinary matter (2006). Hard to explain with modified gravity alone.
5. **Cosmic microwave background** fluctuations require dark matter in specific amounts.
6. **Structure formation:** galaxies and the cosmic web could not have formed by today without dark matter's gravity, since ordinary matter was coupled to radiation until ~380 000 years after the Big Bang.

### 5.1 Derivation: what a flat rotation curve implies

For a star on a circular orbit of radius $r$, gravity supplies the centripetal force. If the mass is distributed with spherical symmetry, only the mass $M(r)$ inside the orbit matters (Newton's shell theorem):
$$\frac{mv^2}{r} = \frac{GM(r)m}{r^2} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad v(r) = \sqrt{\frac{GM(r)}{r}}, \qquad M(r) = \frac{v^2 r}{G}$$
- **Outside most of the mass**, $M(r)$ is constant and $v \propto r^{-1/2}$ — the Keplerian falloff seen in the Solar System (Earth orbits at 29.8 km/s; Neptune, 30 times farther out, at 5.4 km/s, which is $29.8/\sqrt{30}$).
- **A flat curve**, $v = $ constant, requires $M(r) \propto r$: the enclosed mass keeps growing linearly with radius, even where almost no starlight is left.
- The density that produces this follows from $dM/dr = 4\pi r^2 \rho$: with $M = v^2 r/G$, $dM/dr = v^2/G$, so
$$\rho(r) = \frac{v^2}{4\pi G r^2}$$
This $\rho \propto r^{-2}$ profile is called a **singular isothermal sphere**, a useful first model of a dark matter halo. Simulations of cold dark matter predict halos with a similar shape at intermediate radii (steepening further out).

### Worked Example 4: The mass of the Milky Way and the dark matter around us

The Sun orbits at $v = 230$ km/s at $r = 8.2$ kpc. (a) Find the mass inside the Sun's orbit. (b) If the rotation curve stayed flat to 100 kpc, what would the mass be? (c) Estimate the local halo density, and (d) the flux of dark matter particles through a detector on Earth if each particle has a mass of 100 GeV/$c^2$.

1. Convert: $r = 8.2 \times 3.086\times10^{19}$ m $= 2.53\times10^{20}$ m; $v = 2.30\times10^{5}$ m/s.
2. (a) $M = v^2 r/G = (2.30\times10^{5})^2 \times 2.53\times10^{20}/6.674\times10^{-11} = 2.0\times10^{41}$ kg $\approx 1.0\times10^{11}\ M_\odot$.
3. (b) For a flat curve $M \propto r$: $M(100\ \text{kpc}) = 1.0\times10^{11} \times 100/8.2 \approx 1.2\times10^{12}\ M_\odot$ — about twenty times the mass in stars.
4. (c) $\rho = v^2/(4\pi G r^2) = 9.9\times10^{-22}$ kg/m³ $\approx 0.015\ M_\odot$ per cubic parsec. In particle-physics units, multiply by $c^2$ and convert: about 0.55 GeV per cm³. Models that separate the disk from the halo give a local dark matter density of about 0.3–0.4 GeV/cm³, the same order of magnitude.
5. (d) Taking 0.3 GeV/cm³ and 100 GeV particles: $n = 0.3/100 = 3\times10^{-3}$ particles per cm³. They stream past at roughly the Sun's orbital speed, $2.3\times10^{7}$ cm/s, so the flux is $n v \approx 7\times10^{4}$ particles per cm² per second.

**Answer:** (a) $\sim10^{11}\ M_\odot$; (b) $\sim10^{12}\ M_\odot$; (c) $\sim10^{-21}$ kg/m³, or a few tenths of a GeV per cm³; (d) tens of thousands of particles per cm² per second. Even so, because they interact so weakly, a detector with tonnes of liquid xenon would record at most a handful of collisions per year at the interaction strengths still allowed by experiments — which is why experiments such as LUX-ZEPLIN are built deep underground to escape cosmic rays.

### 5.2 Derivation: weighing a cluster with the virial theorem

For a gravitationally bound system in equilibrium, the time-averaged kinetic energy $K$ and potential energy $U$ obey the **virial theorem**, $2K + U = 0$. Astronomers measure only the line-of-sight velocity dispersion $\sigma_r$; if the motions are random and isotropic, the three-dimensional mean square speed is $3\sigma_r^2$, so $K = \tfrac{3}{2}M\sigma_r^2$. For a uniform sphere, $U = -\tfrac{3}{5}GM^2/R$. Then
$$3M\sigma_r^2 = \frac{3}{5}\frac{GM^2}{R} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad M = \frac{5\sigma_r^2 R}{G}$$
Real clusters are centrally concentrated, which changes the numerical factor, but the scaling $M \sim \sigma^2 R/G$ is robust. This is essentially Zwicky's 1933 argument.

### Worked Example 5: The mass of a rich galaxy cluster

A rich cluster like Coma has a line-of-sight velocity dispersion of about 1000 km/s. Model it as a uniform sphere of radius 2.0 Mpc and suppose its galaxies emit a total of $5\times10^{12}\ L_\odot$.

1. $R = 2.0 \times 3.086\times10^{22}$ m $= 6.17\times10^{22}$ m; $\sigma_r = 1.0\times10^{6}$ m/s.
2. $M = 5\sigma_r^2 R/G = 5 \times 10^{12} \times 6.17\times10^{22}/6.674\times10^{-11} = 4.6\times10^{45}$ kg.
3. In solar masses: $4.6\times10^{45}/1.989\times10^{30} = 2.3\times10^{15}\ M_\odot$.
4. Mass-to-light ratio: $2.3\times10^{15}/5\times10^{12} \approx 460\ M_\odot/L_\odot$. Stellar populations have ratios of roughly 1–10.
5. Check equilibrium: the crossing time $R/\sigma_r = 6.17\times10^{22}/10^{6}$ s $\approx 2$ Gyr, so galaxies have crossed the cluster several times in 13.8 Gyr, making the virial assumption reasonable.

**Answer:** about $2\times10^{15}\ M_\odot$, hundreds of times more than the stars can account for. Adding the X-ray-emitting gas (several times the stellar mass) still leaves most of the mass unseen.

### 5.3 Gravitational lensing

General relativity predicts that a mass $M$ deflects a light ray passing at distance $b$ by an angle
$$\alpha = \frac{4GM}{c^2 b}$$
twice the value a naive Newtonian calculation gives. For light grazing the Sun this is 1.75 arcseconds, confirmed during the 1919 solar eclipse expeditions led by Arthur Eddington and Frank Dyson. When a distant source lies exactly behind a lens, its light reaches us around all sides of the lens, forming an **Einstein ring** of angular radius
$$\theta_E = \sqrt{\frac{4GM}{c^2}\,\frac{D_{ls}}{D_l D_s}}$$
where $D_l$, $D_s$ and $D_{ls}$ are the (angular-diameter) distances to the lens, to the source, and from lens to source. Slightly misaligned sources appear as arcs or multiple images. **Strong lensing** (arcs, rings, multiple images) and **weak lensing** (tiny statistical distortions of thousands of background galaxies) measure total mass — dark and ordinary — without any assumption about whether the mass emits light. Lensing within our own galaxy (**microlensing**), searched for by the MACHO and EROS surveys in the 1990s and 2000s, showed that compact dark objects such as faint stars or planets cannot make up most of the Milky Way's halo.

### Worked Example 6: The Einstein ring of a massive galaxy

A galaxy of total mass $10^{12}\ M_\odot$ lies at $D_l = 1.0$ Gpc in front of a background galaxy at $D_s = 2.0$ Gpc, with $D_{ls} = 1.0$ Gpc (illustrative angular-diameter distances). Find the Einstein radius.

1. $4GM/c^2 = 4 \times 1.327\times10^{20} \times 10^{12}/8.988\times10^{16}$ m $= 5.91\times10^{15}$ m (using $GM_\odot = 1.327\times10^{20}$ m³/s²).
2. $D_{ls}/(D_l D_s) = 1/(2.0\ \text{Gpc}) = 1/(6.17\times10^{25}\ \text{m}) = 1.62\times10^{-26}$ m⁻¹.
3. $\theta_E^2 = 5.91\times10^{15} \times 1.62\times10^{-26} = 9.57\times10^{-11}$, so $\theta_E = 9.8\times10^{-6}$ rad.
4. Converting with 206 265 arcsec per radian: $\theta_E \approx 2.0$ arcsec.

**Answer:** about 2 arcseconds — the size of the Einstein rings seen around massive galaxies in Hubble images. Since $\theta_E \propto \sqrt{M}$, a $10^{14}\ M_\odot$ cluster at the same distances gives about 20 arcsec, matching the giant arcs seen in cluster images. Measuring $\theta_E$ and inverting the formula yields the lens mass, and it consistently exceeds the mass in stars.

### What is it?
Dark matter makes up ~**27%** of the universe's energy content (about **85% of all matter**). It does not emit, absorb or reflect light, interacts gravitationally, and is "cold" (slow-moving) — **cold dark matter (CDM)**. It is **not** ordinary (baryonic) matter: Big Bang nucleosynthesis and the CMB limit baryons to ~5% of the total. Candidates:
- **WIMPs** (weakly interacting massive particles) — searched for in deep underground detectors (LUX-ZEPLIN, XENONnT, PandaX), at colliders, and via annihilation signals; no confirmed detection so far, with ever-tighter limits.
- **Axions** (very light particles, also solving the strong CP problem) — searched for by ADMX and others.
- Sterile neutrinos, primordial black holes, and others.
- **Alternatives:** modified gravity theories (MOND, Milgrom 1983) explain galaxy rotation curves well but struggle with clusters, the Bullet Cluster and the CMB. MOND changes Newton's law at accelerations below about $a_0 \approx 1.2\times10^{-10}$ m/s², which predicts that the flat rotation speed depends only on the ordinary mass, $v^4 = GM_b a_0$ — a relation (the baryonic Tully–Fisher relation) that galaxies do follow remarkably well and that dark matter models must also reproduce.

Ordinary neutrinos do have mass and are a small part of the dark matter, but they are "hot": they moved so fast in the early universe that they would have smoothed out small-scale structure. The observed abundance of small galaxies rules out neutrinos as the main component.

## 6. The Expanding Universe

### 6.1 Redshift
Light from distant galaxies is shifted toward longer (redder) wavelengths:
$$z = \frac{\lambda_{\text{observed}} - \lambda_{\text{emitted}}}{\lambda_{\text{emitted}}}, \qquad 1 + z = \frac{a(t_{\text{obs}})}{a(t_{\text{emit}})}$$
**Vesto Slipher** (1912–1917) measured redshifts of spiral nebulae, finding most receding. In cosmology, redshift arises mainly from the **expansion of space** stretching light during its journey (cosmological redshift), not from motion through space. $1 + z$ equals the factor by which the universe has expanded since the light was emitted.

**Derivation of $1 + z = a_{\text{obs}}/a_{\text{emit}}$.** Describe the universe by a **scale factor** $a(t)$: the proper distance between any two galaxies at rest in the expanding frame ("comoving" galaxies) is $d(t) = a(t)\,\chi$, where the comoving distance $\chi$ is fixed. Light travels a proper distance $c\,dt$ in time $dt$, which corresponds to a comoving distance $c\,dt/a(t)$. A wave crest emitted at $t_e$ and received at $t_o$ therefore covers
$$\chi = \int_{t_e}^{t_o}\frac{c\,dt}{a(t)}$$
The next crest leaves at $t_e + \Delta t_e$ and arrives at $t_o + \Delta t_o$, covering the same $\chi$. Subtracting the two integrals, only the short end intervals survive: $\Delta t_o/a(t_o) = \Delta t_e/a(t_e)$. The wavelength is $c$ times the interval between crests, so
$$\frac{\lambda_o}{\lambda_e} = \frac{\Delta t_o}{\Delta t_e} = \frac{a(t_o)}{a(t_e)} = 1 + z$$
Every time interval is stretched by the same factor: a supernova at $z = 1$ appears to brighten and fade twice as slowly as a nearby one, and this **time dilation** is observed in supernova light curves — direct evidence that redshift is not caused by light simply "tiring" on its way.

### 6.2 Hubble's law
**Hubble's law** (Georges Lemaître 1927, Edwin Hubble 1929; now formally the Hubble–Lemaître law): galaxies recede with velocity proportional to distance:
$$\boxed{v = H_0d}$$
$H_0$ is the **Hubble constant**, ~67–73 km/s/Mpc. A galaxy 100 Mpc away recedes at ~7000 km/s.
- Interpretation: space itself is expanding uniformly — like dots on an inflating balloon or raisins in rising bread dough. Every observer sees all distant galaxies receding; there is **no center** to the expansion.
- Lemaître derived the expansion from general relativity and proposed the universe began as a "primeval atom" (1927–1931) — the origin of the Big Bang idea.
- Einstein had added a cosmological constant to keep the universe static; after Hubble's discovery he abandoned it.

**Derivation: why uniform expansion gives a linear law with no center.** Differentiate $d = a(t)\chi$ with $\chi$ fixed:
$$v = \dot d = \dot a\,\chi = \frac{\dot a}{a}\,d = H(t)\,d, \qquad H(t) \equiv \frac{\dot a}{a}$$
Today $H(t_0) = H_0$. Now consider an observer in galaxy B. A galaxy A has velocity $\vec v_A = H\vec r_A$ relative to us, and B has $\vec v_B = H\vec r_B$. Relative to B, A moves at $\vec v_A - \vec v_B = H(\vec r_A - \vec r_B)$: exactly the same law, with distances measured from B. A linear law is the only velocity pattern that looks the same from every galaxy, which is what the cosmological principle demands.

For small redshifts, $z \approx v/c$, so Hubble's law reads $cz \approx H_0 d$. At larger redshifts there is no unique "velocity" or "distance", and one works directly with $z$ and the expansion history $a(t)$.

**Hubble time:** $1/H_0 \approx 14$ billion years for $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc — a rough estimate of the age of the universe. (If every galaxy had always moved at its present speed, all would have been together a time $d/v = 1/H_0$ ago.) Hubble's own 1929 value of about 500 km/s/Mpc gave only 2 billion years, shorter than the age of the Earth then emerging from radioactive dating — an embarrassment that was resolved only when the distance scale was corrected.

### 6.3 Peculiar velocities
Galaxies also move through space under the gravity of their neighbors, typically at a few hundred km/s. These **peculiar velocities** add to the Hubble flow, so Hubble's law is a reliable distance indicator only when $H_0 d$ is much larger than ~300 km/s, beyond about 50–100 Mpc. Andromeda's approach at ~110 km/s is a case where gravity has completely overcome the expansion. Our own motion shows up in the CMB: the Sun moves at about 370 km/s relative to the CMB, making the sky about 3.4 mK warmer in the direction of motion and cooler in the opposite direction (the **CMB dipole**), since $\Delta T/T \approx v/c$.

### Worked Example 7: From a spectrum to a distance

The hydrogen-alpha line (rest wavelength 656.28 nm) in a galaxy's spectrum is observed at 679.90 nm. Find its redshift, recession velocity and distance.

1. $z = (679.90 - 656.28)/656.28 = 23.62/656.28 = 0.0360$.
2. Since $z \ll 1$: $v \approx cz = 0.0360 \times 299\,792$ km/s $\approx 1.08\times10^{4}$ km/s.
3. With $H_0 = 70$ km/s/Mpc: $d = v/H_0 = 10\,790/70 = 154$ Mpc, about 500 million light-years.
4. With $H_0 = 67.4$: $d = 160$ Mpc; with $H_0 = 73.0$: $d = 148$ Mpc.
5. A peculiar velocity of ±300 km/s would change the distance by about ±4 Mpc (3%).

**Answer:** $z = 0.036$, $v \approx 10\,800$ km/s, $d \approx 150$–160 Mpc (about 500 million ly). The 8% spread from the choice of $H_0$ is the Hubble tension in miniature. The light left the galaxy about half a billion years ago, around the time of the Cambrian explosion of animal life on Earth.

### 6.4 Cosmic distances need care

In an expanding universe, "distance" has several meanings. The **proper distance** today (the length of a ruler laid out now) differs from the **light-travel distance** ($c$ times the lookback time), and both differ from the **luminosity distance** inferred from brightness and the **angular-diameter distance** inferred from apparent size. They agree when $z \ll 1$ and diverge strongly at high redshift. For example, the most distant galaxies confirmed by JWST, at $z \approx 14$, emitted their light about 13.5 billion years ago, but are now about 34 billion light-years away.

### 6.5 The Hubble tension
Measurements of $H_0$ from the early universe (CMB with ΛCDM: 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc, Planck) disagree with local distance-ladder measurements (Cepheids + Type Ia supernovae: ~73.0 ± 1.0, SH0ES) at a significance of ~5σ. Alternative local methods (tip of the red giant branch) give intermediate values, around 70. The first gravitational-wave standard siren (the neutron-star merger GW170817, 2017) gave $70^{+12}_{-8}$ km/s/Mpc, too imprecise so far to decide. Whether the tension signals new physics (for example, extra energy in the early universe that shrinks the sound horizon) or unrecognized systematic errors in the distance ladder is one of the biggest open questions in cosmology.

## 7. The Big Bang Model

The universe began ~**13.8 billion years ago** in an extremely hot, dense state and has been expanding and cooling ever since. The Big Bang was **not an explosion into pre-existing space**; it was the rapid expansion of space itself, occurring everywhere. (Fred Hoyle coined the term "Big Bang" in a 1949 radio broadcast, somewhat derisively, as he favored the rival Steady State theory.) The model describes how the universe evolved from a hot, dense state; it does not describe an instant of creation, because at the earliest times (the Planck epoch) the physics is unknown.

### The three pillars of evidence
1. **The expansion of the universe** (Hubble's law).
2. **The cosmic microwave background (CMB).**
3. **Big Bang nucleosynthesis** — the abundances of light elements.
Plus: the evolution of galaxies and quasars with distance (the distant universe looks younger), the large-scale structure, and the ages of the oldest stars (just under the age of the universe).

### 7.1 The cosmic microwave background
- Predicted by Ralph Alpher and Robert Herman (1948) as relic radiation from the hot early universe.
- Discovered accidentally in 1965 by **Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson** at Bell Labs as persistent microwave "noise" from all directions in their horn antenna (after ruling out pigeon droppings); Robert Dicke's group at Princeton interpreted it (Nobel 1978 to Penzias and Wilson).
- **Origin:** ~380 000 years after the Big Bang, the universe cooled to ~3000 K; electrons combined with nuclei to form neutral atoms (**recombination**), and photons could travel freely — the universe became transparent. This "surface of last scattering" is what we see. Expansion since then (by a factor of ~1100) has redshifted the radiation from visible/infrared to microwaves.
- **Perfect blackbody spectrum** at **2.7255 K**, measured by COBE's FIRAS instrument (1990) — the most perfect blackbody known in nature (John Mather; Nobel 2006).
- **Anisotropies:** tiny temperature fluctuations (~1 part in 100 000, discovered by COBE's DMR, George Smoot — Nobel 2006) are the seeds of all later structure. Mapped in detail by **WMAP** (2001–2010) and **Planck** (2009–2013). The **acoustic peaks** in their angular power spectrum encode the universe's geometry (flat), composition, and age with high precision.
- There are ~411 CMB photons per cm³ throughout the universe; a small fraction of the "snow" on old analog TVs came from the CMB.

**Why recombination happened at 3000 K and not at 158 000 K.** Hydrogen's ionization energy is 13.6 eV, which corresponds to $13.6\ \text{eV}/k_B \approx 158\,000$ K. Yet the universe stayed ionized until it was fifty times cooler, because photons outnumber baryons by more than a billion to one (Worked Example 8). Even when the typical photon is far too weak to ionize hydrogen, the rare photons in the high-energy tail of the blackbody spectrum are numerous enough to split every newly formed atom — until the temperature drops to about 3000 K.

**Why the spectrum stays a blackbody.** A blackbody at temperature $T$ has a fixed number of photons in each frequency interval, determined by Planck's law. Expansion stretches every wavelength by $(1+z)$ and dilutes the photon number density by $(1+z)^3$. Substituting into Planck's law shows that the result is exactly a blackbody at the lower temperature
$$T(z) = T_0(1 + z)$$
so the CMB was hotter in the past in direct proportion to $1 + z$. This prediction has been checked at redshifts up to about 3 using absorption lines from molecules and atoms in distant gas clouds, which are excited by the warmer CMB of their era, and through the Sunyaev–Zel'dovich effect in distant clusters.

**What the acoustic peaks are.** Before recombination, photons and baryons formed a single fluid. Gravity (mainly from dark matter) pulled the fluid into overdense regions while photon pressure pushed back, so sound waves rang through the plasma at roughly half the speed of light (a pure photon gas carries sound at $c/\sqrt{3}$; the baryons slow it somewhat). At recombination the waves froze in place. The largest wave that had just reached maximum compression produces the first peak of the power spectrum at multipole $\ell \approx 220$, an angular scale of about 1°. For that known physical size, the observed angle measures the geometry of space, and the result is flat. The relative heights of later peaks measure the densities of baryons and dark matter. The same frozen sound wave left a slight excess of galaxy pairs separated by about 150 Mpc (about 490 million ly) today: the **baryon acoustic oscillation** (BAO) scale, a "standard ruler" used by surveys such as DESI.

### Worked Example 8: The CMB then and now

(a) Find the CMB temperature at recombination ($z = 1090$) and the peak wavelength of its spectrum then and today. (b) Find the number of CMB photons per baryon today, given that the baryon density is about 0.25 protons per m³ (Worked Example 10).

1. $T = T_0(1 + z) = 2.7255 \times 1091 = 2974$ K, about 3000 K.
2. Wien's law, $\lambda_{\max} = b/T$ with $b = 2.898\times10^{-3}$ m K. Today: $\lambda_{\max} = 2.898\times10^{-3}/2.7255 = 1.06\times10^{-3}$ m $= 1.06$ mm (microwaves).
3. At recombination: $\lambda_{\max} = 2.898\times10^{-3}/2974 = 9.7\times10^{-7}$ m $= 0.97$ μm (near-infrared). A 3000 K blackbody also emits plenty of visible light; it would have glowed orange, like a dim incandescent lamp.
4. Photon number density of a blackbody: $n_\gamma = 16\pi\zeta(3)\left(\dfrac{k_BT}{hc}\right)^3$, with $\zeta(3) = 1.202$. For $T = 2.7255$ K this gives $n_\gamma = 4.11\times10^{8}$ m⁻³ $= 411$ cm⁻³.
5. Photons per baryon: $4.11\times10^{8}/0.25 \approx 1.6\times10^{9}$.

**Answer:** (a) about 3000 K, peaking at about 1 μm then and 1.06 mm now; (b) about 1.6 billion photons for every baryon. This enormous ratio — written inversely as the baryon-to-photon ratio $\eta \approx 6\times10^{-10}$ — controls both the timing of recombination and the outcome of nucleosynthesis.

### 7.2 Big Bang nucleosynthesis (BBN)
In the first ~3–20 minutes, when the universe was hot (~10⁹ K) and dense enough for fusion but cooling rapidly, protons and neutrons fused into light nuclei. Predictions (Alpher, Bethe, Gamow, 1948 — the "αβγ paper"; refined later by Chushiro Hayashi in 1950 and others, culminating in the detailed calculations of Robert Wagoner, William Fowler and Fred Hoyle in 1967):
- ~75% hydrogen, ~25% helium-4 by mass (because the neutron-to-proton ratio was ~1:7 when nucleosynthesis began, and nearly all neutrons ended in helium).
- Traces of deuterium (~2.5 × 10⁻⁵ relative to H), helium-3, and lithium-7.
- Observed abundances in primordial gas match these predictions over nine orders of magnitude, and the deuterium abundance precisely fixes the density of ordinary matter (~5% of critical) — consistent with the CMB. (A discrepancy of about a factor of three for lithium-7 — the "lithium problem" — remains unexplained.)
- No elements heavier than lithium formed in significant amounts; those were forged in stars. The αβγ paper had hoped to build all the elements this way, but there are no stable nuclei with mass number 5 or 8, and the universe cooled and thinned too quickly to bridge those gaps.

**Step 1: the neutron-to-proton ratio is set by thermal equilibrium.** At temperatures above about $10^{10}$ K, weak interactions such as $n + \nu_e \rightleftharpoons p + e^-$ and $n + e^+ \rightleftharpoons p + \bar\nu_e$ convert neutrons and protons into each other rapidly. The neutron is heavier by $\Delta m\,c^2 = 1.293$ MeV, so in equilibrium the Boltzmann factor gives
$$\frac{n}{p} = \exp\left(-\frac{\Delta m\,c^2}{k_BT}\right)$$
**Step 2: freeze-out.** The reaction rates fall faster with temperature than the expansion rate does, so at $k_BT \approx 0.7$–0.8 MeV (about $9\times10^{9}$ K, roughly one second after the Big Bang) the conversions stop and the ratio is "frozen".

**Step 3: neutron decay and the deuterium bottleneck.** Free neutrons decay with a mean lifetime of about 879 s. Fusion must start with deuterium ($p + n \to d + \gamma$, binding energy 2.22 MeV), but with over a billion photons per baryon, the high-energy tail of the photon distribution breaks deuterium apart until the temperature falls below roughly $B_d/\ln(1/\eta) \approx 2.22\ \text{MeV}/21 \approx 0.1$ MeV, about $10^{9}$ K. That takes about three minutes, during which some neutrons decay.

**Step 4: almost all surviving neutrons end up in helium-4.** Each helium-4 nucleus contains two neutrons and two protons. If the neutron fraction of all nucleons is $X_n$, the mass fraction of helium is
$$Y = 2X_n = \frac{2(n/p)}{1 + n/p}$$
For $n/p = 1/7$: $Y = (2/7)/(8/7) = 0.25$.

### Worked Example 9: Predicting the helium abundance

Assume weak-interaction freeze-out at $k_BT = 0.75$ MeV and that nucleosynthesis begins 180 s later. Estimate the helium-4 mass fraction.

1. Frozen ratio: $n/p = e^{-1.293/0.75} = e^{-1.724} = 0.178$, about 1 neutron for every 5.6 protons.
2. Neutron fraction: $X_n = 0.178/1.178 = 0.151$.
3. Decay over 180 s: $X_n = 0.151 \times e^{-180/879} = 0.151 \times 0.815 = 0.123$.
4. Ratio at the start of nucleosynthesis: $n/p = 0.123/0.877 = 0.141 \approx 1/7.1$.
5. Helium mass fraction: $Y = 2X_n = 0.247$.

**Answer:** $Y \approx 0.25$, in excellent agreement with the primordial value of about 0.245 measured in metal-poor gas. The result depends on the expansion rate during the first seconds: extra light particles would have sped up the expansion, causing earlier freeze-out and more helium. This line of argument, begun by Gary Steigman, David Schramm and James Gunn in 1977, had limited the number of light neutrino species to at most about four by the 1980s, before particle colliders measured it to be three in 1989.

## 9. Inflation

The basic Big Bang model leaves several puzzles:
1. **Horizon problem:** opposite sides of the sky have the same CMB temperature (to 1 part in 100 000) though they were never in causal contact in standard expansion.
2. **Flatness problem:** the universe is extremely close to spatially flat; any early deviation would have grown enormously.
3. **Monopole problem:** grand unified theories predict abundant magnetic monopoles, never observed.
4. **Origin of structure:** what created the initial density fluctuations?

**How severe is the horizon problem?** Without inflation, the farthest a signal could have traveled by recombination corresponds today to a comoving distance of about 280 Mpc. The last-scattering surface is about 13 900 Mpc away (comoving), so a causally connected patch spans only $280/13\,900 \approx 0.02$ rad, about 1.2° in radius. The CMB sky therefore contains roughly ten thousand patches that could never have exchanged heat, yet all share the same temperature to one part in 100 000.

**How severe is the flatness problem?** From the Friedmann equation (Section 11), the deviation from flatness obeys $\Omega - 1 = kc^2/(a^2H^2)$. During radiation domination $a^2H^2 \propto a^{-2}$, and during matter domination it is $\propto a^{-1}$, so any deviation grows with time. To have $\lvert\Omega - 1\rvert$ below about 0.01 today, it must have been smaller than about $10^{-15}$ at the time of nucleosynthesis — an extraordinarily fine-tuned starting condition unless something drove it there.

**Cosmic inflation** (Alan Guth 1980–81; Andrei Linde; Andreas Albrecht and Paul Steinhardt; Alexei Starobinsky) proposes a brief period of exponential expansion (driven by an "inflaton" field) in the first ~10⁻³² s:
- A tiny causally connected region was stretched to encompass our entire observable universe (solving the horizon problem). A growth factor of $10^{26}$ corresponds to $\ln(10^{26}) \approx 60$ "e-folds" of expansion, the number usually quoted.
- Any curvature was flattened, like the surface of an enormously inflated balloon. During inflation $H$ is nearly constant while $a$ grows exponentially, so $\lvert\Omega - 1\rvert \propto 1/(aH)^2$ is multiplied by about $e^{-120}$, or $10^{-52}$.
- Monopoles were diluted to undetectable densities.
- **Quantum fluctuations** of the inflaton field were stretched to cosmic scales, becoming the density fluctuations seen in the CMB and the seeds of galaxies. Predictions — nearly scale-invariant, Gaussian, adiabatic fluctuations with a slight tilt ($n_s \approx 0.965$) — are confirmed by Planck.
- Inflation predicts primordial **gravitational waves**, detectable as "B-mode" polarization in the CMB; not yet detected (the 2014 BICEP2 claim turned out to be due to galactic dust). Many physicists consider inflation well supported; others note its flexibility and alternatives exist.

## 10. Dark Energy and the Accelerating Universe

In **1998**, two teams studying distant **Type Ia supernovae** (the Supernova Cosmology Project led by Saul Perlmutter, and the High-z Supernova Search Team led by Brian Schmidt and Adam Riess) found that distant supernovae were **fainter** than expected — the expansion of the universe is **accelerating** (Nobel 2011). This requires a repulsive component: **dark energy**.
- Dark energy makes up ~**68%** of the universe's energy content.
- The simplest explanation is Einstein's **cosmological constant** Λ — a constant energy density of empty space (vacuum energy) with negative pressure ($w = P/\rho c^2 = -1$). Its density, about $6\times10^{-27}$ kg/m³ (an energy density of about $5\times10^{-10}$ J/m³), is tiny locally but adds up over cosmic volumes.
- **Cosmological constant problem:** quantum field theory naively predicts a vacuum energy ~10¹²⁰ times larger than observed — perhaps the worst theoretical prediction in physics.
- Alternatives: dynamical dark energy ("quintessence") with $w$ varying over time, or modified gravity. Recent results from the **Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument** (DESI, 2024–2025), combined with supernovae and CMB data, hint at dark energy weakening over time (at ~3–4σ), which, if confirmed, would rule out a pure cosmological constant.
- Corroborating evidence: CMB (geometry is flat, but matter supplies only ~31% of the critical density), baryon acoustic oscillations (a "standard ruler" in galaxy distributions), galaxy cluster counts, and the integrated Sachs–Wolfe effect.

**Why can anything make the expansion speed up?** In general relativity, pressure as well as energy density produces gravity. The **acceleration equation** (derived in Section 11.3) is
$$\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3P}{c^2}\right)$$
For ordinary matter ($P \approx 0$) the right side is negative: gravity decelerates the expansion. A component with $P = w\rho c^2$ gives $\rho(1 + 3w)$, which is negative — producing acceleration — whenever $w < -1/3$. A cosmological constant, with $w = -1$, gives $\rho + 3P/c^2 = -2\rho$: its gravity is repulsive.

**Why "fainter than expected" means acceleration.** If the expansion has been speeding up, it was slower in the past, so it took longer for the universe to expand by a given factor $1 + z$. Light from a supernova at that redshift has traveled longer and farther, and the supernova appears dimmer than it would in a decelerating universe. For example, in today's best-fit model a supernova at $z = 0.5$ appears about 0.2 magnitudes (about 17%) fainter than it would in a low-density universe containing matter alone, and about 0.4 magnitudes fainter than in a universe filled with matter at the critical density. Differences of this size were what the 1998 teams found.

## 11. The ΛCDM Model and the Friedmann Equations

The **standard model of cosmology**, Lambda-Cold Dark Matter, describes the universe with just six basic parameters, fit precisely to CMB and other data (Planck 2018 values):

| Component / parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Ordinary (baryonic) matter | ~4.9% |
| Cold dark matter | ~26.5% |
| Dark energy (Λ) | ~68.5% |
| Neutrinos (which have small masses) | at least ~0.1% (today) |
| Photons (the CMB) | ~0.005% (today) |
| Age of the universe | 13.787 ± 0.020 billion years |
| Hubble constant | 67.4 ± 0.5 km/s/Mpc |
| Spatial curvature | Flat (Ω_k ≈ 0.001 ± 0.002) |
| CMB temperature | 2.7255 K |
| Spectral index $n_s$ | 0.965 |

Everything we have ever seen — stars, planets, gas, people — is less than 5% of the universe.

### 11.1 The Friedmann equation
From general relativity applied to a homogeneous, isotropic universe (Alexander Friedmann, 1922), the expansion rate $H = \dot a/a$ obeys:
$$H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2} + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}$$
where $a(t)$ is the **scale factor** (relative size of the universe; $a = 1$ today), $\rho$ the total density and $k$ the curvature. The **critical density** for a flat universe is $\rho_c = \frac{3H^2}{8\pi G} \approx 9\times10^{-27}$ kg/m³ — about 5–6 hydrogen atoms per cubic meter. Radiation density falls as $a^{-4}$, matter as $a^{-3}$, and a cosmological constant stays constant — so the universe passed from radiation domination to matter domination (~50 000 years) to dark-energy domination (~10.3 billion years).

**A Newtonian derivation.** The full derivation needs general relativity, but a Newtonian argument gives the same equation (without Λ). Take a sphere of radius $R(t) = a(t)\chi$ cut out of a uniform universe of density $\rho$. By the shell theorem, a galaxy of mass $m$ on its surface feels only the mass inside, $M = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi R^3\rho$, which stays fixed as the sphere expands. Its energy is conserved:
$$\frac{1}{2}m\dot R^2 - \frac{GMm}{R} = E$$
1. Substitute $R = a\chi$ and $M = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi a^3\chi^3\rho$: $\tfrac{1}{2}\dot a^2\chi^2 - \tfrac{4}{3}\pi G\rho a^2\chi^2 = E/m$.
2. Divide by $\tfrac{1}{2}a^2\chi^2$: $(\dot a/a)^2 = \tfrac{8\pi G}{3}\rho + \dfrac{2E}{m\chi^2a^2}$.
3. Writing the constant $2E/(m\chi^2)$ as $-kc^2$ gives the Friedmann equation.
The geometry of space corresponds to the energy: $k > 0$ (closed) is a bound universe with negative energy, $k < 0$ (open) has positive energy, and $k = 0$ (flat) sits exactly at "escape velocity". Setting $k = 0$ gives the critical density $\rho_c = 3H^2/(8\pi G)$, and the **density parameter** $\Omega = \rho/\rho_c$ measures the content of the universe in units of it.

### 11.2 How each component dilutes: the fluid equation

As a comoving volume $V \propto a^3$ expands, the first law of thermodynamics with no heat flow, $d(\rho c^2V) = -P\,dV$, gives the **fluid equation**
$$\dot\rho + 3\frac{\dot a}{a}\left(\rho + \frac{P}{c^2}\right) = 0$$
For an equation of state $P = w\rho c^2$ with constant $w$, this integrates to $\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)}$. In a flat universe dominated by a single component, the Friedmann equation $\dot a^2 \propto \rho a^2 \propto a^{-1-3w}$ then gives $a \propto t^{2/(3(1+w))}$ (or exponential growth for $w = -1$):

| Component | $w$ | Density scales as | Expansion when it dominates |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radiation (and relativistic neutrinos) | 1/3 | $a^{-4}$ | $a \propto t^{1/2}$ |
| Matter (ordinary and dark) | 0 | $a^{-3}$ | $a \propto t^{2/3}$ |
| Curvature term (empty open universe) | −1/3 (effective) | $a^{-2}$ | $a \propto t$ |
| Cosmological constant | −1 | constant | $a \propto e^{Ht}$ |

The physical reasons are simple. Matter particles are conserved, so their density falls as the volume, $a^{-3}$. Photons are also diluted by $a^{-3}$, and each one loses energy as its wavelength stretches by $a$, giving $a^{-4}$. Vacuum energy is a property of space itself, so new space comes with the same energy density — the negative pressure supplies exactly the work needed.

Because radiation falls off fastest, it dominated early; matter took over when $\Omega_r(1+z)^4 = \Omega_m(1+z)^3$, i.e. at $1 + z = \Omega_m/\Omega_r \approx 0.315/9.2\times10^{-5} \approx 3400$, about 50 000 years after the Big Bang (radiation here includes the three neutrino species, which were relativistic then).

With all components included, the Friedmann equation becomes an expression for the expansion rate at any redshift:
$$H(z) = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_r(1+z)^4 + \Omega_m(1+z)^3 + \Omega_k(1+z)^2 + \Omega_\Lambda}$$
where $\Omega_k = -kc^2/H_0^2$ and the $\Omega$ values add up to 1.

### 11.3 The acceleration equation and the deceleration parameter

Differentiating the Friedmann equation with respect to time and eliminating $\dot\rho$ with the fluid equation gives
$$\frac{\ddot a}{a} = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}\left(\rho + \frac{3P}{c^2}\right) + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}$$
Cosmologists summarize the result with the **deceleration parameter** $q = -\ddot a\,a/\dot a^2$, which for today's mix of matter and Λ is
$$q_0 = \frac{\Omega_m}{2} - \Omega_\Lambda = \frac{0.315}{2} - 0.685 = -0.53$$
A negative value means acceleration. The expansion was decelerating in the past, when matter was denser; it switched to acceleration when $\Omega_m(1+z)^3 = 2\Omega_\Lambda$ (Practice Problem 11).

### 11.4 The age of the universe

Since $H = \dot a/a$, the time elapsed while the scale factor grows from 0 to 1 is
$$t_0 = \int_0^1 \frac{da}{aH(a)}$$
- **Flat, matter only:** $H = H_0a^{-3/2}$, so $t_0 = \int_0^1 a^{1/2}\,da/H_0 = \dfrac{2}{3H_0}$, and $a = (t/t_0)^{2/3}$.
- **Flat, matter plus Λ** (radiation neglected): the integral can be done exactly, giving
$$t_0 = \frac{2}{3H_0\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}}\,\sinh^{-1}\sqrt{\frac{\Omega_\Lambda}{\Omega_m}}$$

### Worked Example 10: The critical density and the cosmic census

Take $H_0 = 67.4$ km/s/Mpc, $\Omega_b = 0.049$ and $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.685$. Find the critical density, the density of ordinary matter, and the energy density of dark energy.

1. Convert $H_0$ to SI: $H_0 = 67.4 \times 10^{3}/3.086\times10^{22}$ s⁻¹ $= 2.184\times10^{-18}$ s⁻¹.
2. $\rho_c = 3H_0^2/(8\pi G) = 3 \times (2.184\times10^{-18})^2/(8\pi \times 6.674\times10^{-11}) = 8.53\times10^{-27}$ kg/m³.
3. In hydrogen atoms (mass $1.674\times10^{-27}$ kg): $8.53/1.674 \approx 5.1$ atoms per cubic meter. In astronomers' units, $\rho_c \approx 1.26\times10^{11}\ M_\odot$ per cubic megaparsec.
4. Ordinary matter: $\rho_b = 0.049 \times 8.53\times10^{-27} = 4.2\times10^{-28}$ kg/m³, or $4.2\times10^{-28}/1.673\times10^{-27} = 0.25$ protons per m³ — about one proton in every 4 m³.
5. Dark energy: $\rho_\Lambda c^2 = 0.685 \times 8.53\times10^{-27} \times (3.00\times10^{8})^2 = 5.3\times10^{-10}$ J/m³.

**Answer:** $\rho_c \approx 8.5\times10^{-27}$ kg/m³ (about five hydrogen atoms per m³); ordinary matter averages one proton per 4 m³; dark energy amounts to about $5\times10^{-10}$ joules per cubic meter. For comparison, air at sea level is about $10^{26}$ times denser than the critical density — the universe on average is an excellent vacuum.

### Worked Example 11: Three estimates of the age of the universe

With $H_0 = 67.4$ km/s/Mpc, compute (a) the Hubble time, (b) the age of a flat universe containing only matter, and (c) the age of a flat universe with $\Omega_m = 0.315$ and $\Omega_\Lambda = 0.685$.

1. (a) $1/H_0 = 1/(2.184\times10^{-18}\ \text{s}^{-1}) = 4.578\times10^{17}$ s; dividing by $3.156\times10^{16}$ s per Gyr gives 14.5 Gyr.
2. (b) $t_0 = \tfrac{2}{3} \times 14.5$ Gyr $= 9.7$ Gyr.
3. (c) $\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda/\Omega_m} = \sqrt{0.685/0.315} = 1.475$ and $\sinh^{-1}(1.475) = \ln(1.475 + \sqrt{1 + 1.475^2}) = 1.181$.
4. Prefactor: $2/(3\sqrt{0.685}) = 0.805$.
5. $t_0 = 0.805 \times 1.181 \times 14.5$ Gyr $= 13.8$ Gyr.

**Answer:** (a) 14.5 Gyr; (b) 9.7 Gyr; (c) 13.8 Gyr, matching the Planck value of 13.787 Gyr. A matter-only universe would be younger than its oldest stars (globular clusters about 12–13 Gyr old) — the "age problem" of the early 1990s. Dark energy resolves it: deceleration in the past was weaker and acceleration recently has made the expansion faster, so the universe needed more time to reach today's expansion rate.

### 11.5 The observable universe
Although the universe is 13.8 billion years old, the **observable universe** has a radius of about **46 billion light-years** (comoving distance; often quoted as 46.5 billion), because space has expanded while light traveled. The comoving distance to a source at redshift $z$ is $\chi = c\int_0^z dz'/H(z')$; taking $z \to \infty$ gives the radius of the observable universe. It contains ~10⁸⁰ atoms. The whole universe may be much larger, or infinite. Galaxies beyond the **Hubble sphere** (radius $c/H_0 \approx 14.5$ billion ly) currently recede faster than light — permitted in general relativity, since it is space itself expanding — and in the far future, accelerating expansion will carry most galaxies beyond our observable horizon.

## 12. The Fate of the Universe

Depends on the nature of dark energy:
- **Big Freeze (heat death):** with a cosmological constant, expansion accelerates forever. Galaxies beyond the Local Group recede out of view (in ~100–150 billion years, the merged Milky Way–Andromeda galaxy will be alone in its observable universe); star formation ends (~10¹⁴ years) as gas runs out; stars burn out; white dwarfs fade; stellar remnants may eventually decay (if protons decay); black holes evaporate by Hawking radiation (~10⁶⁷–10¹⁰⁰ years); the universe approaches maximum entropy — cold, dark and dilute. Currently the favored scenario.
- **Big Rip:** if dark energy grows stronger ($w < -1$, "phantom energy"), it eventually tears apart galaxies, stars, planets and even atoms in finite time.
- **Big Crunch:** if dark energy weakened and reversed (or matter dominated), expansion could halt and reverse into a collapse — some interpretations of recent DESI hints revive this possibility for the very distant future.
- **Vacuum decay:** if the Higgs vacuum is metastable, a bubble of lower-energy vacuum could nucleate and expand at near light speed — extremely improbable on any relevant timescale.

With a pure cosmological constant the future expansion becomes exponential, $a \propto e^{H_\Lambda t}$ with $H_\Lambda = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda} \approx 56$ km/s/Mpc, so distances between unbound galaxies grow by a factor of $e$ roughly every 17 billion years. Gravitationally bound systems — atoms, planets, stars, galaxies and the Local Group — do not take part in this expansion.

## 13. Applications

Cosmology is the most "useless" science in the narrow sense — no one will ever travel to a quasar — but its tools and discoveries reach far into everyday life:
- **Navigation and geodesy.** Quasars are so distant that they show no measurable motion across the sky, so their positions define the **International Celestial Reference Frame**. Radio telescopes thousands of kilometers apart observe the same quasars (very-long-baseline interferometry, VLBI) and time the arrival of their signals; this monitors Earth's rotation and orientation, which satellite navigation systems need, and measures the drift of continents at centimeters per year.
- **Imaging technology.** Charge-coupled devices (CCDs), invented at Bell Labs in 1969, were quickly adopted and refined by astronomers, whose demand for sensitive, low-noise detectors helped drive the technology now found in digital cameras and medical X-ray sensors. Adaptive optics, built to sharpen telescope images blurred by the atmosphere, is used to image the living retina in ophthalmology.
- **Signal processing.** Radio interferometry reconstructs images from Fourier components of the sky (aperture synthesis, recognized with the 1974 Nobel Prize to Martin Ryle); the same mathematics underlies image reconstruction in MRI. In the 1990s, radio astronomers at Australia's CSIRO, led by John O'Sullivan, developed techniques that became a key patent in Wi-Fi.
- **Detector physics.** CMB experiments pushed superconducting bolometers and cryogenics to extraordinary sensitivity; dark matter searches developed ultrapure materials and ultra-low-radioactivity techniques now used in other rare-event experiments, such as searches for neutrinoless double-beta decay.
- **Computing and data science.** Simulating the growth of the cosmic web requires following billions of particles under gravity; tree algorithms and parallel computing methods developed for these simulations are shared with molecular dynamics and plasma physics. Surveys producing petabytes of data drive machine-learning classification, and projects such as Galaxy Zoo (2007) pioneered online citizen science.
- **Fundamental physics as a laboratory.** The universe tests physics at energies no accelerator can reach: BBN counts neutrino species, the CMB bounds the sum of neutrino masses, and dark energy confronts quantum field theory.

## 14. Connections to Other Fields

- **General relativity:** the Friedmann equations, gravitational lensing, black holes and gravitational waves all come from Einstein's theory, and cosmology tests it on the largest scales.
- **Thermodynamics and statistical physics:** the CMB is a textbook blackbody; its cooling, $T \propto 1/a$, is the adiabatic expansion of a photon gas, for which $TV^{1/3}$ stays constant. Boltzmann factors govern the neutron-to-proton ratio and recombination.
- **Nuclear physics:** BBN uses measured nuclear reaction rates and the neutron lifetime; stars later forged carbon, oxygen and heavier elements (stellar nucleosynthesis, worked out by Burbidge, Burbidge, Fowler and Hoyle in 1957).
- **Particle physics:** dark matter candidates (WIMPs, axions), neutrino masses, baryogenesis and the Higgs field link cosmology to the Standard Model and beyond.
- **Chemistry:** the first atoms formed at recombination; the first molecule to form was the helium hydride ion HeH⁺ (finally detected in space in 2019, in a planetary nebula), and molecular hydrogen H₂ later provided the cooling that let the first gas clouds collapse into stars.
- **Biology:** every carbon, nitrogen and oxygen atom in living things was made in stars after the Big Bang; only hydrogen, most helium and a little lithium are primordial. The timing of galaxy and star formation sets when planets with the ingredients for life could first exist.
- **Mathematics:** non-Euclidean geometry describes curved space; differential equations govern $a(t)$; spherical harmonics and Fourier analysis describe CMB maps and galaxy clustering; Bayesian statistics and Markov chain Monte Carlo methods are used to fit cosmological parameters.
- **Earth and planetary science:** the Solar System formed 9.2 billion years after the Big Bang from gas enriched by generations of stars; VLBI on quasars monitors plate tectonics.

## 15. Common Misconceptions

- **"The Big Bang was an explosion at a point in space."** There is no center and no edge. The Big Bang happened everywhere: every point in today's universe was part of the hot, dense early state. An explosion would produce a pressure gradient and a center; the uniform expansion of Hubble's law has neither.
- **"Since other galaxies move away from us, we must be at the center."** Section 6.2 shows that a linear law looks the same from every galaxy; an observer anywhere would find that they appear to be at the center.
- **"Galaxies receding faster than light violate relativity."** Special relativity forbids objects from overtaking light locally. Recession speeds beyond the Hubble sphere arise from the expansion of space between distant objects, and general relativity permits them. We can still see some galaxies that are receding faster than light today, because their light was emitted when they were in a region from which it could eventually reach us.
- **"Cosmological redshift is just a Doppler shift."** For small $z$, $v \approx cz$ works, but redshift really measures how much the universe expanded during the light's journey. Applying the special-relativistic Doppler formula at large $z$ gives "velocities" that have no clear meaning in an expanding universe.
- **"Everything expands, including atoms, people and the Solar System."** Bound systems are held together by forces far stronger than the tiny effect of cosmic expansion on their scale; they remain at fixed size. Only the distances between unbound systems, such as separate galaxy groups, grow.
- **"The observable universe has a radius of 13.8 billion light-years."** 13.8 billion years is the light-travel time from the most distant regions, but the space between has expanded meanwhile; the current distance to the edge of what we can see is about 46 billion light-years.
- **"Dark matter is just ordinary matter that is too dim to see."** BBN and the CMB fix ordinary matter at about 5% of the total, while all the matter amounts to about 31%. Microlensing surveys rule out dim compact objects as the main halo component, and ordinary gas would show up in X-rays or radio emission.
- **"Dark matter and dark energy are the same thing, or related."** They have opposite effects: dark matter clumps and pulls (it builds galaxies), while dark energy is smooth and drives acceleration. They dilute differently ($a^{-3}$ versus constant). The word "dark" is the only thing they definitely share.
- **"When the Milky Way and Andromeda merge, stars will crash into each other."** Stars are separated by tens of millions of their own diameters; almost none will collide. Tides will rearrange orbits, and the Sun will probably end up in a different part of the merged galaxy.
- **"The Hubble constant is constant forever."** It is the same everywhere in space at a given time, but $H(t)$ changes as the universe evolves. It was much larger in the past and in a Λ-dominated future approaches about 56 km/s/Mpc.
- **"The CMB comes from a particular place."** It fills all of space; we see the photons that happen to be arriving from a spherical shell at the right distance. Observers in another galaxy see their own shell.
- **"'Early-type' galaxies formed early."** The terms "early" and "late" type are leftovers of Hubble's abandoned evolutionary guess. Elliptical galaxies do have old stars, but the labels themselves carry no information about age.

1. (a) $L_{\text{Edd}} = 1.26\times10^{31}\ \text{W} \times 10^{9} = 1.26\times10^{40}$ W $\approx 3.3\times10^{13}\ L_\odot$.
2. Accretion rate: $\dot m = L/(0.1c^2) = 1.26\times10^{40}/(8.99\times10^{15})$ kg/s $= 1.4\times10^{24}$ kg/s $\approx 22\ M_\odot$ per year.
3. (b) Of the infalling mass $\dot m$, a fraction $\varepsilon$ is radiated, so the hole grows at $dM/dt = (1 - \varepsilon)\dot m$, with $\dot m = L_{\text{Edd}}/(\varepsilon c^2)$.
4. Substituting $L_{\text{Edd}} = 4\pi GMm_pc/\sigma_T$: $\dfrac{dM}{dt} = \dfrac{1 - \varepsilon}{\varepsilon}\,\dfrac{4\pi Gm_p}{\sigma_T c}\,M = \dfrac{M}{t_e}$, so $M = M_0e^{t/t_e}$ with $t_e$ as given.
5. $\sigma_Tc/(4\pi Gm_p) = (6.652\times10^{-29} \times 3.00\times10^{8})/(4\pi \times 6.674\times10^{-11} \times 1.673\times10^{-27})$ s $= 1.42\times10^{16}$ s $= 450$ Myr. With $\varepsilon = 0.1$: $t_e = (0.1/0.9) \times 450 = 50$ Myr.
6. (c) Growth by $10^{9}/100 = 10^{7}$ needs $\ln(10^{7}) = 16.1$ e-folds, i.e. $16.1 \times 50 = 810$ Myr of uninterrupted Eddington-limited accretion.

**Answer:** (a) $1.3\times10^{40}$ W and about 22 $M_\odot$ per year; (b) $t_e \approx 50$ Myr; (c) 810 Myr is longer than the 760 Myr available, even if accretion began at the Big Bang and never paused — and the first stars, which could leave such seeds, formed only after about 100 million years. This is why billion-solar-mass black holes in quasars at $z \approx 7$ suggest either heavier "direct-collapse" seeds of about $10^{4}$–$10^{5}\ M_\odot$ (a $10^{5}\ M_\odot$ seed needs only 9.2 e-folds, about 460 Myr) or periods of faster-than-Eddington growth.

**11.**
1. (a) $q_0 = \Omega_m/2 - \Omega_\Lambda = 0.1575 - 0.685 = -0.53$: accelerating.
2. (b) At redshift $z$, $q = 0$ when the matter term $\tfrac{1}{2}\Omega_m(1+z)^3$ equals $\Omega_\Lambda$: $(1+z)^3 = 2\Omega_\Lambda/\Omega_m = 2 \times 0.685/0.315 = 4.35$, so $1 + z = 1.632$ and $z = 0.63$.
3. (c) Equal densities: $(1+z)^3 = \Omega_\Lambda/\Omega_m = 2.175$, so $1 + z = 1.296$ and $z = 0.30$.

**Answer:** $q_0 \approx -0.53$; acceleration began at $z \approx 0.63$ (about 6.1 billion years ago, when the universe was about 7.7 billion years old); dark energy overtook matter at $z \approx 0.30$ (about 3.5 billion years ago). Acceleration began before dark energy dominated, because the pressure of Λ counts three times in the acceleration equation.

**12.** Before recombination, the universe was a plasma in which free electrons scattered photons constantly, so light could not travel far: the universe was opaque, like the inside of a cloud or of the Sun. The CMB is the light released when the fog cleared, so it is the earliest light we can ever see. Messengers that interact more weakly could carry earlier information: **neutrinos** decoupled at about one second (the cosmic neutrino background, about 1.95 K, not yet detected directly), and **gravitational waves** could travel freely from as early as inflation (primordial gravitational waves, sought through CMB B-mode polarization and future detectors).

### Key equations

- Distance modulus: $m - M = 5\log_{10}(d/10\ \text{pc})$
- Circular velocity and enclosed mass: $v^2 = GM(r)/r$; flat curve $\Rightarrow M \propto r$, $\rho = v^2/(4\pi Gr^2)$
- Virial mass of a uniform sphere: $M = 5\sigma_r^2R/G$
- Light deflection and Einstein radius: $\alpha = 4GM/(c^2b)$; $\theta_E = \sqrt{(4GM/c^2)\,D_{ls}/(D_lD_s)}$
- Eddington luminosity: $L_{\text{Edd}} = 4\pi GMm_pc/\sigma_T \approx 1.26\times10^{31}\ \text{W}\,(M/M_\odot)$; accretion power $L = \varepsilon\dot mc^2$
- Redshift and scale factor: $1 + z = \lambda_o/\lambda_e = a(t_o)/a(t_e)$
- Hubble–Lemaître law: $v = H_0d$, with $H = \dot a/a$; Hubble time $1/H_0$
- CMB temperature: $T(z) = T_0(1+z)$, $T_0 = 2.7255$ K
- Neutron-to-proton ratio and helium fraction: $n/p = e^{-\Delta mc^2/k_BT}$; $Y = 2(n/p)/(1 + n/p)$
- Friedmann equation: $H^2 = \frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho - \frac{kc^2}{a^2} + \frac{\Lambda c^2}{3}$; critical density $\rho_c = 3H^2/(8\pi G)$
- Fluid equation: $\dot\rho + 3H(\rho + P/c^2) = 0$, giving $\rho \propto a^{-3(1+w)}$
- Acceleration equation: $\ddot a/a = -\frac{4\pi G}{3}(\rho + 3P/c^2) + \Lambda c^2/3$; $q_0 = \Omega_m/2 - \Omega_\Lambda$
- Expansion rate: $H(z) = H_0\sqrt{\Omega_r(1+z)^4 + \Omega_m(1+z)^3 + \Omega_k(1+z)^2 + \Omega_\Lambda}$
- Ages: $t_0 = 2/(3H_0)$ (flat, matter only); $t_0 = \frac{2}{3H_0\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda}}\sinh^{-1}\sqrt{\Omega_\Lambda/\Omega_m}$ (flat, matter plus Λ)
