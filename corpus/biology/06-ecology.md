# Ecology: Populations, Communities, Ecosystems and the Biosphere

**Ecology** (from Greek *oikos*, "house") is the scientific study of interactions between organisms and their environment — both living (biotic) and non-living (abiotic). Ernst Haeckel coined the term in 1866. Ecology addresses questions from why a species lives where it does to how carbon cycles through the planet, and it provides the scientific basis for conservation and environmental management.

## 1. Levels of Ecological Organization

| Level | Definition | Example question |
|---|---|---|
| Organism | Individual's adaptations to its environment | How does a camel tolerate dehydration? |
| Population | Group of the same species in an area | Why does the lynx population cycle? |
| Community | All populations of different species in an area | How do wolves affect vegetation? |
| Ecosystem | Community plus abiotic environment | How does nitrogen cycle in a forest? |
| Landscape | Multiple interacting ecosystems | How do corridors affect species movement? |
| Biosphere | All ecosystems on Earth | How does climate change affect global productivity? |

## 2. The Abiotic Environment and Biomes

Key abiotic factors: temperature, water, sunlight, wind, soil and nutrients, salinity, pH, oxygen, disturbances (fire, floods). **Climate** — especially temperature and precipitation — largely determines the distribution of terrestrial **biomes** (large regions with characteristic vegetation).

Global climate patterns arise from uneven solar heating (more intense at the equator), Earth's tilt (seasons), atmospheric circulation cells (Hadley cells: rising moist air at the equator → tropical rainforests; descending dry air at ~30° latitude → deserts), ocean currents, and topography (**rain shadows** on the leeward side of mountains).

| Biome | Climate | Vegetation | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tropical rainforest | Hot, wet year-round (>2000 mm/yr) | Tall, layered evergreen forest | Highest terrestrial biodiversity (>50% of species on ~6–7% of land); nutrient-poor soils |
| Tropical savanna | Warm; distinct wet and dry seasons | Grasses with scattered trees | Large grazing herds; fire-maintained |
| Desert | Very dry (<250 mm/yr); hot or cold | Succulents, drought-adapted shrubs | ~1/3 of land surface; CAM plants |
| Chaparral (Mediterranean) | Mild wet winters, hot dry summers | Evergreen shrubs | Fire-adapted |
| Temperate grassland | Cold winters, hot summers, moderate rainfall | Grasses | Deep fertile soils; prairies, steppes, pampas — largely converted to agriculture |
| Temperate deciduous forest | Four seasons, moderate precipitation | Broadleaf trees shedding leaves in winter | |
| Temperate rainforest | Mild, very wet | Giant conifers | Pacific Northwest, New Zealand |
| Boreal forest (taiga) | Long cold winters, short summers | Conifers | Largest terrestrial biome |
| Tundra | Very cold, short growing season, permafrost | Mosses, lichens, low shrubs | Arctic and alpine; large carbon stores in permafrost |

**Aquatic biomes** cover ~75% of Earth's surface: oceans (intertidal, neritic, open ocean — pelagic and benthic, photic and aphotic zones), coral reefs (very high biodiversity; built by corals with symbiotic algae), estuaries (highly productive nurseries), freshwater lakes (with thermal stratification and seasonal turnover), rivers and wetlands. **Deep-sea hydrothermal vent** communities are based on **chemosynthesis** rather than photosynthesis.

## 3. Population Ecology

A **population** is characterized by size ($N$), **density** (individuals per area), **dispersion** (clumped — most common; uniform — territorial; random) and age structure.

### Population growth
Change in population: $\Delta N = B - D + I - E$ (births, deaths, immigration, emigration).

**Exponential growth** (unlimited resources):
$$\frac{dN}{dt} = rN \quad\Rightarrow\quad N(t) = N_0e^{rt}$$
$r$ = per capita growth rate (birth rate − death rate). The **doubling time** is $t_d = \ln2/r \approx 0.693/r$. Exponential growth produces a **J-shaped curve**; it occurs when populations colonize new habitats or recover from crashes (e.g. invasive species, bacteria in fresh medium). Darwin calculated that a single pair of elephants (the slowest-breeding animal) would produce ~19 million descendants in 750 years if unchecked.

**Logistic growth** (limited resources):
$$\frac{dN}{dt} = rN\left(1 - \frac NK\right)$$
$K$ = **carrying capacity** — the maximum population size the environment can sustain. Growth is fastest at $N = K/2$ and slows to zero as $N \to K$, producing an **S-shaped (sigmoid) curve** (Pierre-François Verhulst, 1838; Raymond Pearl). Real populations may overshoot and oscillate around $K$.

### Limiting factors
- **Density-dependent factors** (effect increases with density): competition for food and space, predation, disease, parasitism, territoriality, toxic wastes, stress. These regulate populations toward $K$.
- **Density-independent factors** (effect independent of density): weather, natural disasters, fire, pollution.

### Population cycles
The famous **snowshoe hare–Canada lynx** cycle (~10 years), documented in Hudson's Bay Company fur records from the 1800s: lynx numbers track hares with a lag. Driven by predation and hare food supply (and stress effects on hare reproduction). The **Lotka–Volterra predator–prey equations** model such oscillations:
$$\frac{dH}{dt} = aH - bHP, \qquad \frac{dP}{dt} = cHP - dP$$

### Life-history strategies
| Trait | r-selected | K-selected |
|---|---|---|
| Environment | Unpredictable, disturbed | Stable, near carrying capacity |
| Offspring | Many, small | Few, large |
| Parental care | Little or none | Extensive |
| Maturity | Early | Late |
| Lifespan | Short | Long |
| Examples | Insects, weeds, bacteria, mice | Elephants, whales, humans, oak trees |
(A simplification; most species lie on a continuum.)

### Survivorship curves
- **Type I:** low mortality until old age (humans in developed countries, large mammals).
- **Type II:** constant mortality rate throughout life (many birds, lizards).
- **Type III:** very high early mortality; survivors live long (fish, oysters, most plants and insects).

### Human population
~8.2 billion people (2025). It took ~200 000 years to reach 1 billion (~1804), then 123 years to reach 2 billion (1927), and only 12 years each from 6 to 7 (2011) and 7 to 8 billion (2022). Growth rate peaked at ~2.1% per year around 1965–1970 and is now ~0.9%. The **demographic transition** model describes the shift from high birth and death rates to low ones with economic development. UN projections suggest a peak of ~10.3 billion in the 2080s. **Age-structure diagrams** (pyramids) indicate future growth: broad bases signal rapid growth (e.g. many sub-Saharan African countries); narrow bases signal decline (Japan, Italy, South Korea — whose fertility rate fell below 0.8 births per woman).

## 4. Community Ecology

### The ecological niche
A species' **niche** is its role and position in the ecosystem — the full range of conditions and resources it uses (G. Evelyn Hutchinson's "n-dimensional hypervolume"). The **fundamental niche** is what it could occupy; the **realized niche** is what it actually occupies given competitors and predators.

**Connell's barnacles (1961):** on Scottish rocky shores, *Chthamalus* could live throughout the intertidal zone (fundamental niche) but was restricted to the upper zone by competition with *Balanus* (realized niche).

### Interspecific interactions

| Interaction | Effect on species 1 | Effect on species 2 | Examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| Competition | − | − | Plants competing for light; lions and hyenas |
| Predation (including herbivory) | + | − | Wolves and elk; deer browsing plants |
| Parasitism | + | − | Tapeworms, malaria *Plasmodium*, ticks, mistletoe |
| Mutualism | + | + | Flowers and pollinators; mycorrhizal fungi and plant roots (~80–90% of land plants); corals and zooxanthellae; gut bacteria; lichens (fungus + alga); legumes and nitrogen-fixing *Rhizobium*; cleaner fish |
| Commensalism | + | 0 | Barnacles on whales; cattle egrets and grazing cattle; epiphytes on trees |
| Amensalism | − | 0 | Penicillium mold inhibiting bacteria |

### Competition
- **Competitive exclusion principle** (Georgii Gause, 1934): two species competing for the same limiting resources cannot coexist indefinitely — one will outcompete the other. Gause showed this with *Paramecium aurelia* and *P. caudatum* in culture.
- **Resource partitioning:** coexisting similar species divide resources (MacArthur's warblers feeding in different parts of spruce trees, 1958; Caribbean *Anolis* lizards using different perches).
- **Character displacement:** competing species evolve greater differences where they co-occur (Galápagos finch beak sizes).

### Predation and defenses
- Predator adaptations: speed, camouflage, venom, keen senses, cooperative hunting.
- Prey defenses: **cryptic coloration** (camouflage), **aposematic (warning) coloration** (poison dart frogs, wasps), chemical defenses (skunks, milkweed alkaloids in monarch butterflies), **mimicry** — **Batesian** (harmless species mimics a harmful one: hoverflies resembling wasps; viceroy and monarch — the viceroy is now known to be partly unpalatable itself) and **Müllerian** (several harmful species converge on a shared warning pattern: bees and wasps; *Heliconius* butterflies) — plus behavioral defenses (alarm calls, herding, playing dead).
- Plants defend against herbivores with thorns, tough leaves, and secondary compounds (nicotine, caffeine, tannins, cyanogenic glycosides).

### Keystone species and trophic cascades
A **keystone species** has a disproportionately large effect relative to its abundance.
- **Robert Paine's starfish experiment** (1960s, Washington state): removing the predatory sea star *Pisaster* from tide pools let mussels dominate, and species diversity dropped from 15 to 8 — coining the "keystone species" concept.
- **Sea otters** eat sea urchins; where otters were hunted out, urchins exploded and destroyed **kelp forests**.
- **Wolves in Yellowstone** (reintroduced 1995): altered elk behavior and numbers, allowing aspen and willow recovery in some areas, with effects on beavers and streams (the strength of this "trophic cascade" is debated).
- **Ecosystem engineers** physically modify habitats (beavers building dams, elephants clearing trees, corals building reefs).

### Species diversity
**Diversity** combines **species richness** (number of species) and **evenness** (relative abundance), measured by indices like Shannon's $H' = -\sum p_i\ln p_i$ or Simpson's index. Diversity generally increases toward the tropics (the **latitudinal diversity gradient**). Diverse communities are often more productive and more resilient to disturbance.

**Island biogeography** (MacArthur and Wilson, 1967): the number of species on an island reflects a balance between immigration (higher for islands near the mainland) and extinction (lower on larger islands). Larger, closer islands have more species. The **species–area relationship** $S = cA^z$ (z ≈ 0.25) implies that losing 90% of a habitat eventually loses about half its species. Applied to habitat fragments and nature reserve design.

### Ecological succession
The sequence of community changes after a disturbance:
- **Primary succession:** on bare, lifeless substrate with no soil (lava flows, retreating glaciers, new volcanic islands like Surtsey, 1963). **Pioneer species** (lichens, mosses) weather rock and build soil, followed by grasses, shrubs and eventually trees. Takes centuries to millennia.
- **Secondary succession:** where soil remains after a disturbance (abandoned farmland, after forest fires or hurricanes). Faster (decades). Example: old-field succession in eastern North America — annual weeds → perennials and grasses → shrubs → pines → hardwoods.
- The traditional idea of a stable **climax community** has been replaced by a dynamic view: disturbances (fire, storms, grazing) are frequent, and intermediate levels of disturbance can maximize diversity (**intermediate disturbance hypothesis**).
- Some ecosystems depend on disturbance: many pine species have **serotinous** cones that open only after fire.

## 5. Ecosystem Ecology

### Energy flow
Energy flows **one way** through ecosystems — from the Sun, through organisms, and out as heat (it is not recycled), consistent with the laws of thermodynamics.

**Trophic levels:**
1. **Primary producers (autotrophs):** plants, algae, cyanobacteria (photosynthesis); chemosynthetic bacteria at vents.
2. **Primary consumers:** herbivores.
3. **Secondary consumers:** carnivores eating herbivores.
4. **Tertiary and quaternary consumers:** top predators.
5. **Decomposers and detritivores** (bacteria, fungi, earthworms, dung beetles): break down dead matter and wastes, recycling nutrients — essential to all ecosystems.

**Food chains** are linear sequences (grass → grasshopper → frog → snake → hawk); **food webs** show interconnected feeding relationships and are more realistic.

### Productivity
- **Gross primary productivity (GPP):** total rate of photosynthetic energy capture.
- **Net primary productivity (NPP)** = GPP − respiration by producers: the energy available to consumers. Global terrestrial NPP ≈ 55–60 billion tonnes of carbon per year; oceanic NPP is similar, mostly by phytoplankton (which produce roughly half of Earth's oxygen).
- Most productive ecosystems per area: estuaries, swamps/marshes, tropical rainforests, coral reefs, algal beds. Least: deserts, tundra, open ocean (the open ocean is nevertheless a large total contributor because of its vast area).
- Productivity is limited by light, water, temperature and **nutrients** (nitrogen and phosphorus on land and in many waters; iron in parts of the open ocean — Southern Ocean iron fertilization experiments).

### Ecological efficiency and the 10% rule
Only ~**10%** (typically 5–20%) of the energy at one trophic level is transferred to the next; the rest is lost as heat in respiration, in uneaten or undigested material, and in wastes. Consequences:
- **Energy pyramids** always narrow upward; food chains rarely exceed 4–5 links.
- Top predators are rare and vulnerable.
- Eating lower on the food chain is more energy-efficient: producing 1 kg of beef requires far more crop calories, land and water than 1 kg of grain or legumes.
- **Biomass pyramids** are usually upright on land but can be inverted in aquatic systems (fast-reproducing phytoplankton support a larger standing biomass of zooplankton). Pyramids of numbers can also be inverted (one tree supports many insects).

### Biomagnification
Persistent, fat-soluble pollutants (DDT, PCBs, methylmercury, PFAS) accumulate in organisms (**bioaccumulation**) and become increasingly concentrated at higher trophic levels (**biomagnification**). DDT concentrations increased millions of times from water to fish-eating birds, causing eggshell thinning and population crashes in bald eagles, peregrine falcons and brown pelicans. Rachel Carson's *Silent Spring* (1962) helped launch the environmental movement; DDT was banned for agricultural use in the US in 1972, and these birds recovered. Mercury in large predatory fish (swordfish, shark, king mackerel) leads to consumption advisories, especially for pregnant women.

## 6. Biogeochemical Cycles

Unlike energy, **matter cycles** through ecosystems.

### The water (hydrologic) cycle
Evaporation (mostly from oceans), transpiration from plants (evapotranspiration), condensation, precipitation, runoff, infiltration and groundwater flow. Driven by solar energy. Over 97% of Earth's water is saline; most fresh water is in ice caps, glaciers and groundwater; less than 1% is readily accessible surface fresh water.

### The carbon cycle
- **Photosynthesis** removes CO₂ from the atmosphere; **cellular respiration**, decomposition and combustion return it.
- **Reservoirs:** atmosphere (~870+ Gt C, rising), oceans (~38 000 Gt, mostly dissolved inorganic carbon), terrestrial vegetation (~450–650 Gt) and soils (~1500–2400 Gt), fossil fuels (~1000+ Gt in recoverable reserves), and sedimentary rocks (limestone — by far the largest, tens of millions of Gt).
- **Human impact:** burning fossil fuels and deforestation add ~10–11 Gt C per year; about half accumulates in the atmosphere, the rest is absorbed by oceans (causing **acidification**) and land. Atmospheric CO₂ has risen from ~280 ppm (pre-industrial) to over 420 ppm, measured continuously at Mauna Loa since 1958 (the Keeling Curve) — the highest in at least 800 000 years (ice-core records) and likely millions of years.
- **Methane** (from wetlands, livestock, rice paddies, fossil fuel leaks, landfills) is a potent greenhouse gas (~80 times CO₂'s warming effect over 20 years; ~28–30 over 100 years).

### The nitrogen cycle
N₂ makes up 78% of the atmosphere but is unusable by most organisms because of its strong triple bond.
- **Nitrogen fixation:** N₂ → NH₃/NH₄⁺, by bacteria with the enzyme **nitrogenase** (free-living *Azotobacter*, cyanobacteria, symbiotic *Rhizobium* in legume root nodules), lightning, and the industrial **Haber–Bosch process** (which now fixes about as much nitrogen as all natural terrestrial processes).
- **Nitrification:** NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ (*Nitrosomonas*) → NO₃⁻ (*Nitrobacter*), by aerobic chemoautotrophic bacteria.
- **Assimilation:** plants take up NO₃⁻ and NH₄⁺ to build amino acids and nucleotides; animals obtain nitrogen by eating.
- **Ammonification (mineralization):** decomposers convert organic nitrogen back to NH₄⁺.
- **Denitrification:** anaerobic bacteria (*Pseudomonas*) reduce NO₃⁻ to N₂ (and N₂O, a greenhouse gas and ozone-depleting substance), returning it to the atmosphere.
- **Anammox:** anaerobic ammonium oxidation (NH₄⁺ + NO₂⁻ → N₂), important in oceans.
- **Human impact:** fertilizer runoff causes **eutrophication** — algal blooms, followed by decomposition that depletes oxygen, creating **hypoxic "dead zones"** (the Gulf of Mexico dead zone, fed by the Mississippi River, often exceeds 15 000 km²; there are over 400 coastal dead zones worldwide).

### The phosphorus cycle
No significant atmospheric component; cycles through rocks, soil, water and organisms. Weathering of phosphate rocks releases PO₄³⁻; plants absorb it; it returns via decomposition; it is lost to ocean sediments and returns to land only over geological time (uplift). Phosphorus is essential for DNA, ATP and phospholipids, and often limits productivity in freshwater lakes. Phosphate rock reserves are finite and concentrated in a few countries (mainly Morocco). Phosphate detergents and fertilizers contributed to eutrophication of lakes (e.g. Lake Erie in the 1960s–70s).

### The sulfur cycle
Volcanoes, weathering, decomposition (H₂S), and burning fossil fuels (SO₂ → acid rain); sulfur is required for the amino acids cysteine and methionine.

## 7. Biodiversity and Conservation

### Why biodiversity matters
- **Ecosystem services:** pollination (~75% of leading food crop types benefit from animal pollination), water purification, flood control, soil formation, climate regulation, carbon storage, pest control, nutrient cycling. Their value has been estimated at tens of trillions of dollars per year — exceeding global GDP.
- **Resources:** food, timber, fibers; medicines — about half of modern drugs are derived from or inspired by natural products (aspirin from willow, morphine from poppies, quinine from cinchona, taxol from Pacific yew, artemisinin from sweet wormwood — Tu Youyou, Nobel 2015 — vincristine from Madagascar periwinkle, penicillin from mold).
- **Genetic resources:** wild relatives of crops provide disease resistance.
- **Ethical, cultural and aesthetic values.**

### Threats to biodiversity ("HIPPO")
1. **Habitat loss and fragmentation** — the leading cause (agriculture, urbanization, logging; ~half of tropical forests have been lost or degraded).
2. **Invasive species** — introduced species without natural enemies: cane toads and rabbits in Australia, brown tree snakes on Guam (which eliminated most native forest birds), zebra mussels in the Great Lakes, kudzu in the US, Nile perch in Lake Victoria, rats on islands, chytrid fungus devastating amphibians worldwide.
3. **Pollution** — pesticides, plastics, nutrient runoff, light and noise.
4. **Population growth and overconsumption** of resources.
5. **Overexploitation** — overfishing (collapse of the Grand Banks cod fishery in 1992), hunting and poaching (passenger pigeon — once perhaps 3–5 billion birds, extinct in 1914; dodo, extinct by ~1681; elephants and rhinos for ivory and horn), the wildlife trade.
Plus **climate change**, increasingly important.

The 2019 IPBES global assessment estimated that ~1 million species face extinction, many within decades. Wildlife populations monitored by the Living Planet Index declined by an average of ~73% between 1970 and 2020.

### Climate change and ecosystems
Global average surface temperature has risen ~1.2–1.3 °C above pre-industrial levels, primarily due to greenhouse gas emissions. Ecological effects: species shifting ranges poleward and upslope; earlier springs causing **phenological mismatches** (e.g. birds arriving after peak insect abundance); **coral bleaching** (corals expel symbiotic algae under heat stress; mass bleaching events on the Great Barrier Reef in 1998, 2002, 2016, 2017, 2020, 2022, 2024); sea-ice loss threatening polar bears; ocean acidification impairing shell formation; increased wildfires and droughts; thawing permafrost releasing carbon (a positive feedback).

### Conservation strategies
- **Protected areas** (national parks, marine protected areas). The 2022 Kunming–Montreal Global Biodiversity Framework set the "30×30" target: protect 30% of land and sea by 2030.
- **Habitat corridors** connecting fragments; **restoration ecology** (reforestation, wetland and river restoration, rewilding).
- **Species-level conservation:** endangered species laws (US Endangered Species Act, 1973; CITES treaty regulating international trade, 1975), captive breeding and reintroduction (California condor — down to 27 individuals in 1987, now over 500; Arabian oryx; black-footed ferret), seed banks (Svalbard Global Seed Vault).
- **Conservation genetics:** maintaining genetic diversity in small populations to avoid inbreeding depression (Florida panthers were rescued in 1995 by introducing Texas pumas).
- **Sustainable use:** sustainable fisheries (maximum sustainable yield, catch quotas), agroforestry, certification schemes.
- **Success stories:** recovery of the ozone layer after the Montreal Protocol; humpback whale populations rebounding after the commercial whaling moratorium (1986); bald eagles removed from the US endangered list (2007); giant panda downgraded from "endangered" to "vulnerable" (2016).
