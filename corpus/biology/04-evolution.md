# Evolution: Natural Selection, Speciation and the History of Life

> "Nothing in biology makes sense except in the light of evolution." — Theodosius Dobzhansky (1973)

**Biological evolution** is the change in the heritable characteristics of populations over successive generations. Evolution explains both the **unity** of life (all organisms share a common ancestry, a genetic code and core biochemistry) and its **diversity** (millions of species adapted to every environment on Earth). It is supported by overwhelming evidence from paleontology, comparative anatomy, molecular biology, biogeography, and direct observation.

## 1. Historical Development

- **Ancient ideas:** Anaximander speculated about life arising from water; Aristotle arranged organisms on a "scale of nature" (*scala naturae*) but regarded species as fixed.
- **Carl Linnaeus** (1735): binomial nomenclature and hierarchical classification (*Homo sapiens*) — grouping by similarity, later understood as reflecting common descent.
- **Georges Cuvier** (early 1800s): founded paleontology; demonstrated extinction (mammoths, mastodons) but explained successive faunas by catastrophes.
- **James Hutton** and **Charles Lyell:** **uniformitarianism** — geological processes acting gradually over immense time shaped Earth. Lyell's *Principles of Geology* influenced Darwin deeply. Deep time made evolution possible.
- **Jean-Baptiste Lamarck** (1809): first comprehensive theory of evolution — species change over time — but proposed an incorrect mechanism: **inheritance of acquired characteristics** (e.g. giraffes stretching their necks and passing longer necks to offspring).
- **Thomas Malthus** (1798): populations grow faster than food supplies, leading to a struggle for existence — a key inspiration for both Darwin and Wallace.
- **Charles Darwin** (1809–1882): naturalist on HMS *Beagle* (1831–1836); observed fossils in South America, and variation among Galápagos mockingbirds, tortoises and finches. Developed natural selection by 1838 but delayed publication for two decades.
- **Alfred Russel Wallace** (1823–1913): independently conceived natural selection in 1858 while in the Malay Archipelago and wrote to Darwin. Their papers were presented jointly at the Linnean Society on 1 July 1858.
- **1859:** Darwin published *On the Origin of Species by Means of Natural Selection*.
- **1930s–1940s — the Modern Synthesis:** integrated Darwinian selection with Mendelian genetics and population genetics (Fisher, Haldane, Wright, Dobzhansky, Mayr, Simpson, Huxley).
- **Since the 1960s:** molecular evolution (Kimura's neutral theory), DNA sequencing, evolutionary developmental biology ("evo-devo"), and genomics.

## 2. Natural Selection

### Darwin's argument (observations and inferences)
1. **Overproduction:** organisms produce more offspring than can survive (a single cod releases millions of eggs; even slow-breeding elephants would, unchecked, multiply to vast numbers).
2. **Limited resources** → competition (struggle for existence).
3. **Variation:** individuals in a population vary in their traits.
4. **Heritability:** much of this variation is inherited.
5. **Inference — differential survival and reproduction:** individuals with heritable traits better suited to their environment tend to leave more offspring.
6. **Inference — accumulation:** over generations, favorable traits become more common in the population — **adaptation**.

### Key concepts
- **Fitness:** an individual's relative contribution of offspring to the next generation (reproductive success), not strength. "Survival of the fittest" (Herbert Spencer's phrase) means survival and reproduction of the best-adapted.
- **Adaptation:** a heritable trait that increases fitness in a particular environment (camouflage, antibiotic resistance, the eye, echolocation).
- Selection acts on **individuals' phenotypes**, but **populations evolve** (allele frequencies change).
- Selection is not goal-directed and does not produce perfection: it works with existing variation and is constrained by history, trade-offs and genetics. The vertebrate eye has a "blind spot" because its photoreceptors face away from the light (the octopus eye, evolved independently, does not).
- Natural selection is **not random** (it consistently favors certain variants), although mutation (the source of variation) is random with respect to need.

### Types of selection
- **Directional selection:** favors one extreme (larger beaks in drought years; antibiotic resistance; peppered moths during industrialization).
- **Stabilizing selection:** favors intermediate values and reduces variation (human birth weight historically ~3–4 kg).
- **Disruptive (diversifying) selection:** favors both extremes (African seedcracker finches with large or small beaks, depending on seed types).
- **Balancing selection:** maintains multiple alleles — **heterozygote advantage** (sickle-cell allele in malarial regions) and **frequency-dependent selection** (left- and right-mouthed scale-eating cichlids).
- **Sexual selection:** traits that increase mating success — **intrasexual** competition (antlers, large body size in male elephant seals) and **intersexual** mate choice (peacock tails, bird songs). Can produce elaborate traits that reduce survival (the "handicap principle").

### Classic examples of selection observed
- **Peppered moth** (*Biston betularia*) in England: dark (melanic) forms rose from rare to ~98% near Manchester by the late 1800s as soot darkened trees (bird predation favored camouflage), then declined after clean-air laws. The causative mutation — a transposable element insertion in the *cortex* gene — was identified in 2016.
- **Darwin's finches** on Daphne Major (Galápagos), studied by Peter and Rosemary Grant since 1973: after a 1977 drought, only large, hard seeds remained; surviving finches had beaks ~4% deeper on average, and their offspring inherited deeper beaks — evolution measured in a single generation. The *ALX1* and *HMGA2* genes influence beak shape and size.
- **Antibiotic resistance:** bacteria rapidly evolve resistance through mutations and horizontal gene transfer (plasmids carrying resistance genes). MRSA, multidrug-resistant tuberculosis, and carbapenem-resistant Enterobacteriaceae are major threats; antimicrobial resistance was associated with ~5 million deaths in 2019.
- **Pesticide and herbicide resistance** in insects and weeds; **HIV drug resistance** (combination therapy reduces it); **cancer** evolving resistance to chemotherapy.
- **Lenski's long-term evolution experiment** (started 1988): 12 populations of *E. coli* propagated for over 75 000 generations; one population evolved the ability to use citrate aerobically after ~31 000 generations.
- **Lactase persistence** in human populations with dairying traditions; **high-altitude adaptation** in Tibetans (*EPAS1* allele, acquired from Denisovans).
- **Cane toads** in Australia evolving longer legs at the invasion front; **Italian wall lizards** introduced to a new island evolving larger heads and new gut structures within ~36 years.

### Artificial selection
Humans have bred plants and animals for desired traits for ~10 000 years — Darwin's opening argument in *Origin*. Dogs (all descended from wolves, domesticated >15 000 years ago) range from Chihuahuas to Great Danes; wild mustard (*Brassica oleracea*) was bred into cabbage, broccoli, cauliflower, kale, kohlrabi and Brussels sprouts; maize descends from the wild grass teosinte; Belyaev's silver foxes, selected for tameness from 1959, developed floppy ears and spotted coats within decades.

## 3. Evidence for Evolution

### 3.1 The fossil record
- Fossils appear in a consistent order in rock layers (**law of superposition**): bacteria (~3.5 billion years) → simple eukaryotes → multicellular life → fish → amphibians → reptiles → mammals and birds. No fossil rabbits in the Precambrian.
- **Radiometric dating** (U–Pb, K–Ar, ⁴⁰Ar/³⁹Ar, ¹⁴C for recent remains) provides absolute ages. Earth is ~4.54 billion years old.
- **Transitional fossils** document major transitions:
  - **Tiktaalik** (~375 million years ago): fish–tetrapod intermediate with gills, scales and fins but also lungs, a neck and limb-like fins with wrist bones — discovered in 2004 in Arctic Canada after researchers predicted where rocks of the right age and environment would be found.
  - **Archaeopteryx** (~150 Mya; discovered 1861): feathers and wings plus teeth, clawed fingers and a long bony tail — linking dinosaurs and birds. Numerous feathered dinosaurs from China (since 1996) confirm birds are living theropod dinosaurs.
  - **Whale evolution:** *Pakicetus* (~50 Mya, land-dwelling), *Ambulocetus* ("walking whale"), *Rodhocetus*, *Basilosaurus* (fully aquatic with tiny hind limbs). Modern whales occasionally have vestigial pelvic bones; molecular data place whales within the even-toed ungulates, closest to hippos.
  - **Horse evolution** from small, multi-toed *Eohippus* (*Hyracotherium*) ~55 Mya to single-toed *Equus*, with a branching (not linear) history.
  - **Human evolution:** *Sahelanthropus*, *Ardipithecus*, *Australopithecus* (e.g. "Lucy", *A. afarensis*, 3.2 Mya, bipedal with a small brain), *Homo habilis*, *H. erectus*, Neanderthals, *H. sapiens*.
  - **Synapsid-to-mammal transition:** a detailed series showing jaw bones migrating to become the middle ear bones (malleus and incus), also seen in mammalian embryonic development.

### 3.2 Comparative anatomy
- **Homologous structures:** same underlying structure from common ancestry, modified for different functions. The **pentadactyl limb** of humans (arm), cats (leg), whales (flipper) and bats (wing) has the same bones (humerus, radius, ulna, carpals, metacarpals, phalanges).
- **Analogous structures:** similar function, different origin — **convergent evolution** (wings of birds, bats and insects; streamlined bodies of sharks, dolphins and extinct ichthyosaurs; camera eyes of vertebrates and octopuses; succulent stems of cacti and euphorbias).
- **Vestigial structures:** reduced remnants with little or no original function: human coccyx (tailbone), wisdom teeth, plica semilunaris (remnant of a nictitating membrane), arrector pili muscles (goosebumps), the ability of some people to wiggle their ears; whale and python pelvic bones; flightless birds' wings; eyeless cave fish with eye remnants.

### 3.3 Embryology and evo-devo
Vertebrate embryos share features such as **pharyngeal arches** (becoming gills in fish; jaw, ear and throat structures in mammals) and a post-anal tail. (Ernst Haeckel's exaggerated drawings and "recapitulation" idea are outdated, but embryonic similarities are real.) **Hox genes** — conserved master regulators of body plans — are shared from fruit flies to humans (Lewis, Nüsslein-Volhard and Wieschaus, Nobel 1995). The *Pax6* gene controls eye development in both insects and mammals; the mouse gene can induce eyes in flies.

### 3.4 Molecular biology and genomics
- **Universal genetic code** and shared biochemistry (DNA, ATP, ribosomes, the same 20 amino acids) point to a single common ancestor (**LUCA**, the last universal common ancestor, ~4 billion years ago).
- **Sequence similarity reflects relatedness:** humans and chimpanzees share ~98–99% of DNA sequence in aligned regions; cytochrome c differs between humans and chimps by 0 amino acids, rhesus monkeys by 1, dogs by ~11, yeast by ~44.
- **Molecular phylogenies** built from independent genes largely agree with each other and with morphology-based trees.
- **Pseudogenes** shared in the same positions: humans and other primates share a broken *GULO* gene (vitamin C synthesis), explaining why we need dietary vitamin C — the same inactivating mutations appear in our relatives.
- **Endogenous retroviruses** (ERVs): remnants of ancient viral infections at identical genomic positions in humans and chimpanzees — virtually impossible except by common descent. ~8% of the human genome is retroviral in origin.
- **Human chromosome 2** is the fusion of two ancestral ape chromosomes.

### 3.5 Biogeography
- **Island species** resemble those of the nearest mainland: Galápagos species resemble South American ones; Cape Verde species resemble African ones — despite similar climates.
- **Adaptive radiation** on isolated islands: Darwin's finches (~18 species), Hawaiian honeycreepers, Madagascar's lemurs, Lake Victoria's ~500 cichlid fish species (many within the last ~15 000 years).
- **Continental drift** explains distributions: marsupials diversified in Australia after its isolation; ratite birds (ostriches, emus, rheas, kiwis) and the *Glossopteris* fern flora are distributed across former Gondwana fragments.
- Oceanic islands lack native land mammals (except bats) and amphibians, which cannot cross seawater — but introduced species thrive there.

### 3.6 Direct observation
Evolution observed in real time: the examples in Section 2 (finches, peppered moths, bacteria, viruses — the yearly evolution of influenza requiring new vaccines, and SARS-CoV-2 variants), plus speciation events observed in plants (polyploid *Tragopogon* species in the US Pacific Northwest arose in the 20th century).

## 4. Mechanisms of Evolution

| Mechanism | Effect | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Mutation** | Introduces new alleles | Ultimate source of variation; random with respect to fitness |
| **Natural selection** | Increases frequency of beneficial alleles | Only mechanism that consistently produces adaptation |
| **Genetic drift** | Random changes in frequency | Strong in small populations; can fix or eliminate alleles |
| **Gene flow** | Transfers alleles between populations | Homogenizes populations |
| **Non-random mating** | Changes genotype frequencies | Inbreeding increases homozygosity |
| **Recombination** | New combinations of existing alleles | Sexual reproduction |
| **Horizontal gene transfer** | Genes move between species | Common in bacteria (plasmids, transduction, transformation) |

**Neutral theory** (Motoo Kimura, 1968): most molecular variation is selectively neutral and governed by drift and mutation; adaptive evolution occurs at a subset of sites. This underlies the **molecular clock** — neutral mutations accumulate at roughly constant rates, allowing estimates of divergence times when calibrated with fossils (e.g. human–chimpanzee divergence ~6–7 million years ago).

## 5. Speciation

### Species concepts
- **Biological species concept** (Ernst Mayr, 1942): a species is a group of actually or potentially interbreeding natural populations that are reproductively isolated from other such groups. (Limitations: doesn't apply to asexual organisms or fossils; hybridization blurs boundaries.)
- Others: morphological, phylogenetic (smallest monophyletic group), ecological species concepts.
- Roughly 2 million species have been described; estimates of the total range from ~8.7 million eukaryotic species to vastly more if microbes are included.

### Reproductive isolating mechanisms
**Prezygotic barriers** (prevent mating or fertilization):
- **Habitat isolation:** different habitats in the same area.
- **Temporal isolation:** breeding at different times (seasons, times of day).
- **Behavioral isolation:** courtship rituals and signals (bird songs, firefly flash patterns).
- **Mechanical isolation:** incompatible reproductive structures.
- **Gametic isolation:** sperm and egg incompatible (sea urchin bindin proteins).

**Postzygotic barriers** (hybrids fail):
- **Reduced hybrid viability.**
- **Reduced hybrid fertility:** the mule (horse 64 chromosomes × donkey 62 → mule 63) is usually sterile because chromosomes cannot pair properly in meiosis.
- **Hybrid breakdown:** later-generation hybrids are weak or sterile.

### Modes of speciation
- **Allopatric speciation** (most common): a population is divided by a geographic barrier (mountain range, river, sea-level change, continental drift); isolated populations diverge through selection, drift and mutation until reproductive isolation evolves. Example: snapping shrimp on either side of the Isthmus of Panama (formed ~3 million years ago) — 15 pairs of sister species, each pair mostly unable to interbreed. **Peripatric** speciation occurs in small, peripheral founder populations.
- **Sympatric speciation:** without geographic separation:
  - **Polyploidy** (instant speciation, common in plants: ~15% of angiosperm speciation events). Bread wheat is an allohexaploid hybrid of three grass species.
  - **Habitat differentiation:** apple maggot flies (*Rhagoletis pomonella*) shifting from hawthorns to introduced apples in the 1800s, now partially isolated by timing.
  - **Sexual selection:** cichlids in African lakes with female preference for male color.
- **Parapatric speciation:** across an environmental gradient with a narrow hybrid zone (grasses tolerant of heavy metals near mine tailings).

### Tempo
- **Gradualism:** slow, steady change.
- **Punctuated equilibrium** (Niles Eldredge and Stephen Jay Gould, 1972): long periods of stasis interrupted by relatively rapid speciation (geologically rapid — tens of thousands of years).
Both patterns occur.

### Patterns of evolution
- **Divergent evolution and adaptive radiation:** rapid diversification into many ecological niches (mammals after the extinction of non-avian dinosaurs).
- **Convergent evolution:** unrelated lineages evolve similar traits under similar pressures (C₄ photosynthesis evolved over 60 times; echolocation in bats and dolphins; "carcinization" — crab-like forms evolved at least five times among decapods).
- **Coevolution:** reciprocal evolutionary change between interacting species — flowers and pollinators (Darwin predicted a moth with a 30 cm proboscis to pollinate the Madagascar orchid *Angraecum sesquipedale*; it was found in 1903), predator–prey arms races (garter snakes evolving resistance to newt tetrodotoxin), hosts and parasites (the **Red Queen hypothesis** — species must keep evolving just to maintain fitness; a proposed explanation for the prevalence of sex).

## 6. Phylogenetics: Reconstructing Evolutionary History

- **Phylogenetic trees** depict evolutionary relationships; branch points (nodes) represent common ancestors.
- **Cladistics** groups organisms by **shared derived characters** (synapomorphies). A **clade** (monophyletic group) includes an ancestor and **all** its descendants. "Reptiles" excluding birds is **paraphyletic**; "warm-blooded animals" (birds + mammals) is **polyphyletic**.
- Birds are dinosaurs; humans are apes; whales are artiodactyls; fungi are more closely related to animals than to plants.
- Molecular data (rRNA, whole genomes) revolutionized phylogenetics, revealing the three domains and the extent of horizontal gene transfer among microbes.
- **Parsimony, maximum likelihood and Bayesian** methods infer trees from character data.
- **Taxonomic hierarchy:** Domain, Kingdom, Phylum, Class, Order, Family, Genus, Species ("Dear King Philip Came Over For Good Soup"). Humans: Eukarya, Animalia, Chordata, Mammalia, Primates, Hominidae, *Homo*, *sapiens*.

**The sixth mass extinction:** current extinction rates are estimated at tens to hundreds of times background rates, driven by habitat loss, overexploitation, invasive species, pollution and climate change.

## 8. Origin of Life (Abiogenesis)

- **Oparin–Haldane hypothesis** (1920s): organic molecules formed in a reducing early atmosphere ("primordial soup").
- **Miller–Urey experiment** (1952–1953): electrical sparks through a mixture of CH₄, NH₃, H₂ and H₂O produced amino acids. (The early atmosphere is now thought less reducing, but similar syntheses work under various conditions, and amino acids are found in meteorites such as Murchison.)
- **RNA world hypothesis:** RNA can store information and catalyze reactions (ribozymes), so it may have preceded DNA and proteins. The ribosome's catalytic core is RNA — a "molecular fossil".
- **Hydrothermal vents** (especially alkaline vents) offer natural proton gradients and mineral catalysts.
- **Protocells:** lipid vesicles form spontaneously and can grow and divide; clay minerals catalyze RNA polymerization.
- **Panspermia** (life arriving from space) relocates rather than solves the problem.
- How life began remains one of science's great open questions.

## 9. Human Evolution

- Humans are **great apes** (family Hominidae, with chimpanzees, bonobos, gorillas and orangutans); our closest living relatives are chimpanzees and bonobos.
- **Hominins** (the human lineage after splitting from chimpanzees): *Sahelanthropus tchadensis* (~7 Mya), *Ardipithecus ramidus* (4.4 Mya), *Australopithecus afarensis* ("Lucy"; Laetoli footprints, 3.66 Mya — clear bipedalism long before large brains), *Paranthropus* (robust jaws), *Homo habilis* (~2.4 Mya; early stone tools — though tools date to at least 3.3 Mya), *Homo erectus* (~1.9 Mya; first to leave Africa; controlled fire), *Homo heidelbergensis*, Neanderthals (*H. neanderthalensis*, ~400–40 ka, Europe and Western Asia), Denisovans (known mainly from DNA), *Homo floresiensis* ("hobbit", Flores, Indonesia), *Homo naledi* (South Africa).
- **Out of Africa:** genetic and fossil evidence show *H. sapiens* originated in Africa ~300 000 years ago and dispersed globally. Africa harbors the greatest human genetic diversity.
- **Interbreeding:** non-African populations carry ~1–2% Neanderthal DNA; Melanesians and Aboriginal Australians carry ~3–6% Denisovan DNA (Svante Pääbo sequenced the Neanderthal genome; Nobel 2022).
- **Key trends:** bipedalism (freeing hands; adaptations of pelvis, spine, foot), increasing brain size (~450 cm³ in australopiths to ~1350 cm³ in modern humans), tool use, language, culture, and prolonged childhood.
- **Human genetic variation** is small compared with other apes (two chimpanzees from different groups can differ more than two humans from different continents); most variation (~85–90%) is within populations rather than between them, undermining biological concepts of race.

## 10. Common Misconceptions

1. **"Evolution is just a theory."** In science, a theory is a well-substantiated explanation supported by extensive evidence (like the theory of gravity or atomic theory). Evolution is both an observed fact (populations change; common descent is established) and a theory explaining how (natural selection, drift, etc.).
2. **"Humans evolved from chimpanzees."** No — humans and chimpanzees share a common ancestor that lived ~6–7 million years ago.
3. **"Evolution is random."** Mutation is random with respect to fitness, but selection is not.
4. **"Individuals evolve."** Populations evolve; individuals develop.
5. **"Evolution strives for perfection or progress."** Adaptations are compromises; evolution has no goal. Simpler forms (bacteria) are spectacularly successful.
6. **"Organisms evolve because they need to."** Variation arises regardless of need; selection acts on what is available (contra Lamarck).
7. **"There are no transitional fossils."** There are many (Tiktaalik, Archaeopteryx, whale and hominin series).
8. **"The eye is too complex to evolve."** Darwin addressed this: functional intermediates exist in living animals — light-sensitive patches, cup eyes, pinhole eyes (nautilus), lens eyes — each useful. Models suggest a camera eye could evolve in a few hundred thousand generations.
9. **"Evolution violates the second law of thermodynamics."** Living systems are open systems powered by the Sun; local decreases in entropy are offset by larger increases elsewhere.
