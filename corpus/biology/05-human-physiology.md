# Human Physiology: Organ Systems and Homeostasis

**Physiology** is the study of how living organisms function — how cells, tissues, organs and systems work together to keep the body alive. The human body consists of ~37 trillion cells organized into four basic tissue types (epithelial, connective, muscle and nervous), which form organs, which in turn form organ systems.

## 1. Homeostasis

**Homeostasis** (Walter Cannon, 1926, building on Claude Bernard's *milieu intérieur*) is the maintenance of a relatively stable internal environment despite external changes.

| Variable | Normal range (approximate) |
|---|---|
| Core body temperature | 36.5–37.5 °C |
| Blood pH | 7.35–7.45 |
| Fasting blood glucose | 3.9–5.6 mmol/L (70–100 mg/dL) |
| Plasma osmolarity | 275–295 mOsm/kg |
| Arterial blood pressure | ~120/80 mmHg |
| Plasma Na⁺ | 135–145 mmol/L |
| Plasma K⁺ | 3.5–5.0 mmol/L |
| Plasma Ca²⁺ (total) | 2.2–2.6 mmol/L |
| Arterial PO₂ / PCO₂ | ~95–100 / ~40 mmHg |

### Feedback mechanisms
A control system has a **sensor** (receptor), a **control center** (often the brain or an endocrine gland, which compares input to a set point) and an **effector**.
- **Negative feedback** (most common) counteracts deviations: when body temperature rises, the hypothalamus triggers sweating and skin vasodilation; when it falls, shivering and vasoconstriction. Blood glucose regulation by insulin and glucagon is another example.
- **Positive feedback** amplifies a change toward a decisive endpoint: uterine contractions during childbirth (oxytocin), blood clotting cascade, the LH surge triggering ovulation, the upstroke of an action potential.

Failure of homeostasis causes disease: diabetes (glucose), hypertension, dehydration, fever beyond ~41 °C, acidosis.

## 2. The Nervous System

### Organization
- **Central nervous system (CNS):** brain and spinal cord.
- **Peripheral nervous system (PNS):** cranial and spinal nerves.
  - **Somatic** (voluntary control of skeletal muscles; sensory input).
  - **Autonomic** (involuntary control of smooth muscle, cardiac muscle, glands):
    - **Sympathetic** ("fight or flight"): increases heart rate, dilates pupils and bronchioles, mobilizes glucose, inhibits digestion; neurotransmitter norepinephrine at most targets; adrenal medulla releases epinephrine.
    - **Parasympathetic** ("rest and digest"): slows heart, stimulates digestion; neurotransmitter acetylcholine; vagus nerve carries ~75% of parasympathetic fibers.
  - **Enteric nervous system:** ~500 million neurons in the gut wall ("second brain").

### Neurons
- **Cell body (soma)**, **dendrites** (receive input), **axon** (conducts output, from millimeters to over a meter long), **axon terminals** (release neurotransmitters).
- The human brain contains ~86 billion neurons and a similar number of glial cells, with ~100–1000 trillion synapses.
- **Glial cells:** astrocytes (support, blood–brain barrier, neurotransmitter recycling), oligodendrocytes (CNS myelin), Schwann cells (PNS myelin), microglia (immune defense), ependymal cells (cerebrospinal fluid).
- **Myelin** insulates axons, enabling **saltatory conduction** — impulses jump between **nodes of Ranvier** — at up to ~120 m/s (vs. ~0.5–2 m/s in unmyelinated fibers). **Multiple sclerosis** is autoimmune destruction of CNS myelin.

### Resting membrane potential
About **−70 mV** (inside negative), maintained by:
- The **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** (3 Na⁺ out, 2 K⁺ in per ATP), creating gradients (K⁺ high inside; Na⁺ high outside).
- The membrane's much higher permeability to K⁺ at rest (leak channels), so the potential lies near the K⁺ equilibrium potential (~−90 mV), given by the Nernst equation.

### The action potential
An all-or-none electrical impulse (Hodgkin and Huxley, squid giant axon, 1952; Nobel 1963):
1. **Depolarization to threshold** (~−55 mV) by a stimulus.
2. **Rising phase:** voltage-gated **Na⁺ channels** open; Na⁺ rushes in; the membrane potential rises to ~+30 to +40 mV (positive feedback).
3. **Falling phase (repolarization):** Na⁺ channels inactivate; voltage-gated **K⁺ channels** open; K⁺ flows out.
4. **Hyperpolarization (undershoot):** K⁺ channels close slowly; potential briefly dips below rest.
5. **Refractory period:** absolute (Na⁺ channels inactivated — no new AP possible) then relative; ensures one-way propagation and limits firing frequency (up to ~1000 Hz).
The **amplitude** is constant; stimulus intensity is encoded by **frequency** of firing and number of neurons recruited.
- **Tetrodotoxin** (pufferfish) blocks Na⁺ channels; **local anesthetics** (lidocaine) also block them.

### Synaptic transmission
At a **chemical synapse**:
1. The action potential arrives at the axon terminal.
2. Voltage-gated **Ca²⁺ channels** open; Ca²⁺ enters.
3. Synaptic vesicles fuse with the membrane (SNARE proteins) and release **neurotransmitter** into the synaptic cleft (~20–40 nm).
4. Neurotransmitters bind receptors on the postsynaptic membrane: **ionotropic** (ligand-gated ion channels; fast) or **metabotropic** (GPCRs; slower, longer-lasting).
5. **Excitatory postsynaptic potentials (EPSPs)** depolarize; **inhibitory (IPSPs)** hyperpolarize. The postsynaptic neuron integrates thousands of inputs (spatial and temporal summation) at the axon hillock.
6. Signal termination by enzymatic breakdown (acetylcholinesterase), reuptake (transporters — the targets of SSRIs and cocaine), or diffusion.
**Electrical synapses** (gap junctions) transmit directly and very fast.

| Neurotransmitter | Main effects / roles | Related drugs and disorders |
|---|---|---|
| Acetylcholine | Neuromuscular junction, parasympathetic, memory | Myasthenia gravis; botulinum toxin blocks release; curare blocks receptors; Alzheimer's drugs inhibit acetylcholinesterase |
| Glutamate | Main excitatory transmitter in CNS; learning (LTP) | Excitotoxicity in stroke; ketamine (NMDA antagonist) |
| GABA | Main inhibitory transmitter in brain | Benzodiazepines, barbiturates, alcohol enhance GABA_A |
| Glycine | Inhibitory in spinal cord | Strychnine blocks receptors |
| Dopamine | Reward, motivation, movement | Parkinson's disease (loss of substantia nigra neurons; L-DOPA), schizophrenia (antipsychotics block D2), addiction |
| Serotonin (5-HT) | Mood, sleep, appetite | Depression (SSRIs like fluoxetine), LSD and psilocybin act at 5-HT2A |
| Norepinephrine | Alertness, sympathetic responses | ADHD drugs, SNRIs |
| Endorphins | Natural pain relief | Opioids mimic them |

### The brain
- **Cerebrum** (cerebral cortex, ~2–4 mm thick, folded to increase surface area): **frontal lobe** (planning, decision-making, personality, motor cortex, Broca's area for speech production), **parietal lobe** (somatosensory cortex, spatial awareness), **temporal lobe** (hearing, Wernicke's area for language comprehension, memory with the hippocampus), **occipital lobe** (vision). The two hemispheres are connected by the **corpus callosum**; the left hemisphere controls the right side of the body and vice versa.
- **Limbic system:** hippocampus (forming new memories — patient H.M. lost this ability after its removal in 1953), amygdala (fear and emotion).
- **Thalamus:** relay station for sensory information.
- **Hypothalamus:** homeostatic control center (temperature, hunger, thirst, circadian rhythms, controls the pituitary).
- **Cerebellum:** coordination, balance, motor learning (contains more than half of the brain's neurons).
- **Brainstem** (midbrain, pons, medulla oblongata): vital functions — breathing, heart rate, blood pressure, reflexes like swallowing and coughing.
- The brain is ~2% of body mass but uses ~20% of resting energy (~20 W).

**Reflex arc:** sensory receptor → sensory neuron → interneuron (spinal cord) → motor neuron → effector. The knee-jerk (patellar) reflex is monosynaptic.

### Senses
- **Vision:** cornea and lens focus light on the retina; **rods** (~120 million; dim light, black and white; rhodopsin) and **cones** (~6 million; color; three types sensitive to short, medium and long wavelengths). The fovea has the highest cone density.
- **Hearing:** sound waves → eardrum → ossicles (malleus, incus, stapes, amplifying pressure ~20×) → cochlea, where hair cells on the basilar membrane transduce vibrations (high frequencies at the base, low at the apex). Range ~20 Hz–20 kHz.
- **Balance:** vestibular system (semicircular canals for rotation; utricle and saccule for linear acceleration and gravity).
- **Taste:** sweet, sour, salty, bitter, umami (detected by taste receptor cells; much of "flavor" is smell).
- **Smell:** ~400 functional olfactory receptor genes (GPCRs) in humans (Axel and Buck, Nobel 2004).
- **Touch, temperature, pain** (TRPV1 detects heat and capsaicin; TRPM8 cold and menthol; Julius and Patapoutian, Nobel 2021), **proprioception** (muscle spindles; PIEZO2 mechanoreceptors).

## 3. The Endocrine System

Endocrine glands secrete **hormones** into the blood, acting on target cells with specific receptors. Compared with nerves, hormonal signaling is slower and longer-lasting.

**Classes:**
- **Peptide/protein hormones** (insulin, glucagon, growth hormone, ADH, oxytocin): water-soluble; bind cell-surface receptors; act via second messengers.
- **Steroid hormones** (cortisol, aldosterone, testosterone, estrogen, progesterone): lipid-soluble; cross membranes; bind intracellular receptors acting as transcription factors.
- **Amine hormones** (epinephrine — water-soluble; thyroid hormones T₃/T₄ — lipid-soluble, nuclear receptors).

| Gland | Hormone | Main action |
|---|---|---|
| Hypothalamus | Releasing/inhibiting hormones (TRH, CRH, GnRH, GHRH, somatostatin, dopamine) | Control anterior pituitary |
| Posterior pituitary (stores hypothalamic hormones) | ADH (vasopressin) | Water reabsorption in kidneys |
| | Oxytocin | Uterine contraction, milk ejection, social bonding |
| Anterior pituitary | GH (growth hormone) | Growth, metabolism (via IGF-1); excess → gigantism/acromegaly |
| | TSH | Stimulates thyroid |
| | ACTH | Stimulates adrenal cortex |
| | FSH, LH | Gamete production, sex hormones |
| | Prolactin | Milk production |
| Thyroid | T₃, T₄ (contain iodine) | Metabolic rate, development (deficiency → goiter, cretinism) |
| | Calcitonin | Lowers blood Ca²⁺ (minor in humans) |
| Parathyroids | PTH | Raises blood Ca²⁺ |
| Adrenal cortex | Cortisol | Stress response; raises glucose; suppresses immunity |
| | Aldosterone | Na⁺ retention, K⁺ excretion |
| Adrenal medulla | Epinephrine, norepinephrine | Fight-or-flight |
| Pancreas (islets of Langerhans) | Insulin (β cells) | Lowers blood glucose |
| | Glucagon (α cells) | Raises blood glucose |
| Gonads | Testosterone (testes); estrogen, progesterone (ovaries) | Reproductive function, secondary sex characteristics |
| Pineal | Melatonin | Circadian rhythms, sleep |
| Others | Leptin (fat), ghrelin (stomach), EPO (kidney), ANP (heart), GLP-1 (intestine) | Appetite, red cell production, blood volume, insulin secretion |

**Hypothalamic–pituitary axes** operate with **negative feedback**: e.g. TRH → TSH → T₃/T₄, which inhibit TRH and TSH secretion.

**Blood glucose regulation:** after a meal, rising glucose stimulates **insulin**, which promotes glucose uptake (via GLUT4 in muscle and fat), glycogen synthesis and fat storage. During fasting, **glucagon** stimulates glycogenolysis and gluconeogenesis in the liver. **Diabetes mellitus:** type 1 (autoimmune β-cell destruction; insulin required; ~5–10% of cases) and type 2 (insulin resistance and relative deficiency; associated with obesity; ~90% of cases). Over 500 million adults worldwide have diabetes.

## 4. The Cardiovascular System

### The heart
A four-chambered muscular pump (~300 g) beating ~100 000 times per day.
- **Pulmonary circuit:** right atrium → right ventricle → pulmonary arteries → lungs (blood oxygenated) → pulmonary veins → left atrium.
- **Systemic circuit:** left atrium → left ventricle → aorta → body → venae cavae → right atrium.
- **Valves** ensure one-way flow: tricuspid and mitral (bicuspid) atrioventricular valves; pulmonary and aortic semilunar valves. Their closing produces the "lub-dub" heart sounds.
- The left ventricle's wall is thickest, generating systemic pressure.
- Arteries carry blood **away** from the heart (usually oxygenated, except pulmonary arteries); veins carry blood **toward** the heart.

### Electrical conduction
The heartbeat is **myogenic** (initiated within the heart):
**SA node** (natural pacemaker, ~60–100 beats/min) → atria contract → **AV node** (delay ~0.1 s, letting ventricles fill) → **bundle of His** → bundle branches → **Purkinje fibers** → ventricles contract from apex upward.
The **electrocardiogram (ECG)**: P wave (atrial depolarization), QRS complex (ventricular depolarization), T wave (ventricular repolarization). The autonomic nervous system modulates the rate.

### Cardiac output
$$\text{CO} = \text{heart rate}\times\text{stroke volume} \approx 70\text{ beats/min}\times70\text{ mL} \approx 5\text{ L/min at rest}$$
(up to ~20–35 L/min in trained athletes during exercise). The **Frank–Starling law:** greater filling stretches the ventricle and increases stroke volume.

### Blood vessels and blood pressure
- **Arteries:** thick, elastic walls; high pressure. **Arterioles** regulate flow and resistance. **Capillaries:** one cell thick; exchange of gases, nutrients and wastes; total length ~40 000–100 000 km. **Venules and veins:** low pressure; valves prevent backflow; skeletal muscle pump assists return. ~60–70% of blood volume is in veins at any time.
- **Blood pressure:** systolic/diastolic (e.g. 120/80 mmHg). Mean arterial pressure = CO × total peripheral resistance. **Hypertension** (≥130/80 or ≥140/90 depending on guidelines) affects over a billion people and increases risk of stroke, heart attack and kidney disease.
- Regulation: baroreceptor reflex (aortic arch, carotid sinus), renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system, ADH, ANP.

### Blood
~5 L in an adult (~7–8% of body mass).
- **Plasma** (~55%): water, proteins (albumin — osmotic pressure; globulins — antibodies; fibrinogen — clotting), electrolytes, nutrients, hormones, wastes.
- **Red blood cells (erythrocytes)** (~45% = **hematocrit**): biconcave discs, no nucleus or mitochondria in mammals, packed with hemoglobin (~270 million molecules each); ~5 million per μL; live ~120 days; produced in bone marrow (stimulated by EPO). ~2.4 million made every second.
- **White blood cells (leukocytes)** (~4500–11 000/μL): neutrophils (most abundant; phagocytes), lymphocytes (B, T, NK cells), monocytes (become macrophages), eosinophils (parasites, allergy), basophils (histamine).
- **Platelets (thrombocytes)** (~150 000–400 000/μL): cell fragments for clotting.
- **Hemostasis:** vascular spasm → platelet plug → coagulation cascade (thrombin converts fibrinogen to fibrin mesh). Hemophilia results from missing clotting factors; anticoagulants (heparin, warfarin — a vitamin K antagonist, DOACs) prevent unwanted clots.

### Oxygen and CO₂ transport
- ~98.5% of O₂ is carried bound to hemoglobin (each Hb carries up to 4 O₂); the rest dissolved. Cooperative binding gives a sigmoidal dissociation curve; the Bohr effect promotes unloading in active tissues.
- CO₂ is carried as **bicarbonate** (~70%; formed by carbonic anhydrase in red cells), bound to hemoglobin (carbaminohemoglobin, ~20–23%), and dissolved (~7–10%).

**Cardiovascular disease** (coronary artery disease, stroke) is the leading cause of death worldwide (~18 million deaths per year), driven by atherosclerosis — plaque buildup in arteries, promoted by high LDL cholesterol, hypertension, smoking, diabetes and inactivity.

## 5. The Respiratory System

**Pathway:** nose/mouth → pharynx → larynx (vocal cords) → trachea (C-shaped cartilage rings) → bronchi → bronchioles → **alveoli** (~300–500 million; total surface area ~70–100 m², about half a tennis court).

**Gas exchange** occurs by **diffusion** across the thin alveolar–capillary membrane (~0.5 μm), down partial pressure gradients: alveolar PO₂ ~100 mmHg vs. venous ~40 mmHg; PCO₂ ~40 vs. ~46 mmHg. **Surfactant** (phospholipids from type II alveolar cells) reduces surface tension, preventing alveolar collapse.

**Ventilation mechanics:** inhalation is active — the **diaphragm** contracts and flattens, external intercostal muscles raise the ribs; thoracic volume increases, pressure falls below atmospheric (Boyle's law), and air flows in. Quiet exhalation is passive (elastic recoil).
- Tidal volume ~500 mL; vital capacity ~4.5–5 L; residual volume ~1.2 L; breathing rate ~12–20/min at rest.

**Control of breathing:** the medulla's respiratory centers respond mainly to **CO₂ levels** (detected as pH changes in cerebrospinal fluid by central chemoreceptors), not O₂ — except under severe hypoxia (peripheral chemoreceptors in carotid and aortic bodies). This is why hyperventilating before diving underwater is dangerous (it lowers CO₂, delaying the urge to breathe until O₂ is critically low).

**Disorders:** asthma (bronchoconstriction, inflammation), COPD (chronic bronchitis, emphysema — mostly from smoking), pneumonia, lung cancer (leading cause of cancer death), cystic fibrosis (thick mucus).

## 6. The Digestive System

Breaks food into absorbable molecules (mechanical and chemical digestion), absorbs nutrients and eliminates waste.

| Organ | Function | Key secretions/enzymes |
|---|---|---|
| Mouth | Chewing; begins starch digestion | Salivary amylase, lingual lipase |
| Esophagus | Peristalsis to stomach | — |
| Stomach | Storage; protein digestion; kills microbes (pH 1.5–3.5) | HCl (parietal cells), pepsinogen → pepsin (chief cells), intrinsic factor (for B₁₂ absorption), mucus |
| Small intestine (duodenum, jejunum, ileum; ~6 m) | Most digestion and absorption; villi and microvilli increase surface area to ~30 m² (estimates vary widely) | Brush-border enzymes (lactase, sucrase, peptidases) |
| Pancreas (exocrine) | Digestive enzymes and bicarbonate (neutralizes acid) | Pancreatic amylase, lipase, trypsin, chymotrypsin, nucleases |
| Liver | Produces bile (emulsifies fats); metabolism, detoxification, protein synthesis, glycogen storage | Bile salts |
| Gallbladder | Stores and concentrates bile | — |
| Large intestine (colon; ~1.5 m) | Water and electrolyte absorption; feces formation; gut microbiota (vitamin K, short-chain fatty acids) | — |

- Carbohydrates → monosaccharides; proteins → amino acids and small peptides; fats → fatty acids and monoglycerides (absorbed into lacteals as chylomicrons); nucleic acids → nucleotides.
- **Hormonal control:** gastrin (stimulates acid secretion), secretin (bicarbonate from pancreas), cholecystokinin (CCK; enzyme release and gallbladder contraction).
- **Gut microbiome:** ~38 trillion bacteria (about as many as human cells), hundreds of species; involved in digestion, vitamin synthesis, immunity and possibly mood and metabolism.
- *Helicobacter pylori* causes most stomach ulcers (Marshall and Warren, Nobel 2005 — Barry Marshall famously drank a culture to prove it).

## 7. The Urinary System and Osmoregulation

**Kidneys** filter ~180 L of plasma per day, producing ~1–2 L of urine. Functions: excretion of nitrogenous waste (urea), regulation of water and electrolyte balance, blood pressure (renin), acid–base balance, red blood cell production (EPO), and vitamin D activation.

**The nephron** (~1 million per kidney):
1. **Glomerular filtration:** blood pressure forces water and small solutes from glomerular capillaries into **Bowman's capsule**; cells and proteins stay in blood. (Glomerular filtration rate ~125 mL/min.)
2. **Tubular reabsorption:** the **proximal convoluted tubule** reabsorbs ~65% of water and Na⁺ and essentially all glucose and amino acids (glucose appears in urine only when blood levels exceed the transport maximum, as in diabetes — "mellitus" means "honey-sweet").
3. **Loop of Henle:** creates a concentration gradient in the medulla (countercurrent multiplier); the descending limb is permeable to water, the ascending limb pumps out salt.
4. **Distal tubule and collecting duct:** fine-tuning under hormonal control — **ADH** inserts aquaporins (water reabsorption, concentrated urine); **aldosterone** increases Na⁺ reabsorption and K⁺ secretion.
5. **Tubular secretion:** H⁺, K⁺, drugs and toxins are actively secreted into the tubule.

Alcohol inhibits ADH release (increasing urine output); caffeine is a mild diuretic. Kidney failure is treated by dialysis or transplantation.

## 8. The Immune System

### Innate (non-specific) immunity
Fast (minutes to hours), no memory:
- **Barriers:** skin, mucous membranes, stomach acid, lysozyme in tears and saliva, cilia, normal microbiota.
- **Cells:** neutrophils and macrophages (phagocytosis), natural killer (NK) cells (kill infected and cancer cells), dendritic cells (antigen presentation), mast cells.
- **Pattern recognition receptors** (Toll-like receptors) detect conserved microbial molecules (LPS, flagellin, viral RNA) (Beutler and Hoffmann, with Steinman for dendritic cells, Nobel 2011).
- **Inflammation:** redness, heat, swelling, pain — histamine and cytokines increase blood flow and vessel permeability, recruiting immune cells.
- **Fever** inhibits pathogen growth and boosts immune responses.
- **Complement system:** plasma proteins that tag microbes (opsonization), recruit cells, and punch holes in membranes.
- **Interferons:** antiviral signaling proteins.

### Adaptive (specific) immunity
Slower on first exposure (days), highly specific, with **memory**:
- **B lymphocytes** (mature in bone marrow) → **humoral immunity:** activated B cells become **plasma cells** secreting **antibodies** (immunoglobulins). Antibodies are Y-shaped proteins with two heavy and two light chains; variable regions bind specific **antigens**. Functions: neutralization, opsonization, agglutination, complement activation. Classes: IgG (most abundant; crosses placenta), IgM (first produced; pentamer), IgA (secretions, breast milk), IgE (allergy, parasites), IgD.
- **T lymphocytes** (mature in the thymus) → **cell-mediated immunity:**
  - **Helper T cells (CD4⁺)** coordinate responses, activating B cells and cytotoxic T cells via cytokines. HIV infects and destroys CD4⁺ T cells, causing AIDS.
  - **Cytotoxic T cells (CD8⁺)** kill infected and cancerous cells displaying foreign peptides on **MHC class I** molecules.
  - **Regulatory T cells** prevent autoimmunity (Brunkow, Ramsdell and Sakaguchi, Nobel 2025, for peripheral immune tolerance).
- **Diversity:** **V(D)J recombination** of gene segments generates billions of distinct antibody and T-cell receptor specificities from a limited number of genes (Susumu Tonegawa, Nobel 1987). **Clonal selection** (Burnet, 1957): an antigen activates only the lymphocytes that recognize it, which proliferate.
- **Immunological memory:** memory B and T cells enable a faster, stronger **secondary response** — the basis of lasting immunity and vaccination.

### Vaccination
Exposure to harmless forms of antigens (inactivated or attenuated pathogens, subunits, toxoids, viral vectors, mRNA) primes memory without disease. Edward Jenner (1796) used cowpox to protect against smallpox; Louis Pasteur developed rabies and anthrax vaccines. Smallpox was eradicated in 1980; polio is nearly eradicated. **Herd immunity** protects those who cannot be vaccinated when enough of the population is immune (threshold ≈ 1 − 1/R₀; for measles, R₀ ~12–18, requiring ~95% coverage). Vaccines are estimated to have saved over 150 million lives in the last 50 years.

### Immune disorders
Allergies (IgE-mediated hypersensitivity; anaphylaxis), autoimmune diseases (type 1 diabetes, rheumatoid arthritis, lupus, multiple sclerosis), immunodeficiencies (HIV/AIDS, SCID), transplant rejection (MHC mismatch; immunosuppressive drugs), and immunotherapy for cancer.

## 9. The Musculoskeletal System

### Skeleton
An adult has **206 bones** (babies have ~270–300, many of which fuse). Functions: support, protection (skull, rib cage), movement (levers for muscles), mineral storage (99% of body calcium; phosphate), blood cell production (red marrow), and endocrine functions. Bone is living tissue constantly remodeled by **osteoblasts** (build) and **osteoclasts** (resorb); the skeleton is replaced roughly every 10 years. **Osteoporosis** is reduced bone density. Joints: fibrous (skull sutures), cartilaginous (intervertebral discs), synovial (knee, shoulder — freely movable).

### Muscle types

| Feature | Skeletal | Cardiac | Smooth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Location | Attached to bones | Heart | Walls of organs, vessels |
| Appearance | Striated, multinucleate, long fibers | Striated, branched, intercalated discs | Non-striated, spindle-shaped |
| Control | Voluntary (somatic) | Involuntary (autonomic, myogenic) | Involuntary |
| Speed | Fast | Intermediate | Slow, sustained |

### The sliding filament mechanism
(Huxley and Hanson; Huxley and Niedergerke, 1954.) Skeletal muscle fibers contain myofibrils made of repeating **sarcomeres** — the contractile units — with thin **actin** and thick **myosin** filaments.
1. A motor neuron releases **acetylcholine** at the neuromuscular junction, triggering an action potential in the muscle fiber.
2. The AP spreads down **T-tubules**, causing the **sarcoplasmic reticulum** to release **Ca²⁺**.
3. Ca²⁺ binds **troponin**, which shifts **tropomyosin**, exposing myosin-binding sites on actin.
4. **Cross-bridge cycle:** myosin heads (energized by ATP hydrolysis) bind actin, perform the **power stroke** (pulling thin filaments toward the sarcomere center), release when a new ATP binds, and re-cock.
5. Sarcomeres shorten (Z discs move closer; I bands and H zones narrow; A bands stay constant) — filaments slide past each other without changing length.
6. Relaxation: Ca²⁺ is pumped back into the SR (Ca²⁺-ATPase).
**Rigor mortis** occurs after death because ATP is depleted and myosin cannot detach from actin.

Energy sources: stored ATP (seconds), **phosphocreatine** (~10 s), anaerobic glycolysis (lactate; ~1–2 min), aerobic respiration (sustained). **Slow-twitch (type I)** fibers are oxidative and fatigue-resistant (marathon runners); **fast-twitch (type II)** fibers are powerful but fatigue quickly (sprinters).

## 10. The Reproductive System (Overview)

- **Male:** testes produce sperm (spermatogenesis in seminiferous tubules, ~74 days per cycle) and testosterone (Leydig cells); accessory glands (seminal vesicles, prostate) produce semen.
- **Female:** ovaries produce eggs and estrogen/progesterone. The **menstrual cycle** (~28 days): follicular phase (FSH → follicle growth, rising estrogen), **ovulation** (LH surge, ~day 14 — a positive feedback event), luteal phase (corpus luteum secretes progesterone, preparing the endometrium). Without pregnancy, hormone levels fall and menstruation occurs.
- **Fertilization** usually occurs in the oviduct; the zygote divides into a blastocyst that implants in the uterus ~6–10 days later. The placenta secretes **hCG** (detected by pregnancy tests), which maintains the corpus luteum. Human gestation lasts ~38 weeks from fertilization (~40 weeks from the last menstrual period).
- **In vitro fertilization (IVF):** Robert Edwards (Nobel 2010); first IVF baby, Louise Brown, born 1978; over 10 million IVF births since.

## 11. Summary

| System | Main organs | Key functions |
|---|---|---|
| Nervous | Brain, spinal cord, nerves | Rapid communication, control, sensation |
| Endocrine | Pituitary, thyroid, adrenals, pancreas, gonads | Hormonal regulation |
| Cardiovascular | Heart, vessels, blood | Transport of O₂, nutrients, wastes, heat |
| Respiratory | Lungs, airways | Gas exchange |
| Digestive | GI tract, liver, pancreas | Digestion and absorption |
| Urinary | Kidneys, bladder | Excretion, osmoregulation, pH |
| Immune/lymphatic | Lymph nodes, spleen, thymus, marrow | Defense |
| Musculoskeletal | Bones, muscles, joints | Support, movement |
| Integumentary | Skin, hair, nails | Protection, thermoregulation |
| Reproductive | Gonads, accessory organs | Reproduction |

**Core principles:** homeostasis via negative feedback; structure fits function (large surface areas for exchange — alveoli, villi, capillaries); gradients drive transport; communication by nerves (fast) and hormones (slow, widespread).
