# Atomic Structure, Electron Configuration and the Periodic Table

Chemistry is the science of matter and its transformations. All chemical behavior — why sodium explodes in water, why carbon forms millions of compounds, why gold doesn't tarnish — ultimately comes from the structure of atoms, and in particular from the arrangement of their electrons. The periodic table organizes this knowledge into one of the most powerful tools in science.

## 1. The Development of Atomic Theory

- **Democritus (~400 BCE):** matter consists of indivisible particles, *atomos*.
- **Antoine Lavoisier (1789):** law of conservation of mass; modern concept of elements.
- **Joseph Proust (1799):** law of definite proportions — a compound always contains the same elements in the same mass ratio.
- **John Dalton (1803–1808):** atomic theory — elements consist of identical atoms; compounds form from atoms in simple whole-number ratios; reactions rearrange atoms. Law of multiple proportions.
- **J. J. Thomson (1897):** discovered the electron using cathode rays; "plum pudding" model.
- **Robert Millikan (1909):** measured the electron charge.
- **Ernest Rutherford (1911):** gold-foil experiment revealed a tiny, dense, positive nucleus; most of the atom is empty space.
- **Niels Bohr (1913):** quantized electron orbits explain hydrogen's spectrum.
- **Henry Moseley (1913):** X-ray spectra showed that atomic number (nuclear charge), not atomic mass, orders the elements.
- **James Chadwick (1932):** discovered the neutron.
- **Quantum mechanics (1925–1926):** Schrödinger, Heisenberg and others replaced orbits with orbitals.

## 2. Subatomic Particles

| Particle | Symbol | Charge | Mass (kg) | Mass (u) | Location |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Proton | p⁺ | +1 ($+1.602\times10^{-19}$ C) | $1.6726\times10^{-27}$ | 1.00728 | Nucleus |
| Neutron | n⁰ | 0 | $1.6749\times10^{-27}$ | 1.00866 | Nucleus |
| Electron | e⁻ | −1 | $9.109\times10^{-31}$ | 0.000549 | Around nucleus |

- The nucleus has a radius of ~1–10 fm; the atom ~0.1 nm (100 000 times larger). If the nucleus were a marble, the atom would be the size of a football stadium.
- **Atomic number** $Z$ = number of protons; defines the element.
- **Mass number** $A$ = protons + neutrons.
- A neutral atom has $Z$ electrons. **Ions** have gained (anions, negative) or lost (cations, positive) electrons.

### Isotopes and atomic mass
**Isotopes** have the same $Z$ but different numbers of neutrons, and nearly identical chemistry. Notation: $^{A}_{Z}$X, e.g. $^{12}_6$C, $^{13}_6$C, $^{14}_6$C.

The **atomic mass** listed in the periodic table is the weighted average over natural isotopic abundances:
$$\bar M = \sum_i f_iM_i$$

**Worked example 2.1:** chlorine is 75.76% ³⁵Cl (34.969 u) and 24.24% ³⁷Cl (36.966 u).
$\bar M = 0.7576\times34.969 + 0.2424\times36.966 = 26.492 + 8.960 = 35.45$ u.

**Mass spectrometry** measures isotopic masses and abundances. Isotope ratios are used in dating, tracing food origins, paleoclimate reconstruction (¹⁸O/¹⁶O in ice cores), and detecting doping in sports.

## 3. Quantum Mechanical Model of the Atom

Electrons do not orbit like planets. They occupy **orbitals** — regions described by wavefunctions, where $|\psi|^2$ gives the probability density of finding the electron.

### Quantum numbers

| Symbol | Name | Allowed values | Describes |
|---|---|---|---|
| $n$ | Principal | 1, 2, 3, ... | Shell; energy and size |
| $\ell$ | Angular momentum (azimuthal) | 0 to $n - 1$ | Subshell shape: s (0), p (1), d (2), f (3) |
| $m_\ell$ | Magnetic | $-\ell$ to $+\ell$ | Orbital orientation |
| $m_s$ | Spin | $+\frac12$ or $-\frac12$ | Electron spin |

| Shell $n$ | Subshells | Orbitals | Max electrons ($2n^2$) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1s | 1 | 2 |
| 2 | 2s, 2p | 1 + 3 = 4 | 8 |
| 3 | 3s, 3p, 3d | 1 + 3 + 5 = 9 | 18 |
| 4 | 4s, 4p, 4d, 4f | 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 = 16 | 32 |

### Orbital shapes
- **s orbitals:** spherical. The 2s orbital has one radial node; 3s has two.
- **p orbitals:** dumbbell-shaped with a nodal plane through the nucleus; three orientations ($p_x$, $p_y$, $p_z$).
- **d orbitals:** five orbitals, four of them cloverleaf-shaped ($d_{xy}$, $d_{xz}$, $d_{yz}$, $d_{x^2-y^2}$) and $d_{z^2}$ with a "doughnut" ring.
- **f orbitals:** seven orbitals with complex shapes.
- Total nodes in an orbital: $n - 1$ ($\ell$ angular nodes + $n - \ell - 1$ radial nodes).

## 4. Electron Configurations

### Rules
1. **Aufbau principle:** electrons fill orbitals from lowest energy upward. The approximate order follows the **$(n + \ell)$ rule** (Madelung rule): lower $n + \ell$ fills first; for equal $n + \ell$, lower $n$ first:
$$1s < 2s < 2p < 3s < 3p < 4s < 3d < 4p < 5s < 4d < 5p < 6s < 4f < 5d < 6p < 7s < 5f < 6d < 7p$$
2. **Pauli exclusion principle:** no two electrons in an atom have the same four quantum numbers — each orbital holds at most 2 electrons, with opposite spins.
3. **Hund's rule:** within a subshell, electrons occupy separate orbitals with parallel spins before pairing. (Parallel spins keep electrons apart, reducing repulsion — an exchange effect.)

### Examples

| Element | $Z$ | Configuration | Noble-gas notation |
|---|---|---|---|
| H | 1 | 1s¹ | — |
| He | 2 | 1s² | — |
| C | 6 | 1s² 2s² 2p² | [He] 2s² 2p² |
| N | 7 | 1s² 2s² 2p³ | [He] 2s² 2p³ (three unpaired) |
| O | 8 | 1s² 2s² 2p⁴ | [He] 2s² 2p⁴ |
| Ne | 10 | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ | [He] 2s² 2p⁶ |
| Na | 11 | 1s² 2s² 2p⁶ 3s¹ | [Ne] 3s¹ |
| Cl | 17 | [Ne] 3s² 3p⁵ | |
| K | 19 | [Ar] 4s¹ | |
| Fe | 26 | [Ar] 3d⁶ 4s² | |
| Cu | 29 | **[Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s¹** (exception) | |
| Cr | 24 | **[Ar] 3d⁵ 4s¹** (exception) | |
| Br | 35 | [Ar] 3d¹⁰ 4s² 4p⁵ | |
| Ag | 47 | [Kr] 4d¹⁰ 5s¹ (exception) | |
| Au | 79 | [Xe] 4f¹⁴ 5d¹⁰ 6s¹ (exception) | |

**Exceptions** (Cr, Cu, Mo, Ag, Au, and others): half-filled (d⁵) and completely filled (d¹⁰) subshells are especially stable because of exchange energy and the near-degeneracy of $ns$ and $(n-1)d$ orbitals.

**Ions:** remove electrons from the highest-$n$ shell first. For transition metals, **4s electrons are removed before 3d**: Fe²⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁶; Fe³⁺ = [Ar] 3d⁵ (half-filled, notably stable). Isoelectronic species have the same configuration: O²⁻, F⁻, Ne, Na⁺, Mg²⁺ all have [Ne].

**Valence electrons** — those in the outermost shell (plus partially filled d or f subshells for transition elements) — determine chemical behavior. **Core electrons** are inner electrons.

**Magnetism:** atoms or ions with unpaired electrons are **paramagnetic** (attracted to magnetic fields); those with all electrons paired are **diamagnetic** (weakly repelled).

## 5. The Periodic Table

### History
Dmitri Mendeleev (1869) arranged the 63 known elements by atomic mass and chemical properties, leaving **gaps for undiscovered elements** and predicting their properties. His predictions for "eka-aluminium" (gallium, discovered 1875), "eka-boron" (scandium, 1879) and "eka-silicon" (germanium, 1886) were strikingly accurate — for germanium he predicted atomic mass 72 (actual 72.6), density 5.5 g/cm³ (5.35), and a white oxide EO₂. Lothar Meyer developed a similar table independently. Moseley (1913) showed atomic number is the true ordering principle, resolving anomalies like Te/I and Ar/K.

Today the table contains **118 confirmed elements**; the last four (Nh 113, Mc 115, Ts 117, Og 118) were named in 2016. Elements beyond uranium (Z > 92) are synthetic, except for trace natural plutonium and neptunium.

### Structure
- **Periods** (rows 1–7): correspond to filling successive shells. Period lengths 2, 8, 8, 18, 18, 32, 32 reflect subshell capacities.
- **Groups** (columns 1–18): elements with similar valence configurations and therefore similar chemistry.
- **Blocks:** s-block (groups 1–2 + He), p-block (groups 13–18), d-block (transition metals, groups 3–12), f-block (lanthanides and actinides).

### Important groups

| Group | Name | Valence configuration | Characteristics |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Alkali metals (Li, Na, K, Rb, Cs, Fr) | ns¹ | Soft, very reactive metals; form +1 ions; react violently with water (reactivity increases down the group) |
| 2 | Alkaline earth metals (Be, Mg, Ca, Sr, Ba, Ra) | ns² | Reactive metals; form +2 ions |
| 3–12 | Transition metals | (n−1)d¹⁻¹⁰ ns⁰⁻² | Multiple oxidation states, colored compounds, catalysts, magnetic properties |
| 13 | Boron group | ns² np¹ | B is a metalloid; Al a light, abundant metal |
| 14 | Carbon group | ns² np² | C (nonmetal) → Si, Ge (metalloids) → Sn, Pb (metals) |
| 15 | Pnictogens | ns² np³ | N₂ unreactive (triple bond); P, As, Sb, Bi |
| 16 | Chalcogens | ns² np⁴ | O, S, Se, Te, Po; form −2 ions |
| 17 | Halogens (F, Cl, Br, I, At) | ns² np⁵ | Very reactive nonmetals; diatomic molecules; form −1 ions; F most reactive |
| 18 | Noble gases (He, Ne, Ar, Kr, Xe, Rn, Og) | ns² np⁶ (He: 1s²) | Very unreactive; full valence shells. Xe forms compounds (XeF₂, XeF₄, XeO₃; Bartlett, 1962) |
| — | Lanthanides (La–Lu) | 4f filling | Rare earths: magnets (Nd, Sm), phosphors (Eu, Tb), lasers (Er, Nd) |
| — | Actinides (Ac–Lr) | 5f filling | All radioactive; U, Pu nuclear fuels |

**Metals** (left and center; ~80% of elements): lustrous, conductive, malleable, tend to lose electrons. **Nonmetals** (upper right): poor conductors, tend to gain or share electrons. **Metalloids** (B, Si, Ge, As, Sb, Te) lie along the "staircase" and have intermediate properties — several are semiconductors.

## 6. Periodic Trends

### Effective nuclear charge
An outer electron does not feel the full nuclear charge $Z$; inner electrons **shield** it. The **effective nuclear charge** is
$$Z_{\text{eff}} = Z - S$$
where $S$ is the shielding constant (estimated with **Slater's rules**). Across a period, $Z$ increases while shielding by same-shell electrons is poor, so $Z_{\text{eff}}$ increases. Down a group, new shells are added and $Z_{\text{eff}}$ on valence electrons changes only slowly. Most trends follow from $Z_{\text{eff}}$ and $n$.

Example (Slater): for Na's 3s electron, $S = 8\times0.85 + 2\times1.00 = 8.8$, so $Z_{\text{eff}} = 11 - 8.8 = 2.2$.

### Atomic radius
- **Decreases across a period** (left → right): increasing $Z_{\text{eff}}$ pulls electrons closer.
- **Increases down a group:** electrons occupy higher shells.
- Cations are smaller than their parent atoms; anions are larger.
- **Lanthanide contraction:** poor shielding by 4f electrons makes 5d elements (Hf, Ta, W, ...) nearly the same size as their 4d counterparts (Zr, Nb, Mo, ...), so they have very similar chemistry (Zr and Hf are notoriously hard to separate).

### Ionization energy (IE)
The energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom: $\text{X(g)} \to \text{X}^+\text{(g)} + e^-$.
- **Increases across a period**, **decreases down a group** — opposite to atomic radius.
- Helium has the highest first IE (24.6 eV); cesium among the lowest (3.9 eV).
- **Anomalies:** IE(B) < IE(Be) because boron's 2p electron is higher in energy and better shielded than beryllium's 2s; IE(O) < IE(N) because oxygen's fourth 2p electron is paired, adding repulsion, whereas nitrogen's half-filled 2p³ is stable.
- **Successive ionization energies** increase, with a huge jump when a core electron must be removed — revealing the number of valence electrons. Mg: IE₁ = 738, IE₂ = 1451, IE₃ = 7733 kJ/mol (jump after 2 electrons).

### Electron affinity (EA)
Energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron. Generally more exothermic (more negative) across a period toward the halogens. Chlorine has the most exothermic electron affinity (−349 kJ/mol), greater than fluorine (−328 kJ/mol), because fluorine's small 2p shell is crowded. Noble gases and group 2 have positive (unfavorable) electron affinities.

### Electronegativity (χ)
The tendency of an atom **in a molecule** to attract shared electrons (Linus Pauling, 1932).
- **Increases across a period**, **decreases down a group.**
- **Fluorine** is the most electronegative element (3.98 on the Pauling scale); cesium and francium the least (~0.7–0.8).
- Common values: F 3.98, O 3.44, N 3.04, Cl 3.16, Br 2.96, C 2.55, S 2.58, H 2.20, P 2.19, Na 0.93, K 0.82.
- Electronegativity differences predict bond polarity: ΔEN < 0.4 nonpolar covalent; 0.4–1.7 (or ~2) polar covalent; larger mostly ionic. (The boundaries are approximate; bonding is a continuum.)

### Metallic character
Increases down a group and toward the left. Francium and cesium are the most metallic.

### Summary of trends

| Property | Across a period (→) | Down a group (↓) |
|---|---|---|
| Atomic radius | Decreases | Increases |
| Ionization energy | Increases | Decreases |
| Electron affinity (magnitude) | Generally increases | Generally decreases |
| Electronegativity | Increases | Decreases |
| Metallic character | Decreases | Increases |
| $Z_{\text{eff}}$ on valence electrons | Increases | Roughly constant/slight increase |

### Diagonal relationships
Elements diagonally adjacent in periods 2 and 3 (Li–Mg, Be–Al, B–Si) show similar properties because their size-to-charge ratios are similar.

## 7. Notable Facts

- **Most abundant elements:** universe — H (~74% by mass), He (~24%); Earth's crust — O (46%), Si (28%), Al (8%), Fe (5%); human body (by mass) — O (65%), C (18%), H (10%), N (3%), Ca, P.
- **Densest element:** osmium (22.59 g/cm³). **Lowest melting metal:** mercury (−38.8 °C). **Highest melting element:** carbon (sublimes ~3600 °C at 1 atm); highest melting metal tungsten (3422 °C).
- **Liquids at room temperature:** mercury and bromine (cesium and gallium melt just above, at 28.5 °C and 29.8 °C — gallium melts in your hand).
- **Element names:** many honor scientists (Cm curium, Es einsteinium, Md mendelevium, No nobelium, Bh bohrium), places (Am, Cf, Ga, Ge, Fr, Po, Ts), mythology (Ti titanium, Pm promethium), or properties (Cl from Greek *chloros*, "pale green"; I from *iodes*, "violet").
- **Symbols from Latin:** Na (natrium), K (kalium), Fe (ferrum), Cu (cuprum), Ag (argentum), Au (aurum), Sn (stannum), Sb (stibium), Hg (hydrargyrum), Pb (plumbum), W (wolfram, German).
