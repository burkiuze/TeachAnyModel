# Carbohydrates, Lipids, Membranes and Nucleic Acids

Along with proteins, three other classes of biomolecules underlie all life: **carbohydrates** (energy and structure), **lipids** (energy storage, membranes and signaling) and **nucleic acids** (information storage and transfer). This document describes their chemical structures and how structure determines function.

## Part I — Carbohydrates

### 1. Definitions and classification
**Carbohydrates** ("hydrates of carbon", often with formula Cₙ(H₂O)ₙ) are polyhydroxy aldehydes or ketones and their derivatives and polymers. They are the most abundant biomolecules on Earth; photosynthesis converts ~100 billion tonnes of CO₂ into carbohydrates each year.
- **Monosaccharides:** simple sugars (glucose, fructose, galactose, ribose).
- **Disaccharides:** two units (sucrose, lactose, maltose).
- **Oligosaccharides:** 3–~20 units (blood-group antigens, glycoprotein chains).
- **Polysaccharides:** many units (starch, glycogen, cellulose, chitin).

### 2. Monosaccharides
Classified by:
- **Carbonyl type:** **aldoses** (aldehyde, at C1) or **ketoses** (ketone, usually at C2).
- **Number of carbons:** trioses (3), tetroses (4), pentoses (5), hexoses (6), heptoses (7).
Examples: glyceraldehyde (aldotriose), dihydroxyacetone (ketotriose), ribose (aldopentose), glucose (aldohexose), fructose (ketohexose).

**Stereochemistry:** an aldohexose has 4 stereocenters → 2⁴ = 16 stereoisomers (8 D and 8 L). In Fischer projections, the **D/L** designation refers to the configuration of the stereocenter farthest from the carbonyl (C5 in hexoses): –OH on the right → **D**. Nearly all natural sugars are D.
- **D-Glucose:** OH groups at C2, C3, C4, C5 are right, left, right, right in the Fischer projection.
- **D-Mannose:** C2 epimer of glucose. **D-Galactose:** C4 epimer of glucose.
- **D-Fructose:** ketohexose with the same configuration as glucose at C3–C5.

### 3. Cyclic structures
In water, pentoses and hexoses exist mainly as **cyclic hemiacetals** (or hemiketals), formed by intramolecular attack of a hydroxyl group on the carbonyl carbon:
- **Pyranoses** (six-membered rings, like pyran): glucose C5–OH attacks C1.
- **Furanoses** (five-membered rings, like furan): fructose C5–OH attacks C2; ribose in nucleic acids.

The former carbonyl carbon becomes a new stereocenter — the **anomeric carbon** — giving two **anomers**:
- **α-anomer:** anomeric –OH *trans* to the CH₂OH (C6) group (drawn "down" in a standard Haworth projection of a D-sugar).
- **β-anomer:** anomeric –OH *cis* to the CH₂OH (drawn "up").

**Mutarotation:** in solution, α and β anomers interconvert through the open-chain form. Pure α-D-glucopyranose ([α] = +112°) and pure β-D-glucopyranose (+18.7°) each change to an equilibrium value of +52.7° (~36% α, ~64% β, <0.02% open chain). β is favored because, in the chair conformation, **all substituents of β-D-glucose are equatorial**.

**Haworth projections** draw rings as flat hexagons with substituents above or below; chair conformations show true 3D shapes.

### 4. Reactions of monosaccharides
- **Reducing sugars:** sugars with a free anomeric carbon (hemiacetal) can open to the aldehyde form and reduce Cu²⁺ (Benedict's, Fehling's tests) or Ag⁺ (Tollens'). All monosaccharides (including fructose, via enediol tautomerization) are reducing sugars. Historically used to test for glucose in urine (diabetes).
- **Oxidation:** aldehyde → carboxylic acid (aldonic acids; gluconic acid); both ends → aldaric acids; C6 → uronic acids (glucuronic acid, used in liver detoxification by glucuronidation).
- **Reduction:** carbonyl → alcohol, giving **alditols** (sorbitol from glucose; xylitol, a sweetener; mannitol, a diuretic).
- **Glycoside formation:** the anomeric –OH reacts with an alcohol (acid catalyst) to form an **acetal** called a **glycoside**, linked by a **glycosidic bond**. Glycosides cannot mutarotate and are non-reducing.
- **Phosphorylation:** sugar phosphates (glucose-6-phosphate, fructose-1,6-bisphosphate, ribose-5-phosphate) are metabolic intermediates; phosphorylation traps glucose inside cells.
- **Non-enzymatic glycation:** glucose reacts with protein amino groups (Maillard reaction). In the body, **HbA1c** (glycated hemoglobin) reflects average blood glucose over ~3 months; in cooking, Maillard reactions create browning and flavor in bread crusts, roasted coffee and seared meat.

**Amino sugars and deoxy sugars:** glucosamine, N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc, the chitin monomer), sialic acid, 2-deoxyribose (DNA).

### 5. Disaccharides

| Disaccharide | Components | Linkage | Reducing? | Source |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Maltose** | Glucose + glucose | α(1→4) | Yes | Starch digestion, malt |
| **Lactose** | Galactose + glucose | β(1→4) | Yes | Milk (~5% in cow's milk, ~7% in human milk) |
| **Sucrose** | Glucose + fructose | α1→β2 (both anomeric carbons) | **No** | Cane and beet sugar |
| **Cellobiose** | Glucose + glucose | β(1→4) | Yes | Cellulose breakdown |
| **Trehalose** | Glucose + glucose | α1→α1 | No | Insects, fungi; protects against desiccation |

- **Lactose intolerance:** most adult humans lose intestinal lactase activity after weaning; undigested lactose is fermented by colon bacteria, causing gas and discomfort. **Lactase persistence** evolved independently in several pastoralist populations (Northern Europe, East Africa, Middle East) within the last ~10 000 years — a famous example of recent human evolution and gene–culture coevolution.
- **Sucrose** hydrolysis (by invertase or acid) gives "invert sugar" (glucose + fructose), sweeter than sucrose; honey is largely invert sugar. Relative sweetness: fructose ~1.7, sucrose 1.0, glucose ~0.7, lactose ~0.2; aspartame ~200; sucralose ~600.

### 6. Polysaccharides

| Polysaccharide | Monomer | Linkages | Structure | Function |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Amylose** (starch, ~20–30%) | α-D-glucose | α(1→4) | Unbranched helix (6 residues/turn) | Plant energy storage; blue-black complex with iodine |
| **Amylopectin** (starch, ~70–80%) | α-D-glucose | α(1→4) + α(1→6) branches every ~24–30 residues | Branched | Plant energy storage |
| **Glycogen** | α-D-glucose | α(1→4) + α(1→6) branches every ~8–12 residues | Highly branched | Animal energy storage (liver, muscle) |
| **Cellulose** | β-D-glucose | β(1→4) | Straight chains, hydrogen-bonded into microfibrils | Plant cell walls; most abundant organic polymer |
| **Chitin** | N-acetyl-β-D-glucosamine | β(1→4) | Like cellulose | Arthropod exoskeletons, fungal cell walls |
| **Glycosaminoglycans** (hyaluronic acid, heparin, chondroitin sulfate) | Disaccharide repeats with uronic acids and amino sugars | Various | Negatively charged, hydrated | Joint lubrication, cartilage, anticoagulation |
| **Peptidoglycan** | GlcNAc–NAM with peptide cross-links | β(1→4) | Mesh | Bacterial cell walls (target of penicillin and lysozyme) |

**Why can't humans digest cellulose?** The β(1→4) linkage gives straight chains that pack into strong, hydrogen-bonded fibers; human amylases only hydrolyze α linkages. Ruminants (cows), termites and others rely on symbiotic microbes with cellulases. Cellulose is still valuable as dietary fiber.

**Glycogen's branching** creates many non-reducing ends, allowing rapid mobilization of glucose by glycogen phosphorylase during exercise or fasting; it also increases solubility.

**Glycoproteins and glycolipids:** oligosaccharides attached to proteins and lipids on cell surfaces mediate cell recognition. **ABO blood groups** differ by the terminal sugar on red-cell surface glycans: type A has N-acetylgalactosamine, type B has galactose, type O lacks both. Influenza virus binds sialic acid on host cells (hemagglutinin) and is released by neuraminidase — the target of oseltamivir (Tamiflu).

## Part II — Lipids

### 7. Definition
**Lipids** are biomolecules defined by **solubility**: insoluble in water, soluble in nonpolar organic solvents. They are structurally diverse: fatty acids, triglycerides, phospholipids, sphingolipids, steroids, terpenes, eicosanoids, waxes and fat-soluble vitamins.

### 8. Fatty acids
Long-chain carboxylic acids, usually with an even number of carbons (12–24; built two carbons at a time from acetyl-CoA).
- **Saturated:** no C=C; straight chains pack tightly → higher melting points; solid fats (butter, lard, coconut oil). Examples: lauric (C12:0), myristic (C14:0), palmitic (C16:0, mp 63 °C), stearic (C18:0, mp 70 °C).
- **Unsaturated:** one or more C=C, almost always **cis** in nature, creating ~30° kinks that disrupt packing → lower melting points; liquid oils. Oleic (C18:1 cis-Δ9, mp 13 °C; olive oil), linoleic (C18:2 cis-Δ9,12, ω-6; mp −5 °C), α-linolenic (C18:3, ω-3; mp −11 °C), arachidonic (C20:4, ω-6), EPA (C20:5, ω-3) and DHA (C22:6, ω-3; abundant in brain and retina; fish oil).
- Notation: C18:1 cis-Δ9 means 18 carbons, one double bond starting at C9 (counting from the carboxyl carbon). **Omega (ω)** numbering counts from the methyl end.
- **Essential fatty acids:** linoleic and α-linolenic acids cannot be made by humans (we lack enzymes to introduce double bonds beyond C9).
- **Trans fats:** produced by partial hydrogenation; straighter chains behave like saturated fats and raise LDL cholesterol and cardiovascular risk.

### 9. Triacylglycerols (triglycerides)
Esters of **glycerol** with three fatty acids — the main energy storage form in animals (adipose tissue) and plants (seed oils).
- Highly reduced and anhydrous: ~37 kJ/g (9 kcal/g) vs. ~17 kJ/g for hydrated glycogen. A 70 kg person stores ~15 kg of fat (~550 000 kJ, enough for weeks) but only ~0.4 kg of glycogen (about a day's worth).
- **Saponification** (NaOH) → glycerol + soap (fatty acid salts).
- Hydrolysis by **lipases** in digestion (with bile salts emulsifying fats) and in adipose tissue (hormone-sensitive lipase during fasting).
- **Rancidity:** oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids (radical chain reactions) and hydrolysis produce off-flavors; antioxidants (vitamin E, BHT) slow it.

### 10. Membrane lipids
**Phospholipids (glycerophospholipids):** glycerol esterified to two fatty acids (positions 1 and 2) and a phosphate group (position 3) linked to a polar head group (choline → phosphatidylcholine/lecithin; ethanolamine; serine; inositol). They are **amphipathic**: a hydrophilic head and two hydrophobic tails.
**Sphingolipids:** built on sphingosine; sphingomyelin (myelin sheaths around nerves), glycolipids (cerebrosides, gangliosides). Defects in their breakdown cause lysosomal storage diseases (Tay–Sachs, Gaucher, Niemann–Pick).

In water, amphipathic lipids self-assemble spontaneously (driven by the hydrophobic effect) into:
- **Micelles** (single-tailed lipids like soaps and detergents; spheres with tails inward).
- **Lipid bilayers** (two-tailed phospholipids) — the basis of all cell membranes, ~5 nm thick.
- **Liposomes** (closed bilayer vesicles) — used for drug delivery and in mRNA vaccines (lipid nanoparticles).

### 11. Steroids and other lipids
**Steroids** have a fused four-ring skeleton (three six-membered rings and one five-membered ring):
- **Cholesterol:** membrane component (modulates fluidity), precursor of steroid hormones, bile acids and vitamin D. Transported in blood by lipoproteins (LDL — "bad", delivers cholesterol to tissues; HDL — "good", returns it to the liver). High LDL contributes to atherosclerosis; statins inhibit cholesterol synthesis.
- **Steroid hormones:** cortisol (stress, glucocorticoid), aldosterone (salt balance), testosterone and estradiol (sex hormones), progesterone. Being lipid-soluble, they cross membranes and bind intracellular receptors that regulate gene expression.
- **Bile acids** (cholic acid) emulsify dietary fats.
- **Vitamin D** forms in skin from 7-dehydrocholesterol by UV-B light.

**Terpenes/isoprenoids** (built from five-carbon isoprene units): carotenoids (β-carotene, lycopene), vitamins A, E and K, natural rubber, menthol, limonene, squalene (cholesterol precursor), taxol, and the dolichols and ubiquinone of cells.

**Eicosanoids** (from arachidonic acid): prostaglandins (inflammation, pain, fever, uterine contraction), thromboxanes (platelet aggregation), leukotrienes (asthma). **Aspirin** irreversibly acetylates cyclooxygenase (COX), blocking prostaglandin and thromboxane synthesis — explaining its anti-inflammatory and anti-clotting effects (John Vane, Nobel 1982).

**Waxes:** esters of long-chain fatty acids and long-chain alcohols (beeswax, carnauba wax, lanolin); waterproofing for leaves, feathers and fur.

## Part III — Biological Membranes

### 12. The fluid mosaic model
(Singer and Nicolson, 1972.) Membranes are a two-dimensional fluid of lipids in which proteins are embedded or associated:
- **Integral (intrinsic) proteins** span the bilayer (transmembrane α-helices of ~20 hydrophobic residues, or β-barrels).
- **Peripheral proteins** associate with surfaces.
- Lipids diffuse rapidly laterally (~1 μm/s) but rarely flip between leaflets (transverse "flip-flop" takes hours to days without flippases). The two leaflets are asymmetric (e.g. phosphatidylserine is normally inside; its exposure on the outside signals apoptosis).
- **Fluidity** increases with unsaturation and shorter chains, and with temperature. Cholesterol buffers fluidity. Organisms adjust membrane composition to temperature (**homeoviscous adaptation**).
- **Lipid rafts:** dynamic, cholesterol- and sphingolipid-rich microdomains.

### 13. Membrane transport
The bilayer is permeable to small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, N₂, steroids) and somewhat to water and small uncharged polar molecules, but nearly impermeable to ions and large polar molecules.
- **Simple diffusion** down a concentration gradient.
- **Facilitated diffusion** via channels (ion channels, aquaporins — Peter Agre, Nobel 2003) and carriers (GLUT glucose transporters).
- **Primary active transport** using ATP: the **Na⁺/K⁺-ATPase** pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP, consuming ~20–30% of resting energy expenditure (and up to ~2/3 in neurons) (Jens Skou, Nobel 1997). The Ca²⁺-ATPase and the gastric H⁺/K⁺-ATPase are other examples.
- **Secondary active transport:** uses an ion gradient created by a primary pump (Na⁺/glucose symporter in the intestine and kidney — the basis of oral rehydration therapy, which has saved millions of lives from cholera and diarrheal disease).
- **Endocytosis and exocytosis** for large molecules.

## Part IV — Nucleic Acids

### 14. Nucleotides
Nucleic acids are polymers of **nucleotides**, each consisting of:
1. A **nitrogenous base:**
   - **Purines** (two fused rings): **adenine (A)**, **guanine (G)**.
   - **Pyrimidines** (one ring): **cytosine (C)**, **thymine (T, DNA only)**, **uracil (U, RNA only)**.
2. A **pentose sugar:** **2'-deoxyribose** (DNA) or **ribose** (RNA; has a 2'-OH).
3. One to three **phosphate groups** (attached to the 5' carbon).

A base + sugar = **nucleoside** (adenosine, guanosine, cytidine, thymidine, uridine); add phosphate(s) = **nucleotide** (AMP, ADP, ATP). The base attaches to C1' of the sugar via an N-glycosidic bond (N9 of purines, N1 of pyrimidines).

**Nucleotides have many roles beyond nucleic acids:** ATP (energy currency), GTP (energy for protein synthesis, signaling via G proteins), cAMP and cGMP (second messengers), NAD⁺, FAD and coenzyme A (contain adenine nucleotides), S-adenosylmethionine (methyl donor).

### 15. DNA structure
Nucleotides are linked by **3'→5' phosphodiester bonds** between the 3'-OH of one sugar and the 5'-phosphate of the next, forming a sugar–phosphate **backbone** with bases projecting from it. Each strand has a direction: a **5' end** (phosphate) and a **3' end** (hydroxyl). Sequences are written 5'→3'.

**Chargaff's rules** (Erwin Chargaff, 1950): in DNA from any organism, [A] = [T] and [G] = [C] (so purines = pyrimidines), but the A+T/G+C ratio varies between species.

**The double helix** (James Watson and Francis Crick, 1953, based on X-ray diffraction data from Rosalind Franklin and Maurice Wilkins; Nobel 1962 to Watson, Crick and Wilkins — Franklin had died in 1958):
- Two **antiparallel** strands (one 5'→3', the other 3'→5') coiled into a **right-handed** helix (B-form).
- **Complementary base pairing** via hydrogen bonds: **A–T (2 hydrogen bonds)** and **G–C (3 hydrogen bonds)**. Each pair is a purine + pyrimidine, so the helix has a uniform width (~2.0 nm).
- Hydrophilic sugar–phosphate backbones on the outside; bases stacked inside, ~0.34 nm apart; ~10.5 base pairs per turn (pitch ~3.4–3.6 nm).
- **Major and minor grooves** allow proteins (transcription factors) to read sequences without unwinding.
- **Stability** comes mainly from **base stacking** (van der Waals and hydrophobic interactions between adjacent base pairs) as well as hydrogen bonding; the negatively charged phosphates are shielded by cations (Mg²⁺, Na⁺) and histones.
- Watson and Crick noted: "It has not escaped our notice that the specific pairing we have postulated immediately suggests a possible copying mechanism for the genetic material" — **semiconservative replication**, demonstrated by Meselson and Stahl in 1958 using ¹⁵N/¹⁴N density labeling.

Other forms: **A-DNA** (wider, under dehydrated conditions; also RNA–DNA hybrids and double-stranded RNA), **Z-DNA** (left-handed, in GC-rich alternating sequences), **G-quadruplexes** (in telomeres), triple helices.

**Scale:** the human genome has ~3.1 billion base pairs per haploid set; a diploid cell contains ~2 meters of DNA packed into a nucleus ~6 μm across, wrapped around **histone** octamers (nucleosomes, ~147 bp each) and further compacted into chromatin and chromosomes.

### 16. DNA denaturation and hybridization
Heating separates the strands (**melting**); the **melting temperature** ($T_m$) rises with GC content (three hydrogen bonds and stronger stacking) and with salt concentration. Melting is monitored by increased UV absorbance at 260 nm (**hyperchromic effect** — stacked bases absorb less). On slow cooling, complementary strands **reanneal**. Hybridization underlies **PCR**, DNA microarrays, Southern blots, fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and CRISPR guide RNA targeting.

### 17. RNA
- Single-stranded, contains **ribose** and **uracil** instead of thymine.
- Folds into complex structures with intramolecular base pairing (hairpins, stem-loops, pseudoknots); G–U "wobble" pairs are common.
- The 2'-OH makes RNA less chemically stable than DNA (it can attack the adjacent phosphodiester bond, especially under basic conditions) — one reason DNA, not RNA, is the long-term genetic storage molecule.
- Types: **mRNA** (messenger, carries the gene's code to ribosomes), **tRNA** (transfer, ~76 nucleotides, cloverleaf/L-shape, carries amino acids and reads codons via anticodons), **rRNA** (ribosomal, structural and catalytic core of the ribosome), and regulatory RNAs (**miRNA**, **siRNA** — RNA interference, Fire and Mello, Nobel 2006; **lncRNA**; **snRNA** in splicing).
- **RNA world hypothesis:** early life may have relied on RNA both as genetic material and catalyst (ribozymes), before DNA and proteins took over these roles.
- **mRNA vaccines** (COVID-19, 2020) use lipid-nanoparticle-encapsulated mRNA with modified nucleosides (pseudouridine) to evade innate immune detection (Karikó and Weissman, Nobel Medicine 2023).

### 18. Summary

| Biomolecule | Monomer | Linkage | Key functions |
|---|---|---|---|
| Polysaccharides | Monosaccharides | Glycosidic (acetal) bonds | Energy storage (starch, glycogen), structure (cellulose, chitin), recognition |
| Triglycerides | Glycerol + 3 fatty acids | Ester bonds | Long-term energy storage, insulation |
| Phospholipids | Glycerol + 2 FA + phosphate–head group | Ester, phosphodiester | Membranes |
| Steroids | Isoprene-derived 4-ring skeleton | — | Membranes (cholesterol), hormones |
| Proteins | Amino acids | Peptide (amide) bonds | Catalysis, structure, transport, signaling |
| Nucleic acids | Nucleotides | 3'→5' phosphodiester bonds | Genetic information, catalysis (ribozymes), regulation |

**Key facts:** β-D-glucose has all-equatorial substituents; sucrose is non-reducing; cellulose is β(1→4) and indigestible to humans; cis double bonds lower fatty-acid melting points; phospholipids self-assemble into bilayers; DNA is an antiparallel double helix with A–T (2 H-bonds) and G–C (3 H-bonds) pairing.
