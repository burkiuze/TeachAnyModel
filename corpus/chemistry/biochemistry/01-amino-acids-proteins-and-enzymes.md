# Amino Acids, Proteins and Enzymes

Proteins are the molecular machines of life. They catalyze nearly all biochemical reactions (enzymes), transport molecules (hemoglobin), provide structure (collagen, keratin), enable movement (actin, myosin), defend against pathogens (antibodies), transmit signals (receptors, hormones like insulin), regulate genes (transcription factors), and store nutrients. All of this diversity arises from linear chains of just 20 standard amino acids folded into precise three-dimensional shapes.

## 1. Amino Acids

### General structure
An **α-amino acid** has an amino group (–NH₂), a carboxyl group (–COOH), a hydrogen atom and a variable **side chain (R group)**, all attached to the central **α-carbon**:
$$\text{H}_2\text{N–C}_\alpha\text{H(R)–COOH}$$
- All standard amino acids except glycine (R = H) are **chiral**; proteins use the **L**-enantiomers (which are *S* in CIP terms, except L-cysteine, which is *R*). D-amino acids occur in bacterial cell walls and some peptide antibiotics.

### Zwitterions and acid–base behavior
At physiological pH, amino acids exist as **zwitterions**: ⁺H₃N–CHR–COO⁻ (net charge zero for neutral side chains). This explains their high melting points (often decomposing above 200 °C), water solubility, and insolubility in nonpolar solvents.
- α-COOH pKa ≈ 2.0–2.4; α-NH₃⁺ pKa ≈ 9–10.
- **Isoelectric point (pI):** the pH at which the net charge is zero. For amino acids with non-ionizable side chains, pI = (pKa₁ + pKa₂)/2 — e.g. glycine: (2.34 + 9.60)/2 = 5.97. For acidic amino acids, average the two lowest pKa values; for basic ones, the two highest.
- **Electrophoresis** separates amino acids and proteins by charge at a given pH; **isoelectric focusing** separates by pI.

## 2. Peptides and the Peptide Bond

Amino acids link through **peptide bonds** (amide bonds) formed by condensation of the α-carboxyl of one with the α-amino group of the next, releasing water:
$$\text{H}_2\text{N–CHR}_1\text{–COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{N–CHR}_2\text{–COOH}\to\text{H}_2\text{N–CHR}_1\text{–CO–NH–CHR}_2\text{–COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$
- Chains have direction: **N-terminus** (free amino) to **C-terminus** (free carboxyl), written left to right.
- **Dipeptides, tripeptides, oligopeptides** (< ~20), **polypeptides**; proteins typically have 50–2000+ residues (human titin: ~34 000).
- The peptide bond is **planar and rigid** (partial double-bond character from resonance, ~40%), almost always *trans*. Rotation occurs only about the N–Cα (**φ**, phi) and Cα–C (**ψ**, psi) bonds. A **Ramachandran plot** shows the sterically allowed φ/ψ combinations (G. N. Ramachandran, 1963).
- In vivo, peptide bond formation is catalyzed by the ribosome (a ribozyme — RNA is the catalyst) and is driven by aminoacyl-tRNA activation using ATP.

**Biologically active peptides:** glutathione (γ-Glu-Cys-Gly, cellular antioxidant), oxytocin and vasopressin (9 residues), insulin (51 residues in two chains linked by disulfides), glucagon, endorphins and enkephalins (natural opioids), aspartame (Asp-Phe methyl ester sweetener), and peptide antibiotics (gramicidin, vancomycin) and toxins (α-amanitin, conotoxins).

## 3. Levels of Protein Structure

### Primary structure
The **sequence** of amino acids, encoded by the gene. Frederick Sanger determined the first protein sequence — insulin — in 1951–1955 (Nobel 1958). The primary structure determines all higher levels of structure. A single change can be devastating: in **sickle-cell anemia**, glutamate 6 of the hemoglobin β chain is replaced by valine (Glu6Val), creating a hydrophobic patch that causes deoxyhemoglobin to polymerize into fibers, distorting red blood cells. Carriers (heterozygotes) have partial resistance to malaria — a classic example of balancing selection.

### Secondary structure
Local, regular folding stabilized by **hydrogen bonds between backbone C=O and N–H groups**:
- **α-Helix** (Pauling, Corey and Branson, 1951): right-handed coil with **3.6 residues per turn**, rise of 1.5 Å per residue (pitch 5.4 Å); each C=O (residue *i*) hydrogen-bonds to the N–H of residue *i* + 4; side chains point outward. Proline (rigid ring, no N–H) and glycine (too flexible) are helix breakers. Keratin (hair, nails) is mostly α-helical coiled coils.
- **β-Sheet:** extended strands lying side by side, hydrogen-bonded between strands; **parallel** or **antiparallel**; side chains alternate above and below the pleated sheet. Silk fibroin is rich in antiparallel β-sheets (repeating Gly-Ala/Ser), making it strong but flexible.
- **Turns and loops:** β-turns (often containing Pro and Gly) reverse chain direction; loops connect elements and often form functional sites.
- **Collagen triple helix:** three left-handed polyproline-II-type helices wound into a right-handed superhelix; every third residue is glycine (Gly-X-Y repeats, often Gly-Pro-Hyp). Hydroxyproline formation requires vitamin C; its deficiency causes **scurvy** (weak collagen, bleeding gums, poor wound healing). Collagen is the most abundant protein in mammals (~25–35% of total protein).

### Tertiary structure
The overall 3D fold of a single polypeptide chain, stabilized by side-chain interactions:
- **Hydrophobic effect** (the dominant driving force): nonpolar side chains cluster in the interior, away from water, increasing water's entropy.
- **Hydrogen bonds** between side chains and backbone.
- **Ionic interactions (salt bridges)** between oppositely charged side chains (Lys/Arg with Asp/Glu).
- **Disulfide bonds** (covalent –S–S– between cysteines), common in extracellular proteins (antibodies, insulin, keratin — perms work by breaking and re-forming hair keratin disulfides).
- **Van der Waals interactions** from tight packing.
- **Metal coordination** (zinc fingers, iron–sulfur clusters).

Proteins are typically classified as **globular** (compact, water-soluble: enzymes, hemoglobin, antibodies), **fibrous** (elongated, structural: collagen, keratin, fibroin, elastin), **membrane** proteins (hydrophobic surfaces in the lipid bilayer: receptors, channels, transporters), and **intrinsically disordered** proteins/regions (lacking stable structure; important in signaling).

**Domains** are independently folding units (~50–250 residues) that often have distinct functions and recur across proteins.

### Quaternary structure
The assembly of multiple polypeptide **subunits**: hemoglobin (α₂β₂ tetramer), RNA polymerase, ATP synthase, viral capsids, the proteasome, and collagen. Subunit interactions enable **cooperativity** and **allosteric regulation**.

### Determining structure
X-ray crystallography (Kendrew's myoglobin, 1958, and Perutz's hemoglobin, 1959; Nobel 1962), NMR spectroscopy, and **cryo-electron microscopy** (Nobel 2017: Dubochet, Frank, Henderson). The Protein Data Bank holds over 200 000 experimentally determined structures. **AlphaFold** (DeepMind, 2020–2021) predicts protein structures from sequence with accuracy often comparable to experiment; Demis Hassabis and John Jumper shared the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry with David Baker (computational protein design).

## 4. Protein Folding

**Anfinsen's experiment** (1950s–1961, Nobel 1972): ribonuclease A was denatured with urea and β-mercaptoethanol (reducing its four disulfide bonds). Upon slow removal of the denaturants, it spontaneously refolded and regained full activity. Conclusion: **the amino acid sequence contains all the information needed to determine the native 3D structure** (thermodynamic hypothesis — the native state is the free-energy minimum under physiological conditions).

**Levinthal's paradox** (1969): a 100-residue protein randomly sampling conformations (~3 per residue, so ~10⁴⁸) would take longer than the age of the universe to find its native state, yet proteins fold in microseconds to seconds. Resolution: folding follows a **funnel-shaped energy landscape**, with progressive, cooperative formation of local structure and a hydrophobic collapse (molten globule), not a random search.

**Molecular chaperones** (heat-shock proteins such as Hsp70, and chaperonins such as GroEL/GroES) prevent aggregation and assist folding in the crowded cellular environment.

**Denaturation:** loss of native structure (and function) without breaking peptide bonds, caused by heat, extreme pH, organic solvents, detergents (SDS), chaotropic agents (urea, guanidinium chloride), heavy metals, and mechanical agitation. Cooking an egg denatures albumin irreversibly (aggregation); a fever above ~42 °C begins to denature critical proteins.

**Misfolding diseases:** proteins that misfold and aggregate into amyloid fibrils (cross-β structure) cause **Alzheimer's disease** (amyloid-β plaques and tau tangles), **Parkinson's disease** (α-synuclein), **Huntington's disease** (polyglutamine expansions), **type 2 diabetes** (amylin), and **prion diseases** (Creutzfeldt–Jakob disease, "mad cow disease"/BSE, kuru), in which a misfolded prion protein (PrPSc) converts normal PrPC into the misfolded form — infectious proteins (Stanley Prusiner, Nobel 1997).

## 5. Oxygen-Binding Proteins: Myoglobin and Hemoglobin

Both contain **heme** — a porphyrin ring with a central **Fe²⁺** that binds O₂ reversibly. The surrounding protein prevents oxidation to Fe³⁺ (methemoglobin cannot bind O₂). A **distal histidine** hydrogen-bonds bound O₂ and sterically disfavors CO binding; even so, CO binds heme ~200–250 times more tightly than O₂, which is why carbon monoxide poisoning is so dangerous.

- **Myoglobin** (muscle; single chain, 153 residues): O₂-storage protein with a **hyperbolic** binding curve; high affinity ($P_{50}$ ≈ 2.8 torr).
- **Hemoglobin** (red blood cells; α₂β₂ tetramer): O₂ transport with a **sigmoidal** binding curve due to **cooperativity** — binding of O₂ to one subunit increases the affinity of the others ($P_{50}$ ≈ 26 torr; Hill coefficient ≈ 2.8–3). It shifts between a low-affinity **T (tense)** state and a high-affinity **R (relaxed)** state (concerted MWC and sequential KNF models describe this). Cooperativity lets hemoglobin load O₂ fully in the lungs (~100 torr) and unload a large fraction in tissues (~20–40 torr).

**Allosteric regulators** shifting the curve to the right (lower affinity, more O₂ delivery):
- **Bohr effect:** lower pH (H⁺ binding stabilizes the T state) — metabolically active tissues produce acid.
- **CO₂** (forms carbamates on N-termini, and via H⁺).
- **2,3-Bisphosphoglycerate (2,3-BPG):** binds in the central cavity of deoxyhemoglobin; levels rise at high altitude.
- **Temperature** increase.
**Fetal hemoglobin** (α₂γ₂) binds 2,3-BPG weakly and thus has higher O₂ affinity than maternal hemoglobin, ensuring O₂ transfer across the placenta.

## 6. Enzymes: Biological Catalysts

### General properties
- **Catalytic power:** rate enhancements of 10⁶–10¹⁷ over uncatalyzed reactions.
- **Specificity:** for substrates (often including stereospecificity) and reactions.
- **Regulation:** activity can be adjusted to cellular needs.
- **Mild conditions:** aqueous solution, ~37 °C, neutral pH.
- Most enzymes are proteins; some are RNA (**ribozymes**: the ribosome's peptidyl transferase, self-splicing introns, RNase P; Cech and Altman, Nobel 1989).
- Named by adding "-ase" to the substrate or reaction (lactase, DNA polymerase, alcohol dehydrogenase). The EC classification has seven classes: oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases, ligases, translocases.

### How enzymes work
Enzymes bind substrates in an **active site** — a cleft or pocket lined with specific amino acid side chains.
- **Lock-and-key model** (Emil Fischer, 1894): complementary shapes.
- **Induced fit** (Daniel Koshland, 1958): binding induces conformational changes in the enzyme that optimize interactions (hexokinase closes around glucose like a clam).
- **Transition-state stabilization** (Linus Pauling, 1946): enzymes bind the **transition state** more tightly than the substrate, lowering the activation energy. **Transition-state analogs** are potent inhibitors and the basis of many drugs; catalytic antibodies were raised against them.

Catalytic strategies:
1. **Proximity and orientation:** bringing reactants together in the right geometry.
2. **General acid–base catalysis:** side chains (His, Asp, Glu, Lys, Cys, Tyr) donate or accept protons.
3. **Covalent catalysis:** a transient covalent enzyme–substrate intermediate (serine proteases' acyl-enzyme).
4. **Metal ion catalysis:** Zn²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺/³⁺, Cu⁺/²⁺ stabilize charges, activate water, or mediate redox (about one third of enzymes require metals).
5. **Electrostatic catalysis and desolvation.**

### Example: serine proteases (chymotrypsin, trypsin, elastase, thrombin)
The **catalytic triad** Ser195–His57–Asp102:
1. His57, oriented by Asp102, abstracts a proton from Ser195, making its oxygen a strong nucleophile.
2. Ser attacks the peptide carbonyl, forming a **tetrahedral intermediate** stabilized by the **oxyanion hole** (backbone N–H groups).
3. The C–N bond breaks; the amine part leaves; an **acyl-enzyme intermediate** forms.
4. Water, activated by His57, attacks the acyl-enzyme; a second tetrahedral intermediate collapses, releasing the carboxylic acid and regenerating the enzyme.
Specificity comes from the **S1 pocket**: chymotrypsin has a deep hydrophobic pocket (cleaves after Phe, Tyr, Trp); trypsin has Asp189 at the bottom (cleaves after Lys, Arg); elastase has a shallow pocket (cleaves after small residues like Ala, Gly). Nerve agents (sarin) and organophosphates irreversibly phosphorylate the active-site serine of acetylcholinesterase, a related serine hydrolase.

### Cofactors, coenzymes and vitamins
Many enzymes need non-protein helpers:
- **Cofactors:** metal ions (Zn²⁺ in carbonic anhydrase and alcohol dehydrogenase; Mg²⁺ with ATP; Fe in cytochromes and catalase; Cu in cytochrome c oxidase; Mo in nitrogenase; Se in glutathione peroxidase).
- **Coenzymes:** small organic molecules, often derived from **vitamins**. An enzyme without its cofactor is an **apoenzyme**; with it, a **holoenzyme**. Tightly bound coenzymes are **prosthetic groups**.

## 7. Enzyme Kinetics and Inhibition (Summary)

**Michaelis–Menten equation:** $v = \frac{V_{\max}[S]}{K_M + [S]}$; $k_{\text{cat}}$ = turnover number; $k_{\text{cat}}/K_M$ = catalytic efficiency (diffusion limit ~10⁸–10⁹ M⁻¹s⁻¹). Inhibition: competitive (raises apparent $K_M$), noncompetitive (lowers $V_{\max}$), uncompetitive (lowers both), irreversible. (Details are in the chemical kinetics document.)

**Effects of temperature and pH:** activity rises with temperature until denaturation (human enzymes optimal ~37 °C; *Taq* polymerase from hot-spring bacteria works at 72 °C, enabling PCR). Each enzyme has an optimal pH: pepsin (stomach) ~2; trypsin (intestine) ~8; most cytoplasmic enzymes ~7.

## 8. Regulation of Enzyme Activity

1. **Allosteric regulation:** effectors bind at sites other than the active site, shifting the enzyme between less active and more active conformations. Allosteric enzymes show sigmoidal kinetics. **Feedback inhibition:** the end product of a pathway inhibits the first committed step (e.g. CTP inhibits aspartate transcarbamoylase; isoleucine inhibits threonine deaminase; ATP inhibits phosphofructokinase-1).
2. **Covalent modification:** reversible **phosphorylation** by kinases (on Ser, Thr, Tyr) and dephosphorylation by phosphatases is the most common regulatory switch (Krebs and Fischer, Nobel 1992, glycogen phosphorylase). Others: acetylation, methylation, ubiquitination (targets proteins for degradation by the proteasome; Ciechanover, Hershko and Rose, Nobel 2004), glycosylation, ADP-ribosylation.
3. **Proteolytic activation of zymogens:** inactive precursors activated by cleavage — digestive enzymes (trypsinogen → trypsin by enteropeptidase; then trypsin activates chymotrypsinogen, proelastase, procarboxypeptidase), the **blood-clotting cascade** (prothrombin → thrombin; fibrinogen → fibrin), caspases in apoptosis, and insulin (from proinsulin). Pancreatitis can result when trypsin is activated prematurely inside the pancreas.
4. **Control of enzyme amount:** gene expression (transcription, translation) and protein degradation.
5. **Compartmentalization** and **isozymes** (different forms of an enzyme in different tissues, e.g. lactate dehydrogenase; cardiac troponins and isozymes are used as markers of heart attacks).

## 9. Enzymes in Medicine and Industry
- **Drugs as enzyme inhibitors:** aspirin and NSAIDs (cyclooxygenase), statins (HMG-CoA reductase), ACE inhibitors (angiotensin-converting enzyme), protease inhibitors (HIV, hepatitis C; nirmatrelvir for SARS-CoV-2), β-lactam antibiotics (bacterial transpeptidase), methotrexate (dihydrofolate reductase), allopurinol (xanthine oxidase), kinase inhibitors (imatinib), proton-pump inhibitors (H⁺/K⁺-ATPase), sildenafil (phosphodiesterase-5).
- **Diagnostics:** blood enzyme levels (ALT, AST for liver damage); glucose oxidase in glucometers; ELISA tests use enzyme labels (horseradish peroxidase, alkaline phosphatase).
- **Industry:** proteases and lipases in detergents; amylases in brewing and baking; glucose isomerase for high-fructose syrup; rennet (chymosin) in cheese making; cellulases for biofuels; lactase for lactose-free milk; engineered enzymes for pharmaceutical synthesis (directed evolution).
- **Biotechnology tools:** restriction enzymes, DNA ligase, polymerases (PCR), reverse transcriptase, CRISPR–Cas9 nuclease (Charpentier and Doudna, Nobel 2020).
