# Carboxylic Acids, Their Derivatives, Enolate Chemistry and Amines

This document covers three intertwined areas: **carboxylic acids and their derivatives** (esters, amides, acid chlorides, anhydrides, nitriles), which react by **nucleophilic acyl substitution**; **enolate chemistry**, which uses the acidity of α-hydrogens to form carbon–carbon bonds; and **amines**, the principal organic bases. Together they underlie the chemistry of fats, soaps, polyesters, nylons, proteins, and most pharmaceuticals.

## Part I — Carboxylic Acids

### 1. Structure and properties
- Functional group –COOH: a carbonyl and a hydroxyl on the same carbon.
- **Hydrogen-bonded dimers** in the gas phase and nonpolar solvents → high boiling points (acetic acid 118 °C, higher than propan-1-ol at 97 °C).
- Short-chain acids are water-soluble and pungent: formic acid (ant stings), acetic acid (vinegar, ~5%), butanoic acid (rancid butter, vomit), hexanoic–decanoic acids (goat-like odors; "capric", "caproic" from Latin *caper*, goat).
- **Fatty acids** (C₁₂–C₂₀): palmitic (C16:0), stearic (C18:0), oleic (C18:1, cis-9), linoleic (C18:2, ω-6), α-linolenic (C18:3, ω-3).
- Other important acids: benzoic acid (preservative), citric acid (citrus fruits, Krebs cycle), lactic acid (sour milk, muscles), oxalic acid (rhubarb leaves, toxic in large amounts), salicylic acid, amino acids, and terephthalic acid (PET plastic).

### 2. Acidity
pKa ~4–5: much more acidic than alcohols (~16) because the **carboxylate anion is resonance-stabilized** — the negative charge is shared equally by two oxygens (both C–O bonds 127 pm). Carboxylic acids react with NaOH **and** with NaHCO₃ (releasing CO₂), unlike phenols and alcohols — a basis for separating acids by extraction.

At physiological pH (~7.4), carboxylic acids exist almost entirely as carboxylate anions (Henderson–Hasselbalch: at pH = pKa + 3, the ratio is 1000:1); hence biochemists say "pyruvate", "lactate", "citrate".

**Substituent effects:** electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity, and the effect falls off with distance:

| Acid | pKa |
|---|---|
| CH₃COOH | 4.76 |
| ClCH₂COOH | 2.87 |
| Cl₂CHCOOH | 1.35 |
| Cl₃CCOOH | 0.66 |
| F₃CCOOH (TFA) | 0.23 |
| CH₃CH₂CHClCOOH (2-chloro) | 2.86 |
| CH₃CHClCH₂COOH (3-chloro) | 4.05 |
| ClCH₂CH₂CH₂COOH (4-chloro) | 4.52 |
| HCOOH | 3.75 |
| C₆H₅COOH | 4.20 |
| *p*-O₂NC₆H₄COOH | 3.44 |
| *p*-CH₃OC₆H₄COOH | 4.47 |

### 3. Preparation of carboxylic acids
1. Oxidation of primary alcohols or aldehydes (Jones, KMnO₄).
2. Oxidation of alkylbenzenes (KMnO₄, benzylic position) → benzoic acids.
3. Oxidative cleavage of alkenes/alkynes (KMnO₄, O₃ with oxidative workup).
4. **Carboxylation of Grignard reagents:** RMgX + CO₂ → RCOO⁻ → RCOOH (adds one carbon).
5. **Hydrolysis of nitriles:** RCN + H₃O⁺, heat → RCOOH (nitriles from SN2 of RX with CN⁻ — another one-carbon extension).
6. Hydrolysis of esters and amides.

## Part II — Carboxylic Acid Derivatives

### 4. The derivatives

| Derivative | Structure | Leaving group | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Acid chloride (acyl chloride) | RCOCl | Cl⁻ | Acetyl chloride |
| Acid anhydride | RCOOCOR | RCOO⁻ | Acetic anhydride |
| Thioester | RCOSR' | R'S⁻ | Acetyl coenzyme A |
| Ester | RCOOR' | R'O⁻ | Ethyl acetate |
| Amide | RCONR'₂ | R'₂N⁻ | Acetamide; peptide bonds |
| (Carboxylate) | RCOO⁻ | O²⁻ (never) | Sodium acetate |
| Nitrile (related, same oxidation level) | RC≡N | — | Acetonitrile |

### 5. Nucleophilic acyl substitution
Derivatives react with nucleophiles by **addition–elimination**, not by SN2:
1. **Addition:** the nucleophile attacks the carbonyl carbon, forming a **tetrahedral intermediate** (alkoxide).
2. **Elimination:** the carbonyl re-forms and the leaving group is expelled.
Net: substitution at the acyl carbon. (Contrast with aldehydes and ketones, which undergo addition because H⁻ and R⁻ are terrible leaving groups.)

Under acidic conditions, the carbonyl is first protonated, activating it toward weak nucleophiles.

### 6. Relative reactivity
$$\text{acid chloride} > \text{anhydride} > \text{thioester} > \text{ester} \approx \text{carboxylic acid} > \text{amide} > \text{carboxylate}$$

Reasons:
1. **Leaving-group ability:** better leaving groups (weaker bases: Cl⁻ > RCOO⁻ > RO⁻ > R₂N⁻) make derivatives more reactive.
2. **Resonance stabilization of the reactant:** lone-pair donation from the heteroatom into the carbonyl stabilizes it and lowers electrophilicity. Nitrogen donates most strongly (amides: ~75 kJ/mol rotational barrier about C–N), oxygen less, chlorine hardly at all (poor 3p–2p overlap).

**Consequence:** a more reactive derivative can be converted into any less reactive one, but not the reverse (directly). Acid chlorides → anhydrides → esters → amides is "downhill".

**Spectroscopic consequence:** C=O IR stretching frequency correlates with reactivity: acid chlorides ~1800 cm⁻¹; anhydrides ~1820 and ~1760 cm⁻¹ (two bands); esters ~1735 cm⁻¹; acids ~1710 cm⁻¹; amides ~1650–1690 cm⁻¹.

**Amides are remarkably stable:** uncatalyzed hydrolysis of a peptide bond at neutral pH has a half-life of several hundred years. This stability is ideal for proteins; enzymes (proteases) cleave them when needed.

### 7. Reactions of derivatives

#### Acid chlorides
- Made from acids with **SOCl₂** (byproducts SO₂ and HCl are gases), (COCl)₂ or PCl₅.
- + H₂O → acid; + ROH (pyridine) → ester; + NH₃/RNH₂ (2 equiv.) → amide; + RCOO⁻ → anhydride.
- + R₂CuLi (Gilman reagent) → ketone; + LiAlH(O*t*Bu)₃ → aldehyde; + AlCl₃/arene → aryl ketone (Friedel–Crafts acylation).

#### Anhydrides
Similar to acid chlorides but milder. **Aspirin synthesis:** salicylic acid + acetic anhydride → acetylsalicylic acid + acetic acid. Acetic anhydride is also used to make cellulose acetate and to acetylate morphine (heroin), so it is a controlled substance in many countries.

#### Esters
- **Fischer esterification:** RCOOH + R'OH ⇌ RCOOR' + H₂O (acid catalyst; equilibrium constant ~4 for acetic acid + ethanol; driven by excess alcohol or removal of water). Isotope labeling with ¹⁸O-alcohol shows the alcohol oxygen ends up in the ester — the acid loses the OH.
- **Acid hydrolysis:** reverse of Fischer esterification (excess water).
- **Saponification** (base hydrolysis): RCOOR' + NaOH → RCOO⁻Na⁺ + R'OH. **Irreversible** because the carboxylate is deprotonated. Saponification of fats (triglycerides) produces **soap** (sodium salts of fatty acids) and glycerol.
- **Transesterification:** ester + different alcohol (acid or base cat.) → new ester. **Biodiesel** is made by transesterifying vegetable oils with methanol, giving fatty acid methyl esters.
- **Aminolysis:** ester + amine → amide.
- **Reduction:** LiAlH₄ → two alcohols (the acyl part becomes a primary alcohol); DIBAL-H at −78 °C → aldehyde.
- **Grignard:** + 2 RMgX → tertiary alcohol.
- **Lactones** are cyclic esters (e.g. γ-butyrolactone; macrolide antibiotics like erythromycin; vitamin C is a lactone).
- Fragrance and flavor esters; **polyesters** (PET from terephthalic acid + ethylene glycol; biodegradable polylactic acid, PLA); **fats and oils** are triesters of glycerol.

#### Amides
- Made from acid chlorides or anhydrides + amines; or directly from acids + amines with coupling agents (DCC, EDC, HATU — the basis of peptide synthesis); or by heating ammonium carboxylate salts.
- Hydrolysis requires strong acid or base and prolonged heating.
- **Reduction:** LiAlH₄ reduces amides to **amines** (C=O → CH₂).
- **Hofmann rearrangement:** primary amide + Br₂/NaOH → amine with one less carbon (via isocyanate).
- **Lactams** are cyclic amides: the four-membered **β-lactam** ring of penicillins and cephalosporins is strained and reactive, acylating the bacterial transpeptidase enzyme that builds cell walls. Bacteria resist with β-lactamases, which hydrolyze the ring; inhibitors like clavulanic acid are co-administered.
- **Polyamides:** nylon-6,6 (adipic acid + hexamethylenediamine; Wallace Carothers at DuPont, 1935), nylon-6 (from caprolactam), Kevlar (aromatic polyamide, aramid), and proteins.
- Amides are planar with restricted C–N rotation, which shapes protein secondary structure (Pauling's α-helix and β-sheet, 1951).

#### Nitriles
- Made by SN2 of alkyl halides with NaCN/KCN, or by dehydrating primary amides (SOCl₂, P₂O₅).
- Hydrolysis (acid or base, heat) → amide → carboxylic acid.
- LiAlH₄ → primary amine (RCH₂NH₂); DIBAL-H → aldehyde; RMgX then H₃O⁺ → ketone.
- Acrylonitrile (CH₂=CHCN) → acrylic fibers and ABS plastic.

#### Thioesters in biochemistry
**Acetyl-CoA** (a thioester) is nature's activated acyl group: more reactive than an ester (sulfur's poorer resonance donation and better leaving group), but stable enough in water. It delivers acetyl groups in fatty-acid synthesis, the citric acid cycle, and acetylation of proteins and neurotransmitters (acetylcholine).

## Part III — Enols and Enolates: α-Carbon Chemistry

### 8. Acidity of α-hydrogens

| Compound | pKa of α-H |
|---|---|
| Ethyl acetate (ester) | ~25 |
| Acetone (ketone) | ~19–20 |
| Acetaldehyde (aldehyde) | ~17 |
| Ethyl acetoacetate (β-keto ester) | ~11 |
| Acetylacetone (β-diketone) | ~9 |
| Diethyl malonate (β-diester) | ~13 |
| Nitromethane | ~10 |
| Acetonitrile | ~25 |

α-Hydrogens are acidic because the **enolate** conjugate base is resonance-stabilized, with negative charge shared between the α-carbon and the oxygen. Hydrogens flanked by **two** carbonyls (1,3-dicarbonyl compounds) are much more acidic because the charge is delocalized over both.

### 9. Keto–enol tautomerism
Carbonyl compounds with α-hydrogens equilibrate with their **enol** tautomers (C=C–OH), catalyzed by acid or base. **Tautomers** are constitutional isomers that interconvert rapidly by proton migration (not resonance structures).
- Simple ketones are overwhelmingly keto (acetone: ~0.0001% enol).
- 1,3-Dicarbonyls have substantial enol content, stabilized by conjugation and intramolecular hydrogen bonding (acetylacetone ~80% enol in neat liquid).
- **Phenol** is an "enol" that stays enolized because of aromaticity.
- Tautomerization **racemizes** α-stereocenters (via the planar enol/enolate) and allows exchange of α-H for deuterium in D₂O.
- Biology: glycolysis involves enol and enolate intermediates (phosphoenolpyruvate has a very high phosphoryl-transfer potential partly because its product, pyruvate enol, tautomerizes to the keto form).

### 10. Enolate formation and reactions
- With **weaker bases** (NaOH, NaOEt), only a small equilibrium fraction of enolate forms from simple ketones — enough for aldol and related reactions.
- With **LDA** (lithium diisopropylamide; pKaH ~36), a strong, bulky, non-nucleophilic base, enolate formation is complete and rapid. At −78 °C, LDA removes the more accessible proton, giving the **kinetic enolate** (less substituted); weaker bases at higher temperature give the **thermodynamic enolate** (more substituted, more stable C=C).

#### α-Halogenation
- **Acidic conditions** (Br₂, CH₃COOH): via the enol; monohalogenation (the halogen withdraws electrons, slowing further enol formation).
- **Basic conditions:** via the enolate; each halogen makes the remaining α-H more acidic, so polyhalogenation occurs. Methyl ketones undergo the **haloform reaction**: RCOCH₃ + 3X₂ + 4OH⁻ → RCOO⁻ + CHX₃ + 3X⁻ + 3H₂O. With iodine, yellow **iodoform** (CHI₃) precipitates — a classical test for methyl ketones (and ethanol/secondary alcohols oxidizable to them).

#### Alkylation of enolates
Enolate + **primary** alkyl halide (SN2) → α-alkylated carbonyl. LDA ensures complete enolate formation and prevents self-condensation.
Example: cyclohexanone + LDA, then CH₃I → 2-methylcyclohexanone.

### 11. The aldol reaction
An enolate (nucleophile) adds to the carbonyl of another molecule (electrophile):
$$2\,\text{CH}_3\text{CHO}\xrightarrow{\text{NaOH}}\text{CH}_3\text{CH(OH)CH}_2\text{CHO}\;(3\text{-hydroxybutanal, "aldol"})$$
- Product: a **β-hydroxy carbonyl** compound (aldehyde + alcohol → "ald-ol"). Discovered by Charles-Adolphe Wurtz and Alexander Borodin (who was also a famous composer) around 1872.
- **Aldol condensation:** upon heating (or under acid), the β-hydroxy carbonyl **dehydrates** (via E1cB under basic conditions) to an **α,β-unsaturated carbonyl** compound (conjugated, therefore favored): 3-hydroxybutanal → but-2-enal (crotonaldehyde).
- **Crossed (mixed) aldol reactions** are useful when one partner cannot form an enolate (no α-H: benzaldehyde, formaldehyde) and is a good electrophile — e.g. the **Claisen–Schmidt** reaction of benzaldehyde with acetone gives benzalacetone/dibenzalacetone. Alternatively, preform one enolate with LDA ("directed aldol").
- **Intramolecular aldol** reactions form 5- and 6-membered rings readily.
- **Biology:** aldolase cleaves fructose-1,6-bisphosphate in glycolysis (a retro-aldol, via an enamine/imine with a lysine residue); gluconeogenesis runs it forward. Citrate synthase performs an aldol-type addition of acetyl-CoA to oxaloacetate.

### 12. The Claisen condensation
An ester enolate attacks another ester molecule (nucleophilic acyl substitution), expelling an alkoxide:
$$2\,\text{CH}_3\text{COOEt}\xrightarrow{1.\;\text{NaOEt};\;2.\;\text{H}_3\text{O}^+}\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_2\text{COOEt} + \text{EtOH}$$
- Product: a **β-keto ester** (ethyl acetoacetate).
- The equilibrium is driven by deprotonation of the acidic β-keto ester product (pKa ~11) by ethoxide; acid workup then gives the neutral product. Hence a full equivalent of base is required, and the starting ester needs two α-hydrogens.
- **Dieckmann condensation:** intramolecular Claisen of a diester → cyclic β-keto ester (five- or six-membered rings).
- **Crossed Claisen:** with a non-enolizable ester partner (ethyl benzoate, ethyl formate, diethyl carbonate, diethyl oxalate).
- **Biology:** thiolase in fatty-acid metabolism uses Claisen chemistry with thioesters (acetyl-CoA); fatty-acid and polyketide biosynthesis build carbon chains two carbons at a time by decarboxylative Claisen condensations with malonyl-CoA.

### 13. Synthetic applications of 1,3-dicarbonyl compounds
**Malonic ester synthesis** (→ substituted acetic acids):
1. Diethyl malonate + NaOEt → stabilized enolate.
2. + RX (SN2) → alkylated malonate (can repeat for dialkylation).
3. Hydrolysis (H₃O⁺, heat) → substituted malonic acid → **decarboxylation** (β-keto/β-diacids lose CO₂ readily via a cyclic six-membered transition state) → RCH₂COOH.

**Acetoacetic ester synthesis** (→ methyl ketones): the same sequence with ethyl acetoacetate gives RCH₂COCH₃.

### 14. Conjugate (Michael) addition and Robinson annulation
α,β-Unsaturated carbonyl compounds have electrophilic **β-carbons** (by resonance). Soft, stabilized nucleophiles add there — **conjugate (1,4-) addition** — while hard, strongly basic nucleophiles (RLi, RMgX) tend to add directly to the carbonyl carbon (1,2-addition).
- **Michael addition:** stabilized enolates (malonates, β-keto esters), amines, thiols, cyanide and **Gilman reagents** (R₂CuLi) add 1,4.
- **Robinson annulation** (Robert Robinson, Nobel 1947): Michael addition of a ketone enolate to methyl vinyl ketone, followed by intramolecular aldol condensation → a new six-membered cyclohexenone ring. Widely used in steroid and terpene synthesis.
- **Biology and medicine:** many drugs and toxins are Michael acceptors that covalently modify cysteine thiols in proteins (e.g. the anticancer drugs ibrutinib and osimertinib, designed as covalent kinase inhibitors). Glutathione detoxifies electrophiles by conjugate addition.

## Part IV — Amines

### 15. Structure and properties
- Derivatives of ammonia: **primary** (RNH₂), **secondary** (R₂NH), **tertiary** (R₃N); **quaternary ammonium** salts (R₄N⁺).
- Nitrogen is sp³, trigonal pyramidal (~108°), with a lone pair. Amines with three different groups are chiral in principle but undergo rapid **pyramidal inversion** (typically ~10⁷–10⁹ times per second at room temperature; barriers ~20–30 kJ/mol) and cannot be resolved; quaternary ammonium salts with four different groups can.
- Primary and secondary amines hydrogen-bond (weaker than alcohols, since N is less electronegative) → boiling points between alkanes and alcohols. Tertiary amines cannot H-bond with themselves.
- Low-molecular-weight amines smell fishy or putrid (trimethylamine in fish; putrescine and cadaverine in decaying flesh).

### 16. Basicity
Amines are the most important organic bases. Basicity is measured by the pKa of the conjugate acid (pKaH): larger pKaH = stronger base.

| Amine | pKaH |
|---|---|
| Ammonia | 9.25 |
| Methylamine | 10.64 |
| Dimethylamine | 10.73 |
| Trimethylamine | 9.80 |
| Triethylamine | 10.75 |
| Piperidine | 11.1 |
| Pyridine | 5.25 |
| Aniline | 4.6 |
| *p*-Nitroaniline | 1.0 |
| *p*-Methoxyaniline | 5.3 |
| Pyrrole | ~ −4 (N lone pair is part of the aromatic sextet) |
| Acetamide (amide) | ~ −0.5 (protonates on O) |
| Guanidine | 13.6 |

Factors:
- **Alkyl groups** donate electrons, increasing basicity relative to NH₃. In water, the order is 2° > 1° > 3° roughly, because solvation of the conjugate acid also matters (fewer N–H bonds to hydrogen-bond with water for 3° ammonium ions).
- **Arylamines** (aniline) are much weaker bases: the lone pair is delocalized into the ring. Electron-withdrawing ring substituents weaken them further; donors strengthen them.
- **Hybridization:** sp² N (pyridine, imines) and sp N (nitriles, pKaH ~ −10) are less basic than sp³ N.
- **Amides** are essentially non-basic due to resonance.
- **Guanidines and amidines** are strongly basic because their conjugate acids are highly resonance-stabilized (arginine side chain, pKaH ~12.5, is always protonated in proteins).

**Ammonium salts** are water-soluble, which is why amine drugs are often formulated as hydrochlorides (e.g. fluoxetine hydrochloride). Acid–base extraction separates amines from neutral compounds.

### 17. Preparation of amines
1. **Alkylation of ammonia/amines** with alkyl halides (SN2) — gives mixtures (overalkylation) since the products are more nucleophilic; useful for making quaternary salts with excess RX.
2. **Gabriel synthesis:** potassium phthalimide + RX, then hydrazine → clean **primary** amines.
3. **Reduction of nitro compounds** (Fe/HCl, H₂/Pd) → arylamines.
4. **Reduction of amides, nitriles and azides** with LiAlH₄ (azides via SN2 with NaN₃, then reduction — clean primary amines).
5. **Reductive amination** of aldehydes/ketones with NH₃ or amines and NaBH₃CN/NaBH(OAc)₃ — the most versatile method (also how the body makes many amino acids, via transamination and glutamate dehydrogenase).
6. **Hofmann and Curtius rearrangements** (from amides/acyl azides) → amines with one fewer carbon.
7. **Buchwald–Hartwig amination** (Pd-catalyzed aryl C–N coupling).

### 18. Reactions of amines
- **Acylation** with acid chlorides/anhydrides → amides (used to protect amines and to form peptide bonds).
- **Sulfonylation** → sulfonamides (Hinsberg test distinguishes 1°, 2° and 3° amines; sulfa drugs).
- **Hofmann elimination:** exhaustive methylation (excess CH₃I) gives a quaternary ammonium iodide; with Ag₂O and heat, E2 elimination gives the **least substituted** (Hofmann) alkene.
- **Nitrous acid:** primary arylamines → arenediazonium salts (Sandmeyer reactions, azo dyes); primary alkylamines → unstable diazonium ions (N₂ loss, carbocations); secondary amines → **N-nitrosamines** (potent carcinogens; found in some cured meats and occasionally as drug impurities).
- **Imine and enamine formation** with aldehydes/ketones.
- **Oxidation** of tertiary amines to N-oxides.

### 19. Biologically important amines
- **Amino acids** and proteins.
- **Neurotransmitters and hormones:** dopamine, norepinephrine, epinephrine (adrenaline), serotonin, histamine, acetylcholine (a quaternary ammonium ion), GABA.
- **Alkaloids** (nitrogen-containing plant natural products, typically basic): caffeine, nicotine, morphine, codeine, cocaine, quinine (antimalarial), atropine, strychnine, ephedrine, mescaline, capsaicin (an amide). Many are potent drugs or poisons.
- **Nucleobases** (adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine, uracil) and vitamins (thiamine, niacin, pyridoxine, folic acid).
- **Pharmaceuticals:** most drugs contain basic nitrogen atoms — antihistamines, antidepressants (fluoxetine), local anesthetics (lidocaine, procaine), β-blockers, opioids, antimalarials.
