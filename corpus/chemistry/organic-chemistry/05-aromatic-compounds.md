# Aromatic Compounds and Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution

Benzene and its relatives — **aromatic compounds** — are unusually stable and react in characteristic ways: instead of adding reagents across their double bonds like alkenes, they undergo **substitution**, preserving the aromatic ring. Aromatic rings are found in amino acids (phenylalanine, tyrosine, tryptophan), DNA bases, hormones (estradiol, thyroxine), neurotransmitters (dopamine, serotonin), countless drugs (aspirin, paracetamol, ibuprofen), dyes, explosives (TNT), polymers (polystyrene, PET, Kevlar), and fuels.

## 1. The Structure of Benzene

### History
- **1825:** Michael Faraday isolated benzene from illuminating gas residues.
- Formula C₆H₆ (degree of unsaturation 4) suggested high reactivity, yet benzene did not decolorize bromine water or react with KMnO₄ like alkenes.
- **1865:** August Kekulé proposed a six-membered ring of alternating single and double bonds that rapidly interconvert (reportedly inspired by a daydream of a snake biting its own tail).
- Only one monosubstituted isomer (C₆H₅X) and three disubstituted isomers (ortho, meta, para) exist — consistent with all six positions being equivalent.

### Modern description
- All six C–C bonds are **identical** (139 pm), intermediate between single (154 pm) and double (134 pm) bonds.
- Planar regular hexagon; all carbons sp² with 120° angles.
- Six p orbitals overlap continuously around the ring, forming a **delocalized π system** — two donut-shaped clouds of π electron density above and below the ring.
- The true structure is a **resonance hybrid** of the two Kekulé structures (often drawn as a hexagon with an inscribed circle). It does not oscillate between them.

### Resonance (aromatic stabilization) energy
- Hydrogenating cyclohexene releases 120 kJ/mol.
- "Cyclohexatriene" with three isolated double bonds would be expected to release ~360 kJ/mol.
- Benzene actually releases **208 kJ/mol** → benzene is ~**150 kJ/mol more stable** than expected. This is the **aromatic stabilization energy**, and it explains benzene's resistance to addition reactions.

### Molecular orbitals
Benzene's six p orbitals combine into six π MOs: three bonding (one lowest, two degenerate) and three antibonding. The six π electrons exactly fill the three bonding MOs — a closed shell, like a noble gas configuration. (A **Frost circle** — inscribing the polygon vertex-down in a circle — quickly shows the MO energy pattern for any cyclic conjugated system.)

## 2. Aromaticity and Hückel's Rule

A compound is **aromatic** if it is:
1. **Cyclic**,
2. **Planar** (or nearly so),
3. **Fully conjugated** (a p orbital on every ring atom),
4. Contains **4n + 2 π electrons** (n = 0, 1, 2, ...): 2, 6, 10, 14, 18... (**Hückel's rule**, Erich Hückel, 1931).

**Antiaromatic** compounds satisfy 1–3 but have **4n** π electrons (4, 8, 12...); they are especially **unstable**. Molecules that would be antiaromatic often distort out of planarity to become merely **nonaromatic**.

| Species | π electrons | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| Benzene | 6 | Aromatic |
| Cyclobutadiene | 4 | Antiaromatic (extremely unstable; exists only below ~35 K or as metal complexes) |
| Cyclooctatetraene (COT) | 8 | Nonaromatic (tub-shaped, behaves as a polyene) |
| COT dianion (C₈H₈²⁻) | 10 | Aromatic (planar) |
| Cyclopentadienyl anion (C₅H₅⁻) | 6 | Aromatic → cyclopentadiene is unusually acidic (pKa 16) |
| Cyclopentadienyl cation | 4 | Antiaromatic |
| Cycloheptatrienyl (tropylium) cation (C₇H₇⁺) | 6 | Aromatic → tropylium bromide is an ionic salt |
| Cyclopropenyl cation (C₃H₃⁺) | 2 | Aromatic (n = 0) |
| [18]Annulene | 18 | Aromatic |
| Azulene | 10 | Aromatic (blue color, dipolar) |

### Aromatic heterocycles
Rings containing atoms other than carbon (N, O, S) can be aromatic:
- **Pyridine (C₅H₅N):** like benzene with N replacing CH. The nitrogen's lone pair lies in the ring plane (sp² orbital) and is **not** part of the π system → available for bonding → pyridine is basic (pKaH 5.2) and nucleophilic. The ring is electron-poor and reacts sluggishly in electrophilic substitution.
- **Pyrrole (C₄H₄NH):** the N lone pair **is** part of the aromatic sextet (4 electrons from two C=C + 2 from N). Therefore pyrrole is essentially non-basic (protonation would destroy aromaticity; pKaH ~ −4) but very electron-rich, undergoing electrophilic substitution readily. Pyrrole units form porphyrins (heme, chlorophyll) and the amino acid tryptophan contains an indole (benzo-fused pyrrole).
- **Furan** (O) and **thiophene** (S): one heteroatom lone pair is in the π system, the other in the plane. Thiophene units form conducting polymers (polythiophene).
- **Imidazole:** two nitrogens, one pyrrole-like and one pyridine-like; amphoteric (pKaH ~7) — that is why the histidine side chain is a versatile acid–base catalyst in enzymes.
- **Purines and pyrimidines** (adenine, guanine, cytosine, thymine, uracil) — the bases of DNA and RNA — are aromatic heterocycles; their flat shapes allow stacking in the double helix.

### Polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons (PAHs)
Fused benzene rings: **naphthalene** (C₁₀H₈, mothballs), **anthracene**, **phenanthrene**, **pyrene**, **benzo[a]pyrene** (a potent carcinogen in tobacco smoke, grilled meat and soot; metabolized to an epoxide that binds DNA). **Graphite** and **graphene** are infinite PAH sheets. **Fullerenes** (C₆₀) have curved aromatic-like surfaces.

## 3. Nomenclature of Benzene Derivatives

- Monosubstituted: substituent + benzene (bromobenzene, nitrobenzene, ethylbenzene) or common names: **toluene** (methylbenzene), **phenol** (hydroxybenzene), **aniline** (aminobenzene), **anisole** (methoxybenzene), **benzoic acid**, **benzaldehyde**, **styrene** (vinylbenzene), **acetophenone** (methyl phenyl ketone), **benzonitrile**.
- Disubstituted: **ortho** (*o*-, 1,2-), **meta** (*m*-, 1,3-), **para** (*p*-, 1,4-). Example: *p*-dichlorobenzene (moth repellent); *o*-xylene; *m*-nitrotoluene.
- More substituents: use numbers, lowest locants, alphabetical order; a common-name parent takes position 1 (2,4,6-trinitrotoluene, TNT).
- As substituents: **phenyl** (C₆H₅–, "Ph"), **benzyl** (C₆H₅CH₂–, "Bn"), **aryl** ("Ar") for any aromatic ring.

## 4. Electrophilic Aromatic Substitution (EAS)

### General mechanism
Aromatic rings are nucleophiles (π electrons), but less reactive than alkenes because reaction temporarily disrupts aromaticity. They react with **strong electrophiles**, usually generated with a catalyst.

1. **Generation of the electrophile** E⁺ (often with a Lewis or Brønsted acid catalyst).
2. **Attack of the π system on E⁺** (slow, rate-determining): forms a resonance-stabilized carbocation called the **arenium ion** (σ complex, Wheland intermediate). Aromaticity is lost; the positive charge is delocalized over the ortho and para positions relative to the new sp³ carbon.
3. **Deprotonation** (fast): a base removes the H⁺ from the sp³ carbon, **restoring aromaticity**.

Net: Ar–H + E⁺ → Ar–E + H⁺. Substitution rather than addition occurs because restoring the aromatic ring (step 3) is far more favorable than adding a nucleophile (which would give a non-aromatic product).

**Kinetic isotope effect:** replacing H by D typically does not slow EAS (no primary isotope effect), confirming that C–H bond breaking is not rate-determining.

### The five classic EAS reactions

| Reaction | Reagents | Electrophile | Product |
|---|---|---|---|
| Halogenation | Br₂/FeBr₃ or Cl₂/FeCl₃ (or AlCl₃) | Br⁺ (Br–Br–FeBr₃ complex) | Ar–Br, Ar–Cl |
| Nitration | Conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ | Nitronium ion NO₂⁺ | Ar–NO₂ |
| Sulfonation | Fuming H₂SO₄ (SO₃) | SO₃ (or HSO₃⁺) | Ar–SO₃H (reversible) |
| Friedel–Crafts alkylation | R–Cl + AlCl₃ | Carbocation R⁺ (or complex) | Ar–R |
| Friedel–Crafts acylation | RCOCl + AlCl₃ (or anhydride) | Acylium ion R–C≡O⁺ | Ar–COR (aryl ketone) |

**Halogenation:** benzene does not react with Br₂ alone; the Lewis acid polarizes Br₂, making one Br strongly electrophilic. Iodination requires an oxidant (HNO₃, CuCl₂) to generate I⁺. Fluorination is too violent to control directly.

**Nitration:** sulfuric acid protonates nitric acid, which loses water to form **NO₂⁺**: $\text{HNO}_3 + 2\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4\rightleftharpoons\text{NO}_2^+ + \text{H}_3\text{O}^+ + 2\text{HSO}_4^-$. Nitroarenes are reduced (Fe/HCl, Sn/HCl, H₂/Pd) to **anilines** — the gateway to dyes and pharmaceuticals. Nitration of toluene three times gives TNT; nitration of glycerol (not EAS) gives nitroglycerin.

**Sulfonation:** reversible — sulfonation in concentrated acid, desulfonation in dilute hot aqueous acid. The SO₃H group can thus serve as a temporary **blocking group** to direct other substituents. Sulfonic acids are strong acids; alkylbenzenesulfonates are the main synthetic detergents; sulfa drugs (sulfonamides) were the first broadly effective antibacterial drugs (1930s).

**Friedel–Crafts alkylation** (Charles Friedel and James Crafts, 1877) — limitations:
1. **Carbocation rearrangements:** 1-chloropropane + benzene/AlCl₃ gives mainly isopropylbenzene (cumene), not propylbenzene.
2. **Polyalkylation:** the alkylated product is more reactive than benzene (alkyl groups activate), so multiple alkylations occur unless benzene is in large excess.
3. **Fails on deactivated rings** (nitrobenzene and stronger deactivators) and with –NH₂, –NHR, –NR₂ groups (which complex AlCl₃).
4. Vinyl and aryl halides don't form carbocations easily and do not react.
Industrially, cumene (from benzene + propene with an acid catalyst) is oxidized to give **phenol and acetone** (the cumene process).

**Friedel–Crafts acylation** avoids these problems:
- The **acylium ion** is resonance-stabilized and **does not rearrange**.
- The ketone product is **deactivated**, so the reaction stops after one acylation.
- To obtain a straight-chain alkylbenzene, acylate and then reduce the ketone: **Clemmensen** (Zn(Hg), HCl) or **Wolff–Kishner** (H₂NNH₂, KOH, heat) reduction, or catalytic hydrogenation for aryl ketones.
- Requires a full equivalent of AlCl₃ (which complexes with the product ketone).

## 5. Substituent Effects: Reactivity and Orientation

A substituent already on the ring affects both **how fast** further substitution occurs (activation/deactivation) and **where** the new group goes (ortho/para vs. meta direction). Both effects come from how the substituent stabilizes or destabilizes the arenium ion intermediate.

### Electronic mechanisms
- **Inductive effects (σ):** electronegative atoms withdraw electron density through σ bonds; alkyl groups donate weakly.
- **Resonance effects (π):** groups with lone pairs on the atom attached to the ring (–OH, –OR, –NH₂, –NHCOR, halogens) donate electrons into the ring by resonance; groups with π bonds to electronegative atoms (–NO₂, –C=O, –CN, –SO₃H) withdraw electrons by resonance.

### Classification

| Category | Substituents | Effect on rate (relative to benzene) | Directing |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Strongly activating** | –NH₂, –NHR, –NR₂, –OH, –O⁻ | Much faster (phenol ~10³; aniline brominates instantly) | ortho/para |
| **Moderately activating** | –OR (alkoxy), –NHCOR (amide), –OCOR | Faster | ortho/para |
| **Weakly activating** | –R (alkyl), –C₆H₅ (aryl), –CH=CH₂ | Slightly faster (toluene ~25×) | ortho/para |
| **Weakly deactivating** | –F, –Cl, –Br, –I | Slower (chlorobenzene ~0.03) | **ortho/para** |
| **Moderately deactivating** | –CHO, –COR, –COOH, –COOR, –CONH₂ | Slower | meta |
| **Strongly deactivating** | –NO₂, –NR₃⁺, –CF₃, –CCl₃, –CN, –SO₃H | Much slower (nitrobenzene ~10⁻⁷) | meta |

**Rules of thumb:**
1. **All activators are ortho/para directors.**
2. **Halogens are the exception:** deactivating (inductive withdrawal dominates the rate) but ortho/para directing (resonance donation stabilizes ortho/para arenium ions).
3. **Most deactivators are meta directors.** A quick test: if the atom attached to the ring bears a positive or partial positive charge (C=O carbon, N of NO₂, –NR₃⁺, CF₃), the group is a meta director.

### Why ortho/para or meta?
Draw the arenium ion resonance structures for attack at each position:
- For **ortho and para attack**, one resonance structure places the positive charge on the carbon **bearing the substituent**.
  - With an electron-donating group (–OCH₃, –NH₂), the lone pair can delocalize onto that carbon, giving an extra, especially stable resonance structure (all atoms with octets, e.g. an oxonium ion) → ortho/para attack is strongly favored.
  - With alkyl groups, a tertiary-like carbocation contributor is stabilized by hyperconjugation → ortho/para.
  - With an electron-withdrawing group (–NO₂, –C=O), placing the positive charge next to a positively polarized atom is very unfavorable → ortho/para attack is disfavored, so **meta** attack (which avoids that structure) wins by default — though it is still slower than benzene.

### Ortho vs. para ratios
Statistically there are two ortho positions and one para, but steric hindrance often favors para, especially with bulky groups or electrophiles. Nitration of toluene: ~59% ortho, ~37% para, ~4% meta. Nitration of *tert*-butylbenzene: ~16% ortho, ~75% para. Para isomers are often easier to isolate (higher melting point, more symmetrical).

### Multiple substituents
- When directing effects reinforce each other, the outcome is clear.
- When they conflict, the **more strongly activating** group generally controls.
- Substitution rarely occurs between two groups that are meta to each other (steric hindrance).
- Example: *p*-methylanisole nitrates ortho to –OCH₃ (stronger activator).

### Controlling reactivity: protecting the amine
Aniline reacts uncontrollably with Br₂ (giving 2,4,6-tribromoaniline instantly) and cannot undergo Friedel–Crafts reactions. Converting it to **acetanilide** (–NHCOCH₃, moderate activator) tames its reactivity and increases para selectivity; hydrolysis afterward regenerates the amine. (Paracetamol/acetaminophen is *N*-(4-hydroxyphenyl)acetamide.)

## 6. Synthesis Strategy for Substituted Benzenes

The **order of reactions** matters because each group directs the next.

**Example 6.1 — *m*-bromonitrobenzene:** nitrate first (–NO₂ is a meta director), then brominate → meta product. (Brominating first would give mainly *o*- and *p*-bromonitrobenzene.)

**Example 6.2 — *p*-nitrobenzoic acid:** start from toluene; nitrate (ortho/para director; separate the para isomer); then oxidize the methyl group with KMnO₄ to –COOH. (Oxidizing first would give benzoic acid, a meta director.)

**Example 6.3 — *m*-propylbenzene derivatives:** use Friedel–Crafts **acylation** (giving a meta-directing ketone), perform the next substitution meta, then reduce the ketone to the alkyl group.

**Example 6.4 — Changing a group's directing nature:** –NO₂ (meta) can be reduced to –NH₂ (ortho/para); an alkyl group (o/p) can be oxidized to –COOH (meta).

## 7. Other Reactions of Aromatic Compounds

### Side-chain reactions
- **Benzylic oxidation:** hot KMnO₄ or Na₂Cr₂O₇/H₂SO₄ oxidizes any alkyl side chain with at least one benzylic H to **–COOH**, regardless of chain length (tert-butyl, lacking benzylic H, is untouched). Industrial: *p*-xylene → terephthalic acid (for PET plastic).
- **Benzylic bromination:** NBS and light/peroxide selectively brominate the benzylic position (via the resonance-stabilized benzylic radical).
- Benzylic halides undergo both SN1 and SN2 readily.

### Reduction
- **Catalytic hydrogenation** of benzene to cyclohexane requires high pressure/temperature or active catalysts (Rh/C) because of aromatic stability.
- **Birch reduction** (Na or Li in liquid NH₃ with an alcohol) gives non-conjugated 1,4-cyclohexadienes.

### Nucleophilic aromatic substitution (SNAr)
Aryl halides normally do not undergo SN1 or SN2. But with strong **electron-withdrawing groups** (especially –NO₂) **ortho or para** to the leaving group, nucleophiles can substitute via an **addition–elimination** mechanism:
1. Nucleophile adds to the carbon bearing the leaving group, forming a resonance-stabilized anion (**Meisenheimer complex**), with negative charge delocalized onto the nitro groups.
2. The leaving group departs, restoring aromaticity.
Example: 1-chloro-2,4-dinitrobenzene + NaOH → 2,4-dinitrophenol (fast, mild conditions). Fluoride is the best leaving group here (F > Cl > Br > I — opposite to SN2), because the rate-determining step is nucleophilic addition, accelerated by fluorine's electronegativity. **Sanger's reagent** (1-fluoro-2,4-dinitrobenzene) was used by Frederick Sanger to identify N-terminal amino acids when sequencing insulin (Nobel 1958).

### Elimination–addition (benzyne)
Unactivated aryl halides react with very strong bases (NaNH₂ in NH₃) via a highly reactive **benzyne** intermediate (a strained "triple bond" in the ring), giving mixtures of products where the nucleophile can end up on the carbon that held the halogen or the adjacent one (shown by ¹⁴C labeling, J. D. Roberts, 1953).

### Arenediazonium salts
Aniline + NaNO₂/HCl at 0–5 °C (diazotization) → **Ar–N₂⁺Cl⁻**. N₂ is an outstanding leaving group, enabling substitutions not possible by EAS:
- **Sandmeyer reactions** (CuCl, CuBr, CuCN) → Ar–Cl, Ar–Br, Ar–CN.
- KI → Ar–I; HBF₄, heat (Schiemann) → Ar–F; H₂O, heat → Ar–OH; H₃PO₂ → Ar–H (removing an amino group after it has served its directing purpose).
- **Azo coupling:** diazonium ions are weak electrophiles that attack activated rings (phenols, anilines) to form **azo dyes** (Ar–N=N–Ar'), brightly colored because of extended conjugation. Examples: methyl orange, Congo red, many food colorings (tartrazine, Allura Red). The first synthetic dye, mauveine, was discovered accidentally by William Perkin (aged 18) in 1856, launching the synthetic chemical industry.

### Cross-coupling reactions
Modern synthesis forms bonds to aromatic rings using palladium catalysis: **Suzuki** (aryl halide + arylboronic acid), **Heck** (aryl halide + alkene), **Negishi** (organozinc), **Buchwald–Hartwig** amination (aryl halide + amine), **Sonogashira** (alkynes). Heck, Negishi and Suzuki shared the 2010 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. These reactions are used to make many pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals and OLED materials.

## 8. Phenols

- Phenol (pKa 10) is ~10⁶ times more acidic than cyclohexanol (pKa ~18) because the phenoxide ion's negative charge is delocalized into the ring. Electron-withdrawing groups at ortho/para positions increase acidity (*p*-nitrophenol pKa 7.2; 2,4,6-trinitrophenol, picric acid, 0.4).
- Phenols dissolve in NaOH (but most do not react with NaHCO₃, unlike carboxylic acids — a useful separation test).
- Highly activated toward EAS; bromine water gives 2,4,6-tribromophenol immediately (white precipitate).
- Oxidation gives **quinones**; hydroquinone/benzoquinone redox pairs appear in photography and biology (ubiquinone in the electron transport chain; vitamin K).
- Phenolic antioxidants (vitamin E, BHT, polyphenols in tea and wine) donate H atoms to trap radicals.
- Famous phenols: phenol itself (Lister's antiseptic, 1867), thymol (thyme), eugenol (cloves), capsaicin (chili peppers), tyrosine, estradiol, bisphenol A, salicylic acid (from willow bark; acetylated to aspirin by Felix Hoffmann at Bayer, 1897).

## 9. Summary

| Concept | Key point |
|---|---|
| Hückel's rule | Cyclic, planar, conjugated, 4n+2 π electrons → aromatic |
| Antiaromatic | 4n π electrons in a cyclic, planar, conjugated system |
| Resonance energy of benzene | ~150 kJ/mol |
| EAS mechanism | E⁺ attack → arenium ion → deprotonation |
| Activators | All ortho/para directors |
| Halogens | Deactivating but ortho/para directing |
| Strong deactivators | Meta directors (–NO₂, –CN, –C=O, –SO₃H, –CF₃, –NR₃⁺) |
| Friedel–Crafts acylation | No rearrangement, no polysubstitution |
| SNAr | Needs EWG ortho/para; F best leaving group |
| Diazonium salts | Convert –NH₂ into many groups; azo dyes |
