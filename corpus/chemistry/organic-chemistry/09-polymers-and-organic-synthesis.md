# Polymer Chemistry and Strategies of Organic Synthesis

This document covers two applied faces of organic chemistry: **polymers** — giant molecules that make up plastics, fibers, rubbers, adhesives and biological macromolecules — and **organic synthesis**, the art and science of building target molecules from simpler starting materials.

## Part I — Polymer Chemistry

### 1. Basic concepts
A **polymer** (Greek *poly*, many; *meros*, parts) is a large molecule (macromolecule) built from many repeating units derived from small molecules called **monomers**.
- Hermann Staudinger proposed (1920) that polymers are long covalent chains, not aggregates of small molecules — controversial at the time; Nobel 1953.
- **Degree of polymerization** ($n$): number of repeat units; typical synthetic polymers have molar masses from 10⁴ to 10⁷ g/mol.
- Synthetic polymers are **polydisperse** (a distribution of chain lengths), characterized by number-average ($M_n$) and weight-average ($M_w$) molar mass; **dispersity** $Đ = M_w/M_n \ge 1$.
- **Homopolymers** contain one type of monomer; **copolymers** contain two or more (random, alternating, block, graft). Examples: ABS (acrylonitrile–butadiene–styrene), SBR (styrene–butadiene rubber, tires), block copolymers in thermoplastic elastomers.

### 2. Chain-growth (addition) polymerization
Monomers (usually alkenes) add one at a time to a growing chain with a reactive end (radical, cation, anion, or metal–carbon bond). No atoms are lost.

**Radical polymerization** (e.g. polystyrene, PVC, LDPE, PMMA):
1. **Initiation:** an initiator (benzoyl peroxide, AIBN) decomposes into radicals that add to a monomer.
2. **Propagation:** the radical chain end adds monomer after monomer (thousands per second), each time regenerating a radical at the chain end (head-to-tail addition producing the more stable radical).
3. **Termination:** two radicals combine or disproportionate.
4. **Chain transfer:** a radical abstracts H from another chain, causing branching (low-density polyethylene has many branches).

**Ionic polymerization:** cationic (isobutylene → butyl rubber, with Lewis acids) or anionic (styrene, butadiene with n-BuLi). Anionic **living polymerization** (Szwarc, 1956) lacks termination, allowing precise chain lengths and block copolymers. Controlled radical methods (ATRP, RAFT) bring similar control to radical polymerization.

**Coordination polymerization (Ziegler–Natta and metallocene catalysts):** titanium or zirconium catalysts with aluminum co-catalysts polymerize ethylene at low pressure into linear, high-density polyethylene (HDPE) and propylene into stereoregular polypropylene (Ziegler and Natta, Nobel 1963).

**Tacticity** (for polymers like polypropylene with a stereocenter in each repeat unit):
- **Isotactic:** all substituents on the same side → crystalline, strong, high melting (~165 °C). Commercial polypropylene.
- **Syndiotactic:** alternating sides → crystalline.
- **Atactic:** random → amorphous, soft, sticky.

**Ring-opening polymerization:** cyclic monomers (caprolactam → nylon-6; lactide → PLA; ethylene oxide → polyethylene glycol; cyclic olefins via metathesis).

### 3. Step-growth (condensation) polymerization
Bifunctional monomers react with each other via functional-group reactions; any two chains can combine. Often a small molecule (H₂O, HCl, CH₃OH) is eliminated.
- **High molar mass requires very high conversion** (Carothers equation: $X_n = \frac{1}{1 - p}$; 99% conversion gives an average of only 100 units) and precise 1:1 stoichiometry.

| Polymer | Monomers | Linkage | Uses |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nylon-6,6 | Hexanedioic (adipic) acid + hexane-1,6-diamine | Amide | Fibers, stockings, ropes, engineering plastics |
| Nylon-6 | Caprolactam (ring-opening) | Amide | Fibers, carpets |
| Kevlar | Terephthaloyl chloride + *p*-phenylenediamine | Aromatic amide (aramid) | Bulletproof vests, high-strength fibers (Stephanie Kwolek, DuPont, 1965) |
| PET (poly(ethylene terephthalate)) | Terephthalic acid + ethylene glycol | Ester | Bottles, polyester fibers (Dacron), films |
| Polycarbonate | Bisphenol A + phosgene (or diphenyl carbonate) | Carbonate | Eyeglass lenses, safety glass, CDs |
| Polyurethanes | Diisocyanates + diols | Urethane (carbamate) | Foams, spandex (Lycra), coatings, adhesives |
| Phenol–formaldehyde (Bakelite) | Phenol + formaldehyde | Methylene bridges (network) | Electrical insulators; first fully synthetic plastic (Leo Baekeland, 1907) |
| Epoxy resins | Bisphenol A diglycidyl ether + amine hardeners | Ether/amine network | Adhesives, composites |
| Silicones | Dichlorodimethylsilane (hydrolysis) | Si–O–Si | Sealants, medical implants, lubricants |
| PLA (poly(lactic acid)) | Lactic acid / lactide | Ester | Biodegradable packaging, 3D printing, sutures |

**Nylon "rope trick":** adipoyl chloride in an organic solvent floated on hexamethylenediamine in water forms nylon at the interface, which can be pulled out as a continuous strand.

### 4. Structure–property relationships
- **Intermolecular forces:** hydrogen bonding between amide groups makes nylons strong and high-melting; polyethylene has only dispersion forces but long chains provide strength.
- **Crystallinity:** regular, linear chains pack into crystalline regions (HDPE, isotactic PP, nylon) → stiffer, stronger, more opaque, higher melting. Branching or atactic structure → amorphous, flexible, transparent.
- **Glass transition temperature ($T_g$):** below $T_g$, amorphous regions are rigid and glassy; above, rubbery and flexible. Polystyrene $T_g$ ≈ 100 °C (rigid at room temperature); polyisoprene rubber $T_g$ ≈ −70 °C (flexible). The *Challenger* disaster (1986) was linked to O-ring rubber losing elasticity in cold weather.
- **Melting temperature ($T_m$):** for crystalline regions.
- **Cross-linking:** covalent bonds between chains create networks.

### 5. Classes of polymers by behavior
- **Thermoplastics:** soften on heating and can be remolded and recycled (polyethylene, polypropylene, PVC, polystyrene, PET, nylon). Most packaging.
- **Thermosets:** irreversibly cross-linked network; do not melt (Bakelite, epoxies, vulcanized rubber, melamine).
- **Elastomers:** lightly cross-linked, with $T_g$ below room temperature; stretch and recover (rubber, silicone, neoprene). **Vulcanization** (Charles Goodyear, 1839) cross-links natural rubber (*cis*-polyisoprene) with sulfur bridges, transforming sticky, temperature-sensitive latex into durable rubber. *Trans*-polyisoprene (gutta-percha) is hard and was used for golf balls and early undersea cable insulation.
- **Fibers:** high-strength, oriented crystalline polymers (nylon, polyester, Kevlar, UHMWPE such as Dyneema).

**Environmental issues:** global plastic production exceeds ~400 million tonnes per year; only ~9% of all plastic ever produced has been recycled. Plastics persist for decades to centuries, fragmenting into **microplastics** found in oceans, soils, and human tissues. Approaches: mechanical recycling (thermoplastics), chemical recycling (depolymerization of PET to monomers; pyrolysis), enzymatic recycling (PETase enzymes discovered in bacteria in 2016, now engineered for faster degradation), biodegradable polymers (PLA, PHAs), and reduced use.

### 7. Biological polymers
- **Proteins:** polyamides of α-amino acids (peptide bonds).
- **Nucleic acids (DNA, RNA):** polyesters of phosphoric acid with sugars (phosphodiester backbone).
- **Polysaccharides:** polyacetals of sugars — cellulose (β-1,4-glucose; the most abundant organic polymer on Earth), starch (α-1,4 with α-1,6 branches), glycogen, chitin.
- **Natural rubber** (polyisoprene), **lignin** (cross-linked phenolic polymer in wood).

## Part II — Strategies of Organic Synthesis

### 8. Goals of synthesis
- Making useful compounds: pharmaceuticals, agrochemicals, materials, dyes, fragrances.
- **Total synthesis** of complex natural products to confirm structures, provide scarce compounds, and develop new methods. Landmarks: urea (Wöhler, 1828); quinine (Woodward and Doering, formal synthesis 1944); strychnine (Woodward, 1954); vitamin B₁₂ (Woodward and Eschenmoser, 1972 — about 100 chemists over 12 years); taxol (Holton and Nicolaou, 1994); palytoxin (Kishi, 1994 — 64 stereocenters); halichondrin B (Kishi, 1992), whose simplified analog eribulin is an anticancer drug.
- Robert Burns Woodward (Nobel 1965) and Elias James Corey (Nobel 1990, who formalized retrosynthetic analysis) are central figures.

### 9. Retrosynthetic analysis
Work **backward** from the target molecule to simpler precursors (symbolized with ⇒, the retrosynthetic arrow):
1. Identify functional groups in the target.
2. **Disconnect** bonds that correspond to reliable reactions, producing idealized fragments called **synthons** (e.g. a nucleophilic carbanion and an electrophilic carbocation).
3. Replace synthons with real reagents (**synthetic equivalents**): e.g. a "CH₃⁻" synthon corresponds to CH₃MgBr; an "R–C⁺=O" synthon to an acid chloride.
4. Repeat until reaching commercially available or easily made starting materials.
5. Evaluate alternative routes: number of steps, yields, selectivity, cost, safety.

**Example — 2-methylhexan-2-ol:** disconnect a C–C bond to the carbinol carbon:
- ⇒ CH₃MgBr + hexan-2-one, or
- ⇒ butylmagnesium bromide + acetone (cheapest), or
- ⇒ 2 CH₃MgBr + methyl pentanoate (ester + 2 equivalents of Grignard).

**Key disconnection patterns:**
- **Alcohols:** C–C bond to the carbinol carbon (Grignard + carbonyl).
- **β-Hydroxy carbonyls / α,β-unsaturated carbonyls:** aldol disconnection.
- **1,3-Dicarbonyls:** Claisen disconnection.
- **1,5-Dicarbonyls:** Michael disconnection.
- **Cyclohexenes:** Diels–Alder disconnection.
- **Alkenes:** Wittig disconnection (C=C ⇒ C=O + ylide).
- **Ethers:** Williamson (C–O bond).
- **Amines:** reductive amination (C–N bond).
- **Aromatic substitution patterns:** consider directing effects and order of introduction.
- **Biaryls:** Suzuki coupling.

### 10. Functional group interconversion (FGI)
Changing one functional group into another without altering the carbon skeleton — e.g. alcohol → alkyl halide → nitrile → carboxylic acid; ketone → alcohol → alkene; nitro → amine. A synthetic chemist must know the "reaction map" connecting functional groups:

```
alkane ← (H₂) alkene ⇄ alkyl halide ⇄ alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid → ester/amide/acid chloride
   alkyne → alkene (Lindlar / Na-NH₃)       alcohol ⇄ ketone           nitrile → acid / amine
   alkyl halide → nitrile, azide, ether, thiol (SN2)    nitro → amine → diazonium → many groups
```

### 11. Selectivity
- **Chemoselectivity:** reacting one functional group in the presence of others (NaBH₄ reduces ketones but not esters; PCC oxidizes alcohols without touching alkenes).
- **Regioselectivity:** choosing among positions (Markovnikov vs. anti-Markovnikov; kinetic vs. thermodynamic enolates; ortho/para vs. meta in EAS).
- **Stereoselectivity:** controlling 3D outcome — diastereoselectivity (e.g. Felkin–Anh and chelation models for addition to chiral carbonyls) and enantioselectivity (asymmetric catalysis, chiral auxiliaries, enzymes, the chiral pool).

### 12. Protecting groups
When a molecule contains a functional group that would interfere, temporarily mask it:

| Group protected | Protecting group | Installed with | Removed by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Alcohol | TBS (silyl ether) | TBSCl, imidazole | F⁻ (TBAF) or acid |
| Alcohol | Benzyl ether (Bn) | NaH, BnBr | H₂, Pd/C |
| Alcohol | THP acetal | Dihydropyran, H⁺ | Aqueous acid |
| Aldehyde/ketone | Acetal (1,3-dioxolane) | Ethylene glycol, H⁺ | Aqueous acid |
| Amine | Boc (carbamate) | Boc₂O | Acid (TFA) |
| Amine | Fmoc | Fmoc-Cl | Base (piperidine) |
| Amine | Cbz | Cbz-Cl | H₂, Pd/C |
| Carboxylic acid | Methyl or tert-butyl ester | MeOH/H⁺ or isobutylene/H⁺ | Hydroxide or acid (TFA) |

**Orthogonal protection** (groups removable under different conditions) is essential in multi-step synthesis, especially of peptides and oligonucleotides. **Solid-phase peptide synthesis** (Bruce Merrifield, Nobel 1984) builds peptides on resin beads using Fmoc or Boc chemistry and is automated; solid-phase phosphoramidite chemistry similarly builds DNA oligonucleotides (used for PCR primers and gene synthesis).

### 13. Efficiency and green chemistry
- **Linear vs. convergent synthesis:** a linear 10-step synthesis at 80% per step gives 10.7% overall; a convergent route (making two halves separately, then joining) improves overall yield and reduces the longest linear sequence.
- **Step economy, atom economy and redox economy.**
- **Atom economy** (Barry Trost): $\frac{\text{MW of product}}{\sum\text{MW of reactants}}\times100\%$. Diels–Alder and hydrogenation: 100%. Wittig: poor (stoichiometric Ph₃PO waste).
- **E-factor** (Roger Sheldon): kg waste per kg product — bulk chemicals ~1–5; pharmaceuticals ~25–100+.
- **The 12 principles of green chemistry** (Paul Anastas and John Warner, 1998) include: prevent waste; maximize atom economy; use less hazardous reagents and safer solvents (water, supercritical CO₂, ethanol instead of chlorinated solvents); design energy-efficient processes; use renewable feedstocks; prefer catalytic over stoichiometric reagents; avoid unnecessary protecting groups; design products to degrade; real-time monitoring; inherently safer chemistry to prevent accidents.
- **Example — ibuprofen:** the original Boots synthesis (6 steps, ~40% atom economy) was replaced by the BHC process (3 catalytic steps, ~77% atom economy, approaching ~99% with recovery of acetic acid byproduct).
- **Biocatalysis:** engineered enzymes (directed evolution; Frances Arnold, Nobel 2018) now perform key steps in drug manufacturing, e.g. the transaminase route to sitagliptin.
- **Flow chemistry** and **automation/AI-assisted retrosynthesis** are increasingly used in modern synthesis planning.
