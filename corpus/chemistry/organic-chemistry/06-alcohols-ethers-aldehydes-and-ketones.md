# Alcohols, Ethers, Aldehydes and Ketones

Oxygen-containing functional groups dominate organic and biological chemistry. Alcohols and ethers contain C–O single bonds; aldehydes and ketones contain the **carbonyl group** (C=O), arguably the single most important functional group in organic chemistry. Sugars are polyhydroxy aldehydes and ketones; many hormones, flavors and drugs contain these groups; and carbonyl chemistry provides most of the reactions used to build carbon skeletons.

## Part I — Alcohols

### 1. Structure and properties
- The O–H group is attached to an sp³ carbon. Classified as **primary** (RCH₂OH), **secondary** (R₂CHOH) or **tertiary** (R₃COH).
- C–O–H angle ~108.5° (oxygen sp³ with two lone pairs).
- **Hydrogen bonding** gives high boiling points: methanol 65 °C vs. ethane −89 °C (similar mass); ethanol 78 °C vs. dimethyl ether −24 °C.
- Methanol, ethanol and propanols are miscible with water; solubility decreases as the hydrocarbon portion grows (roughly one OH can solubilize ~4 carbons). **Diols** (glycols) and **triols** (glycerol) are more water-soluble and viscous.

**Important alcohols:**
- **Methanol** (wood alcohol): fuel, solvent, feedstock (formaldehyde, acetic acid). Toxic: metabolized to formaldehyde and formic acid, causing blindness and death (~30 mL can be fatal). Antidotes (fomepizole or ethanol) block alcohol dehydrogenase.
- **Ethanol:** beverages (fermentation of sugars by yeast: C₆H₁₂O₆ → 2C₂H₅OH + 2CO₂), fuel (E10, E85), solvent, antiseptic (70% is more effective than 95% because water aids protein denaturation and penetration). Industrial ethanol is made by hydrating ethylene.
- **Isopropyl alcohol** (propan-2-ol): rubbing alcohol, disinfectant.
- **Ethylene glycol:** antifreeze, PET monomer (toxic; tastes sweet).
- **Glycerol** (glycerin): backbone of triglycerides and phospholipids; humectant in cosmetics; nitroglycerin precursor.
- **Cholesterol, menthol, retinol** (vitamin A) — biologically important alcohols.

### 2. Acidity and basicity
- Alcohols are weak acids (pKa 15.5–18): methanol 15.5, ethanol 16, *tert*-butanol 18 (bulkier alkoxides are less well solvated, so less acidic). React with Na, K or NaH to form **alkoxides**: 2ROH + 2Na → 2RO⁻Na⁺ + H₂.
- Electron-withdrawing groups increase acidity: 2,2,2-trifluoroethanol (pKa 12.4).
- Alcohols are also weak bases: in strong acid, they are protonated to **oxonium ions** (ROH₂⁺), turning –OH into a good leaving group (water).

### 3. Preparation of alcohols
1. **Hydration of alkenes:** acid-catalyzed (Markovnikov), oxymercuration (Markovnikov, no rearrangement), hydroboration–oxidation (anti-Markovnikov, syn).
2. **Substitution:** RX + OH⁻ (SN2, for primary halides).
3. **Reduction of carbonyl compounds:**
   - Aldehydes → primary alcohols; ketones → secondary alcohols (NaBH₄ or LiAlH₄, or H₂/catalyst).
   - Carboxylic acids and esters → primary alcohols (LiAlH₄; NaBH₄ is too weak).
4. **Grignard and organolithium additions** (C–C bond formation — see below).
5. **Fermentation** (ethanol, butanol).

### 4. Organometallic reagents: Grignard and organolithium
**Grignard reagents** (Victor Grignard, 1900; Nobel 1912): $\text{R–X} + \text{Mg}\xrightarrow{\text{dry ether or THF}}\text{R–MgX}$.
The carbon–magnesium bond is highly polarized (C δ⁻), so the carbon behaves as a **carbanion**: a powerful **nucleophile** and a very strong **base**.
- Must be prepared and used under **anhydrous** conditions: water, alcohols, carboxylic acids, amines and terminal alkynes destroy them by protonation (RMgX + H₂O → RH + Mg(OH)X). (This can be used deliberately to introduce deuterium: RMgX + D₂O → RD.)
- Organolithium reagents (R–Li, e.g. n-BuLi) are similar and even more reactive.

**Grignard reactions with carbonyl compounds** (followed by acidic workup):

| Carbonyl substrate | Product |
|---|---|
| Formaldehyde (H₂C=O) | Primary alcohol (RCH₂OH) |
| Other aldehydes (R'CHO) | Secondary alcohol (RR'CHOH) |
| Ketones (R'₂C=O) | Tertiary alcohol (RR'₂COH) |
| Esters (R'COOR'') | Tertiary alcohol (two R groups add; ketone intermediate is more reactive than the ester) |
| CO₂ | Carboxylic acid (RCOOH) |
| Epoxides (ethylene oxide) | Primary alcohol with two extra carbons (RCH₂CH₂OH) |
| Nitriles | Ketones (after hydrolysis of the imine) |

**Retrosynthesis example:** to make 2-phenylbutan-2-ol, (C₆H₅)C(OH)(CH₃)(CH₂CH₃), disconnect any of the three C–C bonds to the carbinol carbon: PhMgBr + butan-2-one; CH₃MgBr + propiophenone; or CH₃CH₂MgBr + acetophenone.

### 5. Reactions of alcohols
- **Conversion to alkyl halides:** HX (3° via SN1; 1° via SN2); SOCl₂ (→ RCl); PBr₃ (→ RBr).
- **Conversion to sulfonate esters:** TsCl or MsCl with pyridine → excellent leaving groups for SN2/E2.
- **Dehydration:** H₂SO₄/H₃PO₄ + heat → alkenes (E1 for 2°/3°, Zaitsev; rearrangements possible). At lower temperature with excess primary alcohol, intermolecular dehydration gives symmetrical ethers (2EtOH → Et₂O + H₂O at ~140 °C).
- **Esterification:** with carboxylic acids (Fischer esterification, acid catalyst, reversible) or acid chlorides/anhydrides.
- **Oxidation** (one of the most important transformations):

| Alcohol | Mild oxidant (PCC, Swern, Dess–Martin, TEMPO) | Strong oxidant (CrO₃/H₂SO₄ "Jones", Na₂Cr₂O₇, KMnO₄) |
|---|---|---|
| Primary | **Aldehyde** | **Carboxylic acid** (via aldehyde hydrate) |
| Secondary | Ketone | Ketone |
| Tertiary | No reaction (no H on the carbinol carbon) | No reaction (under normal conditions) |

PCC = pyridinium chlorochromate (in CH₂Cl₂, anhydrous — stops at the aldehyde). Swern oxidation uses DMSO and oxalyl chloride (chromium-free). Chromium(VI) is toxic and carcinogenic, motivating "greener" alternatives.

**Biological oxidation:** alcohol dehydrogenase uses NAD⁺ to oxidize ethanol to acetaldehyde (responsible for hangover symptoms), which aldehyde dehydrogenase oxidizes to acetic acid. Disulfiram (Antabuse) blocks the second enzyme, causing acetaldehyde buildup and nausea — used to treat alcoholism. A common genetic variant of aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH2*2), frequent in East Asian populations, causes the "alcohol flush" reaction.

## Part II — Ethers and Epoxides

### 6. Ethers
- R–O–R'; C–O–C angle ~110°. No O–H, so no hydrogen bonding between ether molecules → boiling points close to alkanes of similar mass (diethyl ether bp 35 °C). They can accept hydrogen bonds, so small ethers are somewhat water-soluble (diethyl ether: ~6.9 g/100 mL).
- **Relatively unreactive** → excellent solvents (diethyl ether, THF, 1,4-dioxane, DME). Diethyl ether was used as a surgical anesthetic (first public demonstration by William Morton, 1846).
- **Hazards:** highly flammable; on standing in air, ethers form explosive **peroxides**.
- **Cleavage:** only by strong acids (HBr, HI): R–O–R' + HI → RI + R'OH (SN2 at the less hindered carbon, or SN1 at tertiary carbons).
- **Synthesis:** **Williamson ether synthesis** (alkoxide + primary alkyl halide, SN2); alkoxymercuration of alkenes; acid-catalyzed condensation of primary alcohols.
- **Crown ethers** (cyclic polyethers such as 18-crown-6) bind specific metal cations in their cavities (K⁺ fits 18-crown-6), dissolving salts like KMnO₄ in nonpolar solvents ("purple benzene") and enhancing anion reactivity (phase-transfer catalysis). Charles Pedersen, Donald Cram and Jean-Marie Lehn shared the 1987 Nobel Prize for host–guest chemistry.
- **MTBE** (methyl *tert*-butyl ether) was a gasoline additive, phased out in many places due to groundwater contamination.

### 7. Epoxides (oxiranes)
Three-membered cyclic ethers with ~110 kJ/mol ring strain — therefore **much more reactive** than ordinary ethers.
- **Synthesis:** alkene + peroxy acid (mCPBA), syn and stereospecific; or intramolecular Williamson reaction of halohydrins with base.
- **Ring opening under basic/nucleophilic conditions** (RO⁻, OH⁻, CN⁻, RMgX, LiAlH₄, amines): SN2 at the **less substituted** carbon, with inversion; anti stereochemistry.
- **Ring opening under acidic conditions** (H₃O⁺, ROH/H⁺, HX): the protonated epoxide is attacked at the **more substituted** carbon (which bears more partial positive charge, SN1-like character), still with backside attack (anti).
- Products: *trans*-1,2-diols, β-amino alcohols, β-alkoxy alcohols — common in drugs (β-blockers like propranolol are made by epoxide opening with an amine).
- **Industrial:** ethylene oxide (~30 million tonnes/year) for ethylene glycol, surfactants and sterilization of medical equipment; propylene oxide for polyurethanes. **Epoxy resins** (adhesives, coatings, composites) cure by epoxide ring-opening polymerization.
- **Biology:** cytochrome P450 enzymes epoxidize aromatic compounds; the resulting epoxides can be detoxified (epoxide hydrolase, glutathione) or, as with benzo[a]pyrene diol epoxide, attack DNA and cause cancer.

## Part III — Aldehydes and Ketones

### 8. The carbonyl group
- C=O: carbon sp² and trigonal planar (120°); a σ bond and a π bond; bond length 122 pm.
- **Strongly polarized:** oxygen is δ⁻, carbon δ⁺ (dipole ~2.3–2.9 D). The carbonyl carbon is **electrophilic**; the oxygen is weakly basic and nucleophilic.
- Resonance: C=O ↔ C⁺–O⁻.
- **Aldehydes** (RCHO) have at least one H on the carbonyl carbon; **ketones** (R₂CO) have two carbon groups.
- Polar, but no O–H → boiling points between alkanes and alcohols (propanal 49 °C, acetone 56 °C, propan-1-ol 97 °C, butane −0.5 °C). Small ones are water-soluble (acetone is miscible).

**Notable examples:** formaldehyde (preservative, resins; carcinogen), acetaldehyde, acetone (solvent; a ketone body in diabetes), benzaldehyde (almond), cinnamaldehyde (cinnamon), vanillin (vanilla), citral (lemon), carvone (spearmint), camphor, muscone (musk), steroid hormones (testosterone, progesterone, cortisone), and the open-chain forms of sugars (glucose is an aldehyde; fructose is a ketone).

**Reactivity:** aldehydes are more reactive than ketones toward nucleophiles because (1) **sterics** — an H is smaller than an alkyl group; (2) **electronics** — alkyl groups donate electron density, stabilizing the partial positive charge of ketones.

### 9. Nucleophilic addition: the core reaction
**Basic/neutral conditions** (strong nucleophile): the nucleophile attacks the carbonyl carbon, the π electrons move to oxygen giving a tetrahedral **alkoxide**, which is protonated.
**Acidic conditions** (weak nucleophile): acid protonates the carbonyl oxygen, making the carbon much more electrophilic; the weak nucleophile then attacks.

The carbon changes from sp² (trigonal planar) to sp³ (tetrahedral). Attack can occur from either face of the planar carbonyl: if a stereocenter forms, achiral reactants give a racemate.

### 10. Addition reactions of aldehydes and ketones

| Nucleophile | Reagent | Product | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hydride (H⁻) | NaBH₄ (in MeOH/EtOH) or LiAlH₄ (then H₃O⁺) | 1° alcohol (from aldehyde), 2° alcohol (from ketone) | NaBH₄ is milder and selective for aldehydes/ketones |
| Carbanion | RMgX or RLi, then H₃O⁺ | Alcohols | C–C bond formation |
| Water | H₂O (acid or base cat.) | Hydrate (gem-diol) | Reversible; formaldehyde ~99.9% hydrated; acetone ~0.1%; chloral hydrate is stable |
| Alcohol | ROH, H⁺ | Hemiacetal, then acetal | Acetals are protecting groups; sugars form cyclic hemiacetals |
| Cyanide | HCN/NaCN | Cyanohydrin | Extends chain; hydrolysis → α-hydroxy acid. Some plants (bitter almonds, cassava) store cyanohydrin glycosides |
| Primary amine | RNH₂, mild acid (pH ~4–5) | **Imine** (Schiff base, C=N–R) | Reversible; biological importance |
| Secondary amine | R₂NH, H⁺ | **Enamine** (C=C–NR₂) | Nucleophilic carbon (Stork enamine reactions) |
| Hydroxylamine | NH₂OH | Oxime | Crystalline derivatives; Beckmann rearrangement |
| Hydrazine | H₂NNH₂ | Hydrazone | Wolff–Kishner reduction (KOH, heat → CH₂) |
| 2,4-DNP | 2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine | 2,4-DNP hydrazone (yellow/orange/red precipitate) | Classical test for aldehydes/ketones |
| Phosphorus ylide | Ph₃P=CHR (Wittig reagent) | **Alkene** | C=O replaced by C=C (Wittig, Nobel 1979) |

#### Acetals and protecting groups
Aldehyde/ketone + 2 ROH (acid catalyst, water removed) ⇌ acetal + H₂O. Mechanism: protonation, alcohol addition → **hemiacetal**; protonation of OH, loss of water → oxocarbenium ion; second alcohol adds → **acetal**.
- Acetals are stable to bases, nucleophiles, hydrides and Grignard reagents, but hydrolyze back in aqueous acid.
- **Protecting group strategy:** to reduce an ester with LiAlH₄ in the presence of a ketone, first convert the ketone to a cyclic acetal with ethylene glycol, perform the reduction, then remove the acetal with aqueous acid.
- **Sugars:** glucose exists >99% as a cyclic hemiacetal (pyranose ring) formed by intramolecular attack of the C5–OH on the C1 aldehyde. The new stereocenter at C1 (the **anomeric carbon**) gives α and β anomers, which interconvert in solution (**mutarotation**; equilibrium ~36% α, ~64% β). Glycosidic bonds linking sugars in disaccharides and polysaccharides are acetals.

#### Imines in biology
- **Vision:** retinal forms a protonated imine (Schiff base) with a lysine residue in opsin.
- **Pyridoxal phosphate** (vitamin B₆) forms imines with amino acids in transamination reactions.
- Aldolase and many enzymes use lysine-derived imines (enamine catalysis).
- **Reductive amination** (imine formation followed by reduction with NaBH₃CN or NaBH(OAc)₃) is a key method for synthesizing amines, used extensively in pharmaceutical manufacturing.

#### The Wittig reaction
$$\text{R}_2\text{C=O} + \text{Ph}_3\text{P=CHR}'\to\text{R}_2\text{C=CHR}' + \text{Ph}_3\text{P=O}$$
The ylide is made from triphenylphosphine + alkyl halide (SN2) → phosphonium salt, then a strong base (n-BuLi). The driving force is the very strong P=O bond. It places the C=C exactly where the C=O was — no regiochemical ambiguity. Unstabilized ylides tend to give *Z*-alkenes; stabilized ylides (and the Horner–Wadsworth–Emmons variant) give *E*-alkenes. Used industrially to synthesize vitamin A.

### 11. Oxidation and reduction of aldehydes and ketones
- **Aldehydes are easily oxidized** to carboxylic acids (even by air). Ketones resist oxidation (no H on the carbonyl carbon).
- **Tollens' test:** Ag(NH₃)₂⁺ oxidizes aldehydes, depositing a **silver mirror** on the glass. **Fehling's/Benedict's tests:** Cu²⁺ (blue) is reduced to Cu₂O (brick-red precipitate) by aldehydes and α-hydroxy ketones — historically used to detect glucose in urine ("reducing sugars").
- **Baeyer–Villiger oxidation:** peroxy acids convert ketones into esters (and cyclic ketones into lactones) by inserting O; migratory aptitude: 3° alkyl > 2° alkyl ≈ phenyl > 1° alkyl > methyl.
- **Reductions:** NaBH₄/LiAlH₄ → alcohols; catalytic hydrogenation → alcohols; **Clemmensen** (Zn(Hg)/HCl) and **Wolff–Kishner** (N₂H₄/KOH, heat) → complete reduction of C=O to CH₂.
- **Biological reductions** use NADH/NADPH as hydride donors, often enantioselectively.

### 12. α-Hydrogen chemistry (preview)
Hydrogens on carbons adjacent to the carbonyl (α-hydrogens) are acidic (pKa ~19–20 for ketones, ~17 for aldehydes) because the conjugate base — the **enolate** — is resonance-stabilized. Aldehydes and ketones are in equilibrium with their **enol** tautomers (keto–enol tautomerism). Enols and enolates are nucleophilic at the α-carbon, enabling halogenation, alkylation, and the **aldol reaction**. (See the next document.)

### 13. Spectroscopic signatures
- **IR:** strong C=O stretch ~1715 cm⁻¹ (ketones), ~1725 cm⁻¹ (aldehydes); conjugation lowers it to ~1690 cm⁻¹. Aldehydes show two weak C–H stretches at ~2720 and ~2820 cm⁻¹ (Fermi doublet). Alcohols: broad O–H stretch 3200–3550 cm⁻¹; C–O stretch 1050–1250 cm⁻¹.
- **¹H NMR:** aldehyde H at δ 9–10 ppm (very distinctive); α-hydrogens δ 2.0–2.5; H on carbinol carbon (CH–OH) δ 3.3–4.0; OH proton variable (δ 1–5), exchanges with D₂O.
- **¹³C NMR:** carbonyl carbon δ 190–215 ppm; C–O of alcohols/ethers δ 50–80.
