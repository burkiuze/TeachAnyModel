# Organic Chemistry Foundations: Structure, Functional Groups, Nomenclature and Acidity

**Organic chemistry** is the chemistry of carbon compounds. Once thought to require a "vital force" from living organisms, it became a branch of ordinary chemistry after Friedrich Wöhler synthesized urea from inorganic ammonium cyanate in 1828. Over 100 million organic compounds are known — far more than all compounds of the other elements combined. They include fuels, plastics, pharmaceuticals, dyes, pesticides, flavors and fragrances, and all the molecules of life: proteins, carbohydrates, lipids and nucleic acids.

## 1. Why Carbon Is Special

1. **Four valence electrons** → forms four covalent bonds (tetravalent), allowing complex three-dimensional structures.
2. **Catenation:** carbon bonds strongly to itself (C–C bond energy ~348 kJ/mol), forming long chains, branches and rings. Silicon's Si–Si bonds are much weaker (~226 kJ/mol) and reactive toward oxygen.
3. **Multiple bonds:** C=C, C≡C, C=O, C≡N, made possible by carbon's small size and good p-orbital overlap.
4. **Intermediate electronegativity** (2.55): bonds to H, O, N, S, halogens and metals are all stable but reactive enough to be useful.
5. **Kinetic stability:** C–C and C–H bonds are strong and relatively unreactive under ambient conditions, so complex molecules persist.

## 2. Hybridization and Geometry of Carbon

| Hybridization | Bonds | Geometry | Bond angle | Example |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| sp³ | 4 σ | Tetrahedral | 109.5° | Methane CH₄, ethane |
| sp² | 3 σ + 1 π | Trigonal planar | 120° | Ethene C₂H₄, benzene, C=O carbon |
| sp | 2 σ + 2 π | Linear | 180° | Ethyne C₂H₂, nitriles C≡N |

**Rule of thumb:** count the number of atoms attached plus lone pairs (the "steric number"): 4 → sp³, 3 → sp², 2 → sp. Heteroatoms follow the same logic (the N in NH₃ is sp³; the O in a carbonyl is often described as sp²). Atoms with lone pairs adjacent to π systems typically become sp² to allow conjugation (e.g. the N in amides and pyrrole).

| Bond | Length (pm) | Energy (kJ/mol) |
|---|---|---|
| C–C (sp³–sp³) | 154 | 348 |
| C=C | 134 | 614 |
| C≡C | 120 | 839 |
| C–H (sp³) | 109 | 413 |
| C–H (sp) | 106 | ~556 (ethyne) |

## 3. Representing Organic Molecules

- **Molecular formula:** C₂H₆O (ambiguous — ethanol or dimethyl ether).
- **Condensed formula:** CH₃CH₂OH, (CH₃)₂CHCH₃.
- **Lewis/Kekulé structure:** all atoms and bonds drawn.
- **Skeletal (bond-line) structure:** the standard in organic chemistry. Lines represent C–C bonds; every vertex and line end is a carbon; hydrogens on carbon are implied (enough to give four bonds); heteroatoms and their hydrogens are shown explicitly.
- **3D representations:** wedge–dash drawings (solid wedge toward the viewer, hashed wedge away), Newman projections (looking down a bond), Fischer projections (for stereocenters, especially in sugars), and Haworth projections (cyclic sugars).

### Isomers
Compounds with the same molecular formula but different structures:
- **Constitutional (structural) isomers:** different connectivity. C₄H₁₀: butane and 2-methylpropane (isobutane). C₂H₆O: ethanol and dimethyl ether.
- **Stereoisomers:** same connectivity, different spatial arrangement (see the stereochemistry document).

The number of constitutional isomers grows explosively: C₅H₁₂ has 3; C₁₀H₂₂ has 75; C₂₀H₄₂ has 366 319; C₄₀H₈₂ has about 62 trillion.

### Degree of unsaturation (index of hydrogen deficiency)
For a formula CₙHₕNₙXₓOₒ:
$$\text{DoU} = \frac{2C + 2 + N - H - X}{2}$$
(oxygen and sulfur don't count). Each ring or π bond contributes 1. Example: benzene C₆H₆: $(12 + 2 - 6)/2 = 4$ (one ring + three π bonds). Caffeine C₈H₁₀N₄O₂: $(16 + 2 + 4 - 10)/2 = 6$.

## 4. Functional Groups

A **functional group** is a specific arrangement of atoms that determines a molecule's characteristic reactions. Molecules with the same functional group react similarly regardless of size.

| Class | Functional group | General formula | Example (IUPAC / common name) | Suffix / prefix |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Alkane | C–C, C–H only | CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | CH₃CH₃ ethane | -ane |
| Alkene | C=C | CₙH₂ₙ | CH₂=CH₂ ethene (ethylene) | -ene |
| Alkyne | C≡C | CₙH₂ₙ₋₂ | HC≡CH ethyne (acetylene) | -yne |
| Arene (aromatic) | Benzene ring | Ar–H | C₆H₆ benzene; C₆H₅CH₃ toluene | -benzene |
| Haloalkane (alkyl halide) | C–X (X = F, Cl, Br, I) | R–X | CH₃CH₂Cl chloroethane | halo- |
| Alcohol | –OH on sp³ C | R–OH | CH₃CH₂OH ethanol | -ol / hydroxy- |
| Phenol | –OH on aromatic ring | Ar–OH | C₆H₅OH phenol | -phenol |
| Ether | C–O–C | R–O–R' | CH₃CH₂OCH₂CH₃ ethoxyethane (diethyl ether) | alkoxy- |
| Thiol | –SH | R–SH | CH₃SH methanethiol | -thiol |
| Amine | –NH₂, –NHR, –NR₂ | R–NH₂ | CH₃NH₂ methanamine (methylamine) | -amine / amino- |
| Aldehyde | –CHO | R–CHO | CH₃CHO ethanal (acetaldehyde) | -al / formyl- |
| Ketone | C–CO–C | R–CO–R' | CH₃COCH₃ propanone (acetone) | -one / oxo- |
| Carboxylic acid | –COOH | R–COOH | CH₃COOH ethanoic acid (acetic acid) | -oic acid |
| Ester | –COOR' | R–COO–R' | CH₃COOCH₂CH₃ ethyl ethanoate (ethyl acetate) | -oate |
| Amide | –CONH₂ | R–CONR'₂ | CH₃CONH₂ ethanamide (acetamide) | -amide |
| Acid chloride | –COCl | R–COCl | CH₃COCl ethanoyl chloride (acetyl chloride) | -oyl chloride |
| Acid anhydride | –CO–O–CO– | (RCO)₂O | (CH₃CO)₂O ethanoic anhydride (acetic anhydride) | -oic anhydride |
| Nitrile | –C≡N | R–CN | CH₃CN ethanenitrile (acetonitrile) | -nitrile / cyano- |
| Nitro | –NO₂ | R–NO₂ | CH₃NO₂ nitromethane | nitro- |

**R** denotes a generic alkyl group; **Ar** an aryl (aromatic) group.

### Physical properties and functional groups
- **Alkanes** are nonpolar, insoluble in water, less dense than water; boiling points rise with chain length (dispersion forces).
- **Alcohols** hydrogen-bond: ethanol (bp 78 °C) vs. propane (similar mass, bp −42 °C). Small alcohols are water-miscible; solubility drops as the hydrocarbon chain grows (1-hexanol: ~6 g/L).
- **Carboxylic acids** form hydrogen-bonded dimers, giving high boiling points (acetic acid 118 °C).
- **Amines** hydrogen-bond (weaker than alcohols); low-mass amines smell fishy (putrescine and cadaverine smell of decay).
- **Esters** often smell fruity (isoamyl acetate: banana; ethyl butanoate: pineapple; methyl salicylate: wintergreen; octyl acetate: orange).
- **Aldehydes and ketones** are polar but cannot hydrogen-bond with themselves; bp between alkanes and alcohols. Many are fragrant (benzaldehyde: almond; cinnamaldehyde: cinnamon; vanillin: vanilla; carvone: spearmint/caraway).

## 5. IUPAC Nomenclature

The International Union of Pure and Applied Chemistry provides systematic rules so every compound has a unique name.

### Alkane roots

| Carbons | Root | Alkane | | Carbons | Root | Alkane |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | meth- | methane | | 6 | hex- | hexane |
| 2 | eth- | ethane | | 7 | hept- | heptane |
| 3 | prop- | propane | | 8 | oct- | octane |
| 4 | but- | butane | | 9 | non- | nonane |
| 5 | pent- | pentane | | 10 | dec- | decane |

Higher: undecane (11), dodecane (12), icosane (20), triacontane (30).

### Substituent (alkyl) groups
Methyl (–CH₃), ethyl (–C₂H₅), propyl (–CH₂CH₂CH₃), isopropyl (–CH(CH₃)₂), butyl, sec-butyl (–CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃), isobutyl (–CH₂CH(CH₃)₂), tert-butyl (–C(CH₃)₃), vinyl (–CH=CH₂), allyl (–CH₂CH=CH₂), phenyl (–C₆H₅), benzyl (–CH₂C₆H₅).

### Classification of carbons
- **Primary (1°):** bonded to one other carbon.
- **Secondary (2°):** bonded to two carbons.
- **Tertiary (3°):** bonded to three carbons.
- **Quaternary (4°):** bonded to four carbons.
The same terms apply to the hydrogens on them and to alcohols and alkyl halides (by the carbon bearing the group). Amines are classified differently — by the number of carbons on the **nitrogen**.

### Naming procedure
1. **Identify the principal functional group** (highest priority) — it determines the suffix.
2. **Find the parent chain:** the longest chain containing the principal group (and, under the 2013 recommendations, maximal length first for acyclic chains; older rules gave priority to including multiple bonds).
3. **Number the chain** to give the principal group the lowest locant; then multiple bonds; then substituents (first point of difference).
4. **Name and locate substituents** as prefixes, in **alphabetical order** (ignoring multiplying prefixes di-, tri-, tetra- and hyphenated prefixes like sec-, tert-, but including "iso").
5. **Assemble:** locants–prefixes–parent–suffix. Use commas between numbers and hyphens between numbers and letters.

### Functional group priority (highest first)
Carboxylic acid > anhydride > ester > acid halide > amide > nitrile > aldehyde > ketone > alcohol > thiol > amine > alkene ≈ alkyne > ether > halide/nitro (prefix only).

### Examples
- CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃: **2-methylbutane**.
- (CH₃)₃CCH₂CH(CH₃)₂: **2,2,4-trimethylpentane** ("isooctane", the 100-point reference for octane ratings).
- CH₃CH₂CH(Cl)CH(CH₃)CH₃: **3-chloro-2-methylpentane** (alphabetical: chloro before methyl; numbering gives lowest set {2,3}).
- CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃: **but-1-ene** (older: 1-butene).
- CH₃CH=CHCH₃: **but-2-ene** (exists as *cis*/*trans* or (*Z*)/(*E*) isomers).
- CH₃CH(OH)CH₃: **propan-2-ol** (isopropyl alcohol, rubbing alcohol).
- CH₃COCH₂CH₃: **butan-2-one** (methyl ethyl ketone, MEK).
- CH₃CH₂CH₂CHO: **butanal**.
- HOCH₂CH₂OH: **ethane-1,2-diol** (ethylene glycol, antifreeze).
- CH₃CH(NH₂)COOH: **2-aminopropanoic acid** (alanine).
- CH₂=CHCH₂OH: **prop-2-en-1-ol** (allyl alcohol).
- CH₃CH₂COOCH₃: **methyl propanoate** (esters: alkyl group from the alcohol + "-oate" from the acid).
- Cyclic: **cyclohexane**, **methylcyclopentane**, **cyclohex-2-en-1-one**, **1,3-dimethylcyclohexane**.
- Aromatic: **toluene** (methylbenzene), **phenol**, **aniline** (benzenamine), **benzoic acid**, **benzaldehyde**, **styrene** (ethenylbenzene). Disubstituted benzenes: **ortho** (1,2-), **meta** (1,3-), **para** (1,4-), e.g. *p*-xylene = 1,4-dimethylbenzene.

### Common (trivial) names still widely used
Formaldehyde (methanal), acetone (propanone), acetic acid (ethanoic acid), formic acid (methanoic acid), acetylene (ethyne), ethylene (ethene), propylene (propene), isoprene (2-methylbuta-1,3-diene), chloroform (trichloromethane), glycerol (propane-1,2,3-triol), urea (carbonyl diamide), diethyl ether (ethoxyethane).

## 6. Resonance and Curved Arrows

### Curved-arrow notation
Organic chemists track electron movement with curved arrows:
- **Full arrow** (double-headed): movement of an electron **pair**, from a source (lone pair or bond) to a sink (atom or bond).
- **Fishhook arrow** (single-headed): movement of a single electron (radical reactions).
Arrows always start at electrons and point to where they go. They are used both for resonance structures and for reaction mechanisms.

### Resonance
When a molecule cannot be represented by a single Lewis structure, it is a **resonance hybrid** of contributing structures that differ only in electron placement (atoms do not move).

**Rules for significant contributors:**
1. Full octets on second-row atoms (most important).
2. Fewest formal charges.
3. Negative charge on the more electronegative atom; positive charge on the less electronegative atom.
4. Equivalent contributors make equal contributions and give especially large stabilization.

**Examples:**
- **Carboxylate ion** (RCOO⁻): negative charge shared equally by two oxygens; both C–O bonds are identical (~127 pm).
- **Amide:** the nitrogen lone pair delocalizes into the carbonyl, giving the C–N bond partial double-bond character (~40% — restricted rotation, planar amide group). This makes amides poor bases, the peptide bond planar, and amides much less reactive than esters.
- **Allyl cation, radical and anion:** charge or unpaired electron shared between the two end carbons.
- **Enolate ions:** negative charge shared between carbon and oxygen.
- **Benzene:** two equivalent Kekulé structures; aromatic stabilization ~150 kJ/mol.

**Patterns:** a lone pair adjacent to a π bond; a π bond adjacent to a positive charge (allylic cation); a π bond between atoms of different electronegativity (C=O ↔ C⁺–O⁻); conjugated π bonds in rings.

## 7. Electronic Effects

- **Inductive effect:** polarization of σ bonds by electronegative atoms, transmitted along the chain and decreasing rapidly with distance. Halogens, –NO₂, –CN, –CF₃, carbonyl groups are electron-withdrawing (−I); alkyl groups are weakly electron-donating (+I).
- **Resonance (mesomeric) effect:** donation or withdrawal of π electrons through conjugation. –OH, –OR, –NH₂ donate by resonance (+M) despite being inductively withdrawing; –NO₂, –C=O, –CN withdraw by resonance (−M).
- **Hyperconjugation:** donation from adjacent C–H or C–C σ bonds into an empty p orbital or π* orbital. Explains why carbocation and radical stability increase with alkyl substitution (3° > 2° > 1° > methyl) and why more substituted alkenes are more stable.
- **Steric effects:** spatial crowding that hinders reactions or destabilizes conformations.

## 8. Organic Acids and Bases

Acidity governs much of organic reactivity: deprotonation creates nucleophiles; protonation creates electrophiles and good leaving groups.

### Predicting acid strength: stabilize the conjugate base
Factors in rough order of importance ("**ARIO**"):
1. **Atom bearing the charge:** across a row, electronegativity dominates (CH₄ < NH₃ < H₂O < HF); down a column, size dominates (HF < HCl < HBr < HI; ROH < RSH).
2. **Resonance:** delocalization of the negative charge (carboxylic acids ≫ alcohols; phenols > alcohols; α-H next to carbonyls is acidic because the enolate is resonance-stabilized).
3. **Induction:** nearby electron-withdrawing groups stabilize the anion (trifluoroethanol pKa 12.4 vs. ethanol 16; chloroacetic acid 2.87 vs. acetic 4.76).
4. **Orbital (hybridization):** more s-character holds the lone pair closer to the nucleus: sp C–H (25) > sp² (44) > sp³ (50).
(Also solvation and aromaticity can matter: cyclopentadiene, pKa 16, is unusually acidic because its anion is aromatic.)

### Predicting equilibrium
**Acid–base equilibria favor the side with the weaker acid** (higher pKa). The equilibrium constant is $K = 10^{\text{pKa(product acid)} - \text{pKa(reactant acid)}}$. Example: NaNH₂ (conjugate acid NH₃, pKa 36) deprotonates a terminal alkyne (pKa 25) essentially completely ($K \approx 10^{11}$); hydroxide (water pKa 15.7) cannot.

### Organic bases
Amines are the most important organic bases. Basicity is discussed via the pKa of the conjugate acid (pKaH): higher pKaH → stronger base. Alkylamines (pKaH ~10.6–10.8) > ammonia (9.25) > pyridine (5.2) > aniline (4.6, lone pair delocalized into the ring) > amides (~0, lone pair delocalized into C=O; amides protonate on O). Guanidine (13.6) and amidines (DBU, ~12–13 in water) are strong neutral organic bases due to resonance stabilization of their conjugate acids.

### Nucleophiles and electrophiles
- **Nucleophile** ("nucleus-loving"): electron-rich species that donates an electron pair to form a bond — a Lewis base in a kinetic sense. Examples: OH⁻, RO⁻, CN⁻, I⁻, NH₃, H₂O, carbanions, enolates, π bonds.
- **Electrophile** ("electron-loving"): electron-poor species that accepts an electron pair — Lewis acid. Examples: H⁺, carbocations, carbonyl carbons, alkyl halide carbons (δ⁺), Br₂ (polarizable), BF₃, AlCl₃.

Most polar organic reactions are a nucleophile attacking an electrophile. Identifying them is the key to predicting reactions.

## 9. Types of Organic Reactions

| Type | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Addition | Two reactants combine; π bond broken | Alkene + HBr → alkyl bromide |
| Elimination | One reactant splits into two; π bond formed | Alkyl halide + base → alkene |
| Substitution | One group replaces another | CH₃Br + OH⁻ → CH₃OH + Br⁻ |
| Rearrangement | Atoms reorganize within a molecule | Carbocation hydride/methyl shifts |
| Oxidation/reduction | Change in oxidation state (gain of O/loss of H = oxidation) | Alcohol → aldehyde → carboxylic acid |
| Pericyclic | Concerted cyclic electron shifts | Diels–Alder reaction |

Mechanistic classes: **polar** (ionic; nucleophile + electrophile), **radical** (homolytic bond cleavage; fishhook arrows), and **pericyclic** (concerted, cyclic transition states governed by orbital symmetry — Woodward–Hoffmann rules).

**Oxidation levels of carbon** (useful for planning syntheses): alkane < alcohol (and alkyl halide, amine) < aldehyde/ketone < carboxylic acid (and derivatives, nitrile) < CO₂. Counting bonds from carbon to heteroatoms (each C–O, C–N, C–X bond counts 1; double bonds 2) tracks oxidation level.
