# Hydrocarbons: Alkanes, Alkenes and Alkynes and Their Reactions

Hydrocarbons — compounds containing only carbon and hydrogen — are the backbone of organic chemistry and the main components of petroleum and natural gas. Alkanes are relatively inert and serve mainly as fuels and solvents; alkenes and alkynes, with their reactive π bonds, are versatile starting materials for synthesis and the monomers for most plastics.

## Part I — Alkanes

### 1. Structure and properties
- General formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ (acyclic); cycloalkanes CₙH₂ₙ.
- All carbons sp³, bond angles ~109.5°, free rotation about C–C bonds.
- **Nonpolar**; insoluble in water, soluble in nonpolar solvents; less dense than water (~0.6–0.8 g/mL).
- Only London dispersion forces → boiling points rise with molar mass and decrease with branching.

| Alkane | Formula | bp (°C) | State at 25 °C | Use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Methane | CH₄ | −162 | Gas | Natural gas |
| Ethane | C₂H₆ | −89 | Gas | Ethylene feedstock |
| Propane | C₃H₈ | −42 | Gas | LPG, barbecues |
| Butane | C₄H₁₀ | −0.5 | Gas | Lighters |
| Pentane | C₅H₁₂ | 36 | Liquid | Solvent |
| Hexane | C₆H₁₄ | 69 | Liquid | Solvent, oil extraction |
| Octane | C₈H₁₈ | 126 | Liquid | Gasoline component |
| C₉–C₁₆ | | 150–290 | Liquid | Kerosene, jet fuel, diesel |
| C₁₈+ | | > 300 | Waxy solids | Lubricating oils, paraffin wax, asphalt |

**Petroleum refining:** crude oil is separated by **fractional distillation** into fractions by boiling point; **cracking** (thermal or catalytic, on zeolites) breaks large molecules into smaller, more valuable ones (gasoline, ethylene, propylene); **reforming** converts straight-chain alkanes into branched alkanes and aromatics, increasing the **octane number** (resistance to engine knocking; heptane = 0, 2,2,4-trimethylpentane = 100).

### 2. Reactions of alkanes
Alkanes are called **paraffins** (Latin *parum affinis*, "little affinity") because they are unreactive toward acids, bases, oxidizing and reducing agents at room temperature. Their main reactions are combustion and radical halogenation.

#### Combustion
$$\text{C}_n\text{H}_{2n+2} + \frac{3n+1}{2}\text{O}_2 \to n\text{CO}_2 + (n+1)\text{H}_2\text{O}$$
Highly exothermic (methane: −890 kJ/mol; octane: −5470 kJ/mol). Incomplete combustion produces carbon monoxide (a deadly, odorless gas that binds hemoglobin) and soot.

#### Free-radical halogenation
$$\text{CH}_4 + \text{Cl}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu\text{ or heat}}\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{HCl}$$
**Radical chain mechanism:**
1. **Initiation:** $\text{Cl}_2\xrightarrow{h\nu}2\,\text{Cl}\cdot$ (homolysis of the weak Cl–Cl bond, 242 kJ/mol)
2. **Propagation:**
   - $\text{Cl}\cdot + \text{H–CH}_3\to\text{H–Cl} + \cdot\text{CH}_3$
   - $\cdot\text{CH}_3 + \text{Cl–Cl}\to\text{CH}_3\text{Cl} + \text{Cl}\cdot$ (regenerates Cl·; each initiation can produce thousands of product molecules)
3. **Termination:** any two radicals combine — $\text{Cl}\cdot + \text{Cl}\cdot\to\text{Cl}_2$; $\cdot\text{CH}_3 + \text{Cl}\cdot\to\text{CH}_3\text{Cl}$; $2\,\cdot\text{CH}_3\to\text{C}_2\text{H}_6$.

Further substitution gives CH₂Cl₂, CHCl₃, CCl₄ — mixtures are typical; excess alkane favors monosubstitution.

**Radical stability:** tertiary > secondary > primary > methyl (hyperconjugation), reflected in C–H bond dissociation energies: CH₃–H 439; primary 423; secondary 413; tertiary 404 kJ/mol. Allylic and benzylic radicals are even more stable (resonance; C–H ~360–370 kJ/mol).

**Selectivity: chlorination vs. bromination.**
- Chlorination is fast and **unselective**: relative rates per H (3° : 2° : 1°) ≈ 5 : 3.8 : 1 at 25 °C. Propane gives ~45% 1-chloropropane and ~55% 2-chloropropane.
- Bromination is slower and **highly selective**: ≈ 1600 : 82 : 1. Propane gives ~97% 2-bromopropane.
- Explanation via **Hammond's postulate:** H-abstraction by Cl· is exothermic with an early, reactant-like transition state (little radical character; little discrimination). H-abstraction by Br· is endothermic, with a late, product-like transition state that strongly reflects radical stability.

**Fluorination** is explosively exothermic; **iodination** is endothermic and doesn't proceed.

**Allylic bromination** with **N-bromosuccinimide (NBS)** and light/peroxide selectively brominates the allylic position, keeping Br₂ concentration low to avoid addition to the C=C.

## Part II — Alkenes

### 3. Structure and stability
- General formula CₙH₂ₙ (one C=C); "olefins".
- sp² carbons, planar, 120° angles; π bond (weaker than σ, ~264 kJ/mol) above and below the plane; no free rotation → cis/trans (E/Z) isomers.
- **Stability** (from heats of hydrogenation): more substituted alkenes are more stable (hyperconjugation; sp²–sp³ bonds are stronger): tetrasubstituted > trisubstituted > disubstituted > monosubstituted > ethylene. *trans* > *cis* (less steric strain): heat of hydrogenation of *cis*-but-2-ene is 120 kJ/mol, *trans*-but-2-ene 116 kJ/mol, but-1-ene 127 kJ/mol.
- **Ethylene** (ethene) is the most produced organic chemical (~200 million tonnes/year), made by steam cracking. It is also a **plant hormone** that triggers fruit ripening (a ripe banana speeds up ripening of other fruit in a bag).

### 4. Electrophilic addition: the general mechanism
The π electrons are a nucleophile. Electrophiles (E⁺) add to the double bond:
1. The π bond attacks the electrophile, forming a **carbocation** (rate-determining).
2. A nucleophile attacks the carbocation.

**Carbocation stability:** 3° > 2° > 1° > methyl (hyperconjugation and inductive donation); allylic and benzylic cations are stabilized by resonance. Carbocations are sp², planar, with an empty p orbital.

### 5. Hydrohalogenation (adding HX)
$$\text{CH}_3\text{CH=CH}_2 + \text{HBr}\to\text{CH}_3\text{CHBrCH}_3$$

**Markovnikov's rule** (Vladimir Markovnikov, 1870): in the addition of HX to an unsymmetrical alkene, the H adds to the carbon that already has more hydrogens, and X adds to the more substituted carbon. **Modern statement:** the electrophile adds so as to form the **more stable carbocation intermediate**.

**Carbocation rearrangements:** a less stable carbocation can rearrange to a more stable one via a **1,2-hydride shift** or **1,2-methyl (alkyl) shift**. Example: HCl + 3-methylbut-1-ene gives mostly 2-chloro-2-methylbutane (via hydride shift from a 2° to a 3° cation), not only 2-chloro-3-methylbutane. Rearrangements are a hallmark of carbocation mechanisms.

**Stereochemistry:** the planar carbocation can be attacked from either face; a new stereocenter forms as a racemate.

### 6. Anti-Markovnikov addition of HBr (peroxide effect)
In the presence of peroxides (ROOR) and light/heat, HBr adds **anti-Markovnikov** by a radical chain mechanism (Kharasch, 1933):
- Br· adds to the less substituted carbon, giving the more stable carbon radical.
- The radical abstracts H from HBr, regenerating Br·.
$$\text{CH}_3\text{CH=CH}_2 + \text{HBr}\xrightarrow{\text{ROOR}}\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{Br}$$
Works only for HBr (with HCl and HI, one propagation step is endothermic).

### 7. Hydration: adding water
Three complementary methods:

| Method | Reagents | Regiochemistry | Stereochemistry | Rearrangements? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Acid-catalyzed hydration | H₂O, H₂SO₄ (cat.) | Markovnikov | Not controlled | Yes (carbocation) |
| Oxymercuration–demercuration | 1. Hg(OAc)₂, H₂O; 2. NaBH₄ | Markovnikov | Anti addition (step 1) | No (mercurinium ion) |
| Hydroboration–oxidation | 1. BH₃·THF; 2. H₂O₂, NaOH | **Anti-Markovnikov** | **Syn** addition | No (concerted) |

**Acid-catalyzed hydration** is reversible (the reverse is alcohol dehydration); used industrially to make ethanol from ethylene. Le Chatelier: excess water favors the alcohol.

**Hydroboration–oxidation** (Herbert C. Brown, Nobel 1979): BH₃ adds in a concerted four-center transition state, with boron going to the **less substituted** (less hindered) carbon and H to the more substituted one, both on the same face (syn). Oxidation with basic H₂O₂ replaces B with OH with **retention** of configuration. Result: anti-Markovnikov, syn addition of H and OH.
Example: 1-methylcyclopentene → *trans*-2-methylcyclopentan-1-ol (H and OH syn, so OH is trans to the methyl).

### 8. Halogenation and halohydrin formation
**Addition of Br₂ or Cl₂:**
$$\text{CH}_2\text{=CH}_2 + \text{Br}_2\to\text{BrCH}_2\text{CH}_2\text{Br}$$
Mechanism: the alkene attacks Br₂ to form a cyclic **bromonium ion**; bromide then attacks from the **opposite face** (backside, like SN2), giving **anti addition**.
- Cyclohexene + Br₂ → *trans*-1,2-dibromocyclohexane (racemic).
- *cis*-But-2-ene + Br₂ → racemic (2*R*,3*R*)/(2*S*,3*S*)-2,3-dibromobutane; *trans*-but-2-ene → *meso*-2,3-dibromobutane. A stereospecific reaction.
- **Test for unsaturation:** the red-brown color of bromine disappears when added to an alkene.

**Halohydrins:** with Br₂ or Cl₂ in **water**, water (present in large excess) opens the halonium ion instead of halide. Water attacks the **more substituted** carbon (which bears more positive character), giving Markovnikov orientation of OH, with anti stereochemistry.

### 9. Hydrogenation (reduction)
$$\text{R}_2\text{C=CR}_2 + \text{H}_2\xrightarrow{\text{Pt, Pd or Ni}}\text{R}_2\text{CH–CHR}_2$$
- **Syn addition**: both H atoms add from the catalyst surface to the same face.
- Exothermic (~120 kJ/mol for a disubstituted alkene) but requires a catalyst (Sabatier, Nobel 1912).
- Used to convert liquid vegetable oils into solid margarine and shortening; partial hydrogenation also isomerized some double bonds to *trans* (trans fats).
- Homogeneous catalysts (Wilkinson's catalyst, RhCl(PPh₃)₃) and chiral catalysts allow selective and asymmetric hydrogenation.

### 10. Oxidation of alkenes
**Epoxidation:** peroxy acids (RCO₃H, e.g. mCPBA) transfer an oxygen atom in a concerted, syn manner, forming an **epoxide** (oxirane). Stereospecific: *cis*-alkenes give *cis*-epoxides. Epoxides are strained three-membered rings that open readily with nucleophiles. (Industrially, ethylene oxide is made by direct oxidation over silver.)

**Anti-dihydroxylation:** epoxidation followed by acid-catalyzed opening with water gives *trans*-1,2-diols.

**Syn-dihydroxylation:** OsO₄ (catalytic, with NMO as co-oxidant) or cold dilute KMnO₄ adds two OH groups to the same face via a cyclic ester, giving *cis*-1,2-diols (glycols). Sharpless asymmetric dihydroxylation uses chiral ligands to give one enantiomer.

**Oxidative cleavage:**
- **Ozonolysis:** O₃, then a reductive workup (Zn/H₂O or (CH₃)₂S) cleaves C=C into two C=O groups (aldehydes and/or ketones). Oxidative workup (H₂O₂) converts aldehydes to carboxylic acids. Historically used to locate double bonds in unknown structures.
  Example: 2-methylbut-2-ene + O₃, then (CH₃)₂S → acetone + acetaldehyde.
- **Hot, concentrated KMnO₄:** cleaves C=C; terminal =CH₂ becomes CO₂; =CHR becomes RCOOH; =CR₂ becomes ketones. A purple-to-brown color change (Baeyer test) indicates unsaturation.

### 11. Polymerization
Alkenes link into long chains (**addition polymers**) via radical, cationic, anionic or coordination (Ziegler–Natta; Ziegler and Natta, Nobel 1963) mechanisms.

| Monomer | Polymer | Uses |
|---|---|---|
| CH₂=CH₂ | Polyethylene (PE: LDPE, HDPE) | Bags, bottles, films |
| CH₂=CHCH₃ | Polypropylene (PP) | Containers, carpets, car parts |
| CH₂=CHCl | Poly(vinyl chloride) (PVC) | Pipes, flooring, window frames |
| CH₂=CHC₆H₅ | Polystyrene (PS) | Foam cups, packaging, CD cases |
| CF₂=CF₂ | Polytetrafluoroethylene (PTFE, Teflon) | Nonstick coatings, seals |
| CH₂=C(CH₃)COOCH₃ | Poly(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA, Plexiglas) | Transparent sheets, lenses |
| CH₂=CHCN | Polyacrylonitrile | Acrylic fibers |

## Part III — Alkynes

### 12. Structure and acidity
- General formula CₙH₂ₙ₋₂; sp carbons; linear; one σ and two π bonds; C≡C length 120 pm.
- **Terminal alkynes are weakly acidic** (pKa ~25) because the sp-hybridized carbanion holds its lone pair in an orbital with 50% s-character. Strong bases (NaNH₂, n-BuLi) form **acetylide ions**, R–C≡C⁻ (hydroxide and alkoxides are too weak).
- **Acetylide alkylation:** acetylides are excellent nucleophiles that react with **methyl and primary alkyl halides** by SN2, forming new C–C bonds (with secondary/tertiary halides, E2 elimination dominates):
$$\text{HC≡CH}\xrightarrow{\text{NaNH}_2}\text{HC≡C}^-\text{Na}^+\xrightarrow{\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{Br}}\text{HC≡CCH}_2\text{CH}_3$$
- **Acetylene** (ethyne) burns with oxygen at ~3300 °C (oxyacetylene welding). Historically made from calcium carbide + water (carbide lamps used by miners and cavers).

### 13. Reactions of alkynes
- **Addition of HX:** Markovnikov; one equivalent gives a vinyl halide; two equivalents give a geminal dihalide.
- **Addition of X₂:** one equivalent gives (mostly *E*) dihaloalkenes; two give tetrahaloalkanes.
- **Hydration:**
  - **Hg²⁺-catalyzed** (H₂SO₄, H₂O, HgSO₄): Markovnikov; the initial **enol** tautomerizes to a **ketone** (terminal alkynes give methyl ketones).
  - **Hydroboration–oxidation** (with bulky boranes such as disiamylborane or 9-BBN): anti-Markovnikov; terminal alkynes give **aldehydes** after tautomerization.
- **Keto–enol tautomerism:** enols (C=C–OH) rapidly isomerize to the more stable carbonyl form (C–C=O), typically >99.99% keto at equilibrium for simple ketones.
- **Reduction:**
  - H₂ with Pt, Pd or Ni → **alkane** (complete reduction).
  - H₂ with **Lindlar's catalyst** (Pd on CaCO₃ poisoned with lead acetate and quinoline) → **cis-alkene** (syn addition stops at the alkene).
  - **Na or Li in liquid NH₃** (dissolving-metal reduction) → **trans-alkene** (via radical anion and vinyl radical/anion intermediates that adopt the more stable trans geometry).
- **Oxidative cleavage** (O₃ or KMnO₄) → carboxylic acids (terminal carbon → CO₂).
- **Coupling reactions:** Sonogashira (Pd/Cu, alkyne + aryl halide), Glaser coupling, "click chemistry" (Cu-catalyzed azide–alkyne cycloaddition forming triazoles; Sharpless, Meldal and Bertozzi, Nobel 2022).

## Part IV — Conjugated Dienes

### 14. Conjugation and stability
Dienes with alternating single and double bonds (**conjugated**, e.g. buta-1,3-diene) are more stable than isolated dienes by ~15 kJ/mol, because the p orbitals overlap across all four carbons (delocalized π molecular orbitals). The central C–C bond (147 pm) is shorter than a normal single bond.

Conjugation lowers the HOMO–LUMO gap, shifting UV–visible absorption to longer wavelengths (buta-1,3-diene λmax = 217 nm; β-carotene, with 11 conjugated double bonds, absorbs ~450 nm and appears orange). **Woodward–Fieser rules** predict λmax for conjugated systems.

### 15. 1,2- vs. 1,4-addition: kinetic vs. thermodynamic control
HBr adds to buta-1,3-diene via a resonance-stabilized **allylic cation**, which bromide can attack at C2 (**1,2-adduct**) or C4 (**1,4-adduct**):
- At low temperature (−80 °C): mainly 1,2-addition — **kinetic product** (forms faster; lower activation energy; Br⁻ attacks the carbon nearest where it was generated, which bears more positive charge).
- At higher temperature (40 °C): mainly 1,4-addition — **thermodynamic product** (more stable, more substituted internal alkene); reactions are reversible, so the equilibrium mixture forms.

This is a classic illustration of **kinetic versus thermodynamic control**.

### 16. The Diels–Alder reaction
A **[4+2] cycloaddition** between a conjugated **diene** and a **dienophile** (an alkene or alkyne, best with electron-withdrawing groups) to form a cyclohexene ring (Otto Diels and Kurt Alder, 1928; Nobel 1950).
$$\text{buta-1,3-diene} + \text{ethene}\xrightarrow{\Delta}\text{cyclohexene}$$
Features:
- **Concerted** (one step, cyclic transition state; pericyclic), forming two σ bonds and one new π bond.
- The diene must adopt the **s-cis** conformation (cyclopentadiene, locked s-cis, is extremely reactive and dimerizes at room temperature).
- **Stereospecific:** dienophile stereochemistry is retained (*cis* substituents remain *cis* in the product).
- **Endo rule:** with cyclic dienes, the kinetic product usually has the dienophile's substituents oriented toward the newly forming π bond (endo), due to secondary orbital interactions.
- **Regiochemistry:** "ortho/para" products predominate with unsymmetrical partners.
- Allowed thermally by orbital symmetry (Woodward–Hoffmann rules: [4+2] suprafacial cycloadditions are thermally allowed; [2+2] are photochemically allowed).
The Diels–Alder reaction builds six-membered rings with control of up to four stereocenters in one step, and is widely used in natural product synthesis (e.g. steroids, reserpine, taxol approaches).
